Viral Hepatitis (A, B & C)

Jaundice mechanics and LFT trends used throughout this page are covered in full on Liver Anatomy, Function & Jaundice

What Viral Hepatitis Is

The Basics

  • A systemic virus that mainly affects the liver — the "-itis" means inflammation, so this is inflammation of the liver caused by a virus.
  • Many strains exist, but this course focuses on hepatitis A, B, and C (HAV, HBV, HCV). Hepatitis D and E exist but are much less common in the U.S. — hepatitis E is notably dangerous in pregnancy, but is not otherwise tested here.
  • Other viruses (Epstein-Barr, cytomegalovirus) and non-infectious causes (alcohol abuse, certain drugs, chemicals, bacteria) can also inflame the liver, but "viral hepatitis" in this course means A, B, or C.
  • Hepatitis B and C are the most common; hepatitis A outbreaks still occur periodically.

Pathogenesis

How the Damage Happens

  • Viral infection triggers an immune response — a release of inflammatory mediators in and around the liver, where the virus lives.
  • Inflammatory mediators cause infected cells to lyse (rupture), which causes edema and swelling around the dead cells.
  • That edema and swelling contributes to tissue hypoxia, which — if severe enough — kills healthy hepatocytes (hepatocyte death).
  • Hepatocyte death can ultimately lead to long-term liver failure or problems.

Many cases of viral hepatitis are asymptomatic. When symptoms do occur, they progress the same way regardless of strain (A, B, or C) — only the transmission route, chronicity risk, and treatment differ between strains.

The Three Clinical Phases

PhaseTimingWhat Happens
ProdromalBegins about 2 weeks after exposure.Nonspecific symptoms: fatigue, anorexia, malaise, nausea/vomiting, headache, hyperalgesia (increased sensitivity to pain), cough, low-grade fever. The virus is highly transmissible during this phase.
IctericBegins 1–2 weeks after the prodromal phase; lasts 2–6 weeks.The patient becomes jaundiced — dark urine, possibly clay-colored stools, an enlarged and tender liver. This is often thought of as the "active" phase of illness, even though the earlier nonspecific symptoms were already the disease at work.
RecoveryJaundice resolves; enzymes normalize over roughly 2–12 weeks after jaundice onset.Symptoms diminish over about 6–8 weeks total after exposure, though the liver can remain enlarged and tender even as symptoms fade.

Most patients with acute viral hepatitis recover completely and clear the virus — overall mortality is under 1%, higher in elderly patients or those with comorbidities. The concerning outcomes below are what make hepatitis B and C different from a routine viral illness.

Possible Complications

  • Chronic hepatitis — the virus never fully clears, causing prolonged liver problems.
  • Cirrhosis — permanent liver scarring (see the Cirrhosis & Liver Failure page).
  • Liver cancer — hepatitis B and C are both linked to hepatocellular carcinoma.
  • Fulminant viral hepatitis — a severe initial case that becomes acute liver failure, which can progress to death.

MNEMONIC: Hepatitis A is the "food and water" one (fecal-oral, acute, never chronic, has a vaccine). B and C are the "blood and body fluid" ones (needles/sex, insidious onset, can go chronic) — the key difference between B and C is how OFTEN they go chronic: B about 10%, C about 80%.

Pharmacotherapy

StrainApproach
Hepatitis B (chronic, high-risk patients)Two drug classes, usually combined: interferons (pegylated interferon alpha / "Pegasys") and nucleoside analogs (entecavir, tenofovir). Treatment is reserved for high-risk patients — elevated AST, hepatic inflammation on imaging, or advanced fibrosis. Therapy is prolonged, expensive, has significant adverse effects, and carries a high relapse rate. There is no great treatment for chronic hepatitis B.
Hepatitis CDirect-acting antivirals (DAAs) — a newer drug class that can essentially eliminate the virus from the body in most patients. Sometimes combined with interferon or ribavirin. Anyone with a detectable viral load is now treated; the drugs are expensive but considered cost-beneficial because they work so well.
Acetaminophen (Tylenol) dosing with hepatitis: patients with chronic or active hepatitis may still take Tylenol, but should stay under 2 grams/day (versus the usual 4 g/day limit) since the liver already can't metabolize normally. Avoid Tylenol entirely in serious, advanced liver disease.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

Which three hepatitis strains does this course focus on?
Hepatitis A, B, and C (HAV, HBV, HCV).
What are the three clinical phases of viral hepatitis, in order?
Prodromal, icteric, recovery.
During which phase is the virus most transmissible, and what are its symptoms like?
The prodromal phase — nonspecific symptoms (fatigue, anorexia, malaise, nausea, headache) about 2 weeks after exposure.
How is hepatitis A transmitted, and how does that differ from B and C?
Fecal-oral (foodborne/poor sanitation) — B and C spread through blood/body fluids (IV drug use, sexual contact).
What percentage of hepatitis B infections become chronic? Hepatitis C?
About 10% for hepatitis B; about 80% for hepatitis C.
Which hepatitis strain has no available vaccine?
Hepatitis C.
Which hepatitis strain does NOT progress to chronic infection?
Hepatitis A.
What two drug classes treat chronic hepatitis B, and how effective/tolerable is that treatment overall?
Interferons and nucleoside analogs, usually combined — treatment is prolonged, expensive, has significant side effects, and has a high relapse rate. There's no great treatment for chronic hepatitis B.
What drug class has made hepatitis C essentially curable?
Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs).
What's the daily acetaminophen (Tylenol) limit for a patient with hepatitis, versus the normal limit?
Less than 2 grams/day with hepatitis, versus the usual 4 grams/day.
What is fulminant viral hepatitis?
A severe initial case of viral hepatitis that progresses to acute liver failure, which can lead to death.
Name two long-term complications of chronic hepatitis B or C beyond ongoing liver inflammation.
Any two of: cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (liver cancer), fulminant hepatitis/acute liver failure.
