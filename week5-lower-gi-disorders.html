Lower GI Disorders

These conditions are grouped together as "lower GI" disorders because they all involve the small intestine and below — as opposed to the esophagus and stomach, which are covered on the companion "Esophageal & Gastric Disorders" page.

Appendicitis

Overview

  • Inflammation of the appendix (a small appendage organ) that becomes obstructed, leading to inflammation.
  • The inflammation/obstruction itself isn't the biggest concern — it's the potential complications: the appendix can rupture, become gangrenous, form an abscess, and ultimately cause peritonitis.
  • Classic pain: right lower quadrant (RLQ) and periumbilical area.
  • Rebound tenderness (rebound pain): pain is severe after a provider pushes down in the RLQ and then releases — that release pain is rebound pain.
Sudden, complete relief of severe pain is NOT a good sign. If a patient's severe appendicitis pain suddenly and completely dissipates, it can indicate the appendix has ruptured, putting the patient at very high risk for peritonitis.

Manifestations & Diagnosis

  • Peak incidence age 10–12 years old.
  • Usually begins as dull, steady pain that progresses quickly over 4–6 hours, localizing to the RLQ.
  • Fever, nausea, and anorexia are common.
  • Diagnosed through clinical signs/symptoms, ultrasound (to see if the appendix is inflamed), and sometimes exploratory laparotomy.
  • Look for rebound pain/tenderness at the RLQ.

Peritonitis

Overview

  • Inflammation of the peritoneum, the serous membrane that lines the abdominal cavity and covers the visceral organs — a protective barrier keeping gut contents contained and keeping what doesn't belong in the gut, out.
  • One of the biggest concerns with all abdominal conditions.

Pathophysiology

  • Inflammation leads to fluid shifts known as third spacing, which can lead to deadly complications like hypovolemic shock and sepsis.
  • Also causes decreased peristalsis (the gut slows down and can't digest food), which can lead to paralytic ileus and intestinal obstruction.
  • Patients with peritonitis can become very sick very quickly.

Causes

  • Perforated gastric or duodenal ulcers.
  • Ruptured gallbladder.
  • Pancreatitis.
  • Ruptured spleen.
  • Ruptured bladder.
  • Ruptured appendix.

Notice the theme — rupture or perforation, where contents that are supposed to stay inside the gut/organ get outside of it, causing inflammation of the peritoneum.

Presentation

  • Sudden, severe abdominal pain — the patient can't move, can't talk, has trouble breathing — with tenderness.
  • The biggest clinical sign is a rigid, board-like abdomen — this is different from a distended abdomen; it's hard to the touch.
  • Nausea and vomiting, fever, elevated white blood cell count.
  • Vital sign changes: increased heart rate and decreased blood pressure — from sympathetic nervous system activation due to pain, but mostly related to the fluid shifts peritonitis causes.

Treatment

  • Difficult to treat — anti-inflammatories can help, but the priority is fixing whatever caused it, which then decreases the inflammation.

Pancreatitis and ruptured gallbladder are two of the most common causes of peritonitis — both are covered in full on Gallbladder & Pancreatic Disorders (Week 4); this page does not re-teach them

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Overview

  • A chronic condition characterized by alterations in bowel pattern due to changes in intestinal motility (of the small and large intestines).
  • Usually associated with either chronic/frequent constipation (IBS-C) or chronic/frequent diarrhea (IBS-D).

Symptoms

  • Abdominal distension, fullness, excessive flatus/bloating.
  • Intermittent abdominal pain that is exacerbated by stress and relieved by defecation.
  • Bowel urgency.
  • Often intolerance to certain foods (sorbitol, lactose, or gluten).
  • Sometimes non-bloody stool containing excessive mucus.

For a long time, people with gluten intolerance/celiac-type reactions were simply diagnosed with IBS. Providers are getting better at distinguishing a true food allergy/intolerance from IBS, but when no specific cause can be identified and the patient still has these clinical manifestations — chronic/frequent diarrhea and abdominal pain — it's labeled IBS.

Stress Connection

  • There is a real connection between psychosocial stress and IBS, but it's almost never a primary psychological cause — emotional stress doesn't cause the illness, but it can exacerbate/worsen it (make symptoms more intense).
  • Conversely, IBS itself is stressful to live with and can cause its own emotional/psychological problems — it works both directions.

Cause

  • General cause is unknown, but thought to be triggered by stress, food, hormonal changes, GI infections, and (in women) menstrual cycle changes.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD): Crohn's Disease & Ulcerative Colitis

Overview

  • IBD is an umbrella term for two conditions: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.
  • Unlike IBS, IBD has better-understood pathophysiology and more well-defined treatment.
  • A group of life-changing chronic illnesses, both characterized by chronic inflammation of the intestines with periods of exacerbation and remission.
  • More common in women, Caucasians, people of Jewish descent, and smokers.
  • Generally thought of as autoimmune conditions activated by an infection.

Crohn's Disease

  • Affects the lymphoid tissue of the GI tract, which becomes blocked; the surrounding tissue becomes engorged and inflamed.
  • Causes deep linear fissures and ulcers to develop in patchy patterns along the bowel wall, called skip lesions, which often have a cobblestone appearance.
  • Transmural — affects all layers of the GI tract wall.
  • Complications: malnutrition, anemia, scar tissue and obstructions, fistulas (an abnormal connection between two structures that shouldn't be connected), and a much higher risk of cancer.
  • Presentation: crampy lower abdominal pain, often in the right lower quadrant; watery diarrhea; systemic symptoms (weight loss, fatigue, lack of appetite, fever); malabsorption of nutrients; a palpable abdominal mass, usually RLQ; higher risk of mouth ulcers; fistula-related symptoms depending on location.
  • Granulomas and skip lesions are specific hallmark findings.

Ulcerative Colitis

  • Inflammation of the mucosa only — NOT transmural — specifically of the rectum and colon (large intestine).
  • Usually develops in the third decade of life (Crohn's often manifests earlier).
  • More common in white people of European descent, especially the Ashkenazi Jewish population; occasionally seen in Black/African American patients; very rare in Asian populations.
  • Inflammation begins in the rectum and extends in a continuous segment up the colon (not patchy/skip-lesion like Crohn's) and may involve the entire colon.
  • Inflammation leads to large ulcerations, which lead to necrosis of the epithelial tissue lining the colon and cause crypt abscesses; the colon/rectum try to repair the damage with new granulation tissue, but this tissue is fragile and bleeds easily.
  • Presentation: abdominal pain similar to Crohn's, but bloody diarrhea is very common — a feature not typically seen with Crohn's disease; same systemic symptoms (weight loss, fatigue, lack of appetite, fever).
  • Complications: hemorrhage (from fragile granulation tissue bleeding), perforation, a much higher risk of colon cancer (colorectal carcinoma) than Crohn's, malnutrition, anemia, strictures, high risk of rectal/colon/anal fissures and abscesses, toxic megacolon, higher risk of liver disease from inflammation/scarring of the bile ducts, and high risk of fluid, electrolyte, and pH imbalances.
Toxic megacolon is a rapid, life-threatening dilation of the large intestine — a serious complication of ulcerative colitis.

Both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis put patients at high risk of VTE/DVT — the exact reason isn't fully understood, but it's a recognized risk factor either way.

Diverticular Disease

TermDetail
DiverticulosisThe development of diverticula — small pouches in the lining of the colon that bulge outward through a weak spot in the colon wall. Can be congenital (present from birth) or acquired. Thought to be caused by a low-fiber diet, which results in chronic constipation — patients who are more chronically constipated are more likely to develop it. Usually occurs in the descending colon. Generally asymptomatic and typically discovered accidentally, or if it progresses to diverticulitis.
DiverticulitisOccurs when one of the diverticula pouches becomes inflamed — usually because fecal material becomes lodged in the pouch.

Diverticulitis: Presentation

  • Abdominal pain usually in the left lower quadrant (LLQ) — note this is the opposite side from appendicitis's classic right lower quadrant pain.
  • Fever, increased white blood cell count.
  • Abnormal bowel patterns (constipation or diarrhea).
  • Sometimes passage of a large quantity of frank (bright red) blood.
  • May resolve spontaneously, but can cause perforation, peritonitis, and obstruction depending on how inflamed/how many diverticula are involved if they rupture.

Pharmacology for Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Treatment for IBD is still limited — there are really only two drug classes covered here.

5-Aminosalicylic Acid (5-ASA) Class

  • Sulfasalazine is the key drug to know. It's technically a sulfonamide antibiotic that, within the intestines, converts into two parts: 5-aminosalicylic acid (the active anti-inflammatory component that helps IBD) and sulfapyridine (doesn't do much itself, but is responsible for most side effects).
  • Used for mild-to-moderate IBD.
  • Contraindicated in patients with a sulfa allergy (because it converts to sulfapyridine).
  • Use caution in patients with pre-existing anemia.
  • Side effects: nausea, fever, rash, headache, and hematologic disorders — this drug isn't ideal for patients with a lot of comorbidities/conditions.
  • Mesalamine is another drug in the same 5-ASA family that can be given instead.

DMARDs (Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs)

  • Increasingly the direction of IBD treatment (outside of diet modification and surgery), since IBD is an autoimmune condition.
  • Key drug to know: infliximab (brand name Remicade) — a monoclonal antibody that neutralizes TNF-alpha, an inflammatory mediator, essentially turning off/decreasing that inflammatory response.
  • Because DMARDs suppress the immune system, there's extensive pre-screening required before starting: patients need to be up to date on vaccines, have titers drawn, and be screened for TB/latent TB.
  • Main risks: increased risk of infection (immunosuppression), increased risk of cancer, and increased risk of heart failure.
  • Infliximab (and most DMARDs) is given through infusions — sometimes weekly, sometimes a couple times a week, sometimes monthly depending on the regimen.
  • Associated with infusion reactions and can cause neutropenia.
  • Requires therapeutic drug monitoring and biomarker monitoring — specifically CRP (C-reactive protein) for infliximab.
  • Often used in combination with other IBD medications.
