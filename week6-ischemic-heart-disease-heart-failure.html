Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure

Cardiovascular disease breaks down into three broad categories: "electrical" problems (dysrhythmias — the conduction system), "plumbing" problems (the vessels — ischemic/coronary artery disease), and "pump" problems (the heart muscle itself — heart failure). This page covers plumbing and pump; electrical is covered on its own page, Cardiac Dysrhythmias.

Coronary Artery Disease (Ischemic Heart Disease)

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is driven by atherosclerosis: plaque builds up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing blood flow to the myocardium. The earliest step in this process is endothelial dysfunction — damage to the vessel's inner lining, which sets up the conditions for plaque to form there.

The LAD — the "Widow Maker"

  • The left anterior descending (LAD) artery supplies a large portion of the heart muscle.
  • Because of how much muscle it feeds, a blockage in the LAD is especially dangerous and lethal — this is why it carries the nickname "widow maker."

Risk Factors for CAD

Non-Modifiable

  • Age
  • Family history
  • Gender — estrogen is cardioprotective before menopause, so risk changes after menopause
  • Ethnicity — Black, Hispanic, and Native American populations have higher risk, with a documented disparity in CAD-related deaths linked to structural racism
  • Genetics/genetic variants (SNPs)

Modifiable

  • Hypertension — roughly 2–3 times the risk
  • Smoking
  • Diabetes
  • Obesity/physical inactivity
  • Diet — a DASH-style diet is protective
  • Hyperlipidemia
  • Depression/stress/anxiety

Angina

Differentiating cardiac from non-cardiac chest pain is a key skill — and within cardiac chest pain, telling stable angina apart from unstable angina (and from a full MI) matters just as much.

Stable Angina

  • Triggered by exertion
  • Relieved by rest
  • Typically lasts 2–5 minutes
  • Treated with nitrates

Unstable Angina

  • A more dangerous pattern than stable angina
  • Not reliably relieved by rest
  • The higher-risk counterpart to stable angina — a step closer to a full MI

Myocardial Infarction (MI)

MI Symptom Pattern

  • Pain not relieved by rest — this is what distinguishes MI pain from stable angina
  • Pain that radiates
  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Dyspnea
  • Diaphoresis

Women often present atypically with MI compared to the "classic" presentation described above — a known teaching point, since an atypical picture can delay recognition.

Call 911 if chest pain is not resolved within about 5 minutes — this window is the concern point for unstable angina or a full MI.

Heart Failure: Definition & Core Physiology

Using the AHA's framing, heart failure (HF) is fundamentally the heart's inability to pump enough blood to meet the body's needs.

TermWhat It Means
Cardiac output (CO)CO = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume (SV).
PreloadThe fluid volume the heart is working with.
AfterloadThe pressure/resistance the heart has to pump against — related to blood pressure.
ContractilityHow forcefully the heart squeezes. Preload, afterload, and contractility together determine stroke volume.

The Heart Failure Hemodynamic Chain

  • Contractility decreases first
  • Which leads to increased preload — fluid backs up
  • Which leads to increased afterload

This chain is why HF's hallmark presentation involves fluid volume overload.

Cardiomyopathy — the Structural Root of Heart Failure

Cardiomyopathy is structural muscle disease of the heart that can lead to heart failure. There are three types.

TypeCauses / AssociationsKey Feature
DilatedIschemic damage, valve disease, alcohol use, peripartum (pregnancy-related) cardiomyopathy.Leads to HFrEF (reduced ejection fraction/systolic dysfunction).
HypertrophicRelated to hypertension.High risk of sudden cardiac death and arrhythmias.
RestrictiveLeast common type; often associated with amyloid deposits.The ventricle becomes stiff and non-compliant — it can't relax and fill properly; tends to cause right-sided heart failure.

Causes & Risk Factors of Heart Failure

Causes of Heart Failure

  • Repeated ischemic episodes or prior MI — damaged muscle can't pump as well
  • Papillary muscle rupture
  • Chronic hypertension
  • COPD — specifically leads to right-sided heart failure by straining the right side of the heart against high pulmonary pressures
  • Dysrhythmias
  • Valve disorders
  • Pulmonary emboli

Cross-reference: dysrhythmias are a cause of heart failure listed above — see Cardiac Dysrhythmias for how they actually arise and disrupt the heart's rhythm

Stable angina by itself is generally NOT considered a risk factor for heart failure — the instructor specifically called this out as something students might mistakenly assume.

Risk Factors for Heart Failure

  • Hypertension — the single greatest risk factor
  • Diabetes
  • Having had an MI — about 22% of men and 46% of women who have an MI go on to develop heart failure
  • Age over 65 — heart failure is the most common cause of hospitalization in this age group
  • Black/African-American patients have a higher incidence
  • A strong genetic/familial link
  • COPD
  • Severe anemia
  • Congenital heart defects
  • Viral myocarditis
  • Alcohol, cocaine, or crack abuse
  • Kidney disease

Left-Sided vs. Right-Sided Heart Failure

Left-Sided Heart Failure

  • Blood backs up into the lungs — causes pulmonary congestion
  • Most common cause: poorly controlled hypertension
  • Symptoms: cough, crackles, wheezes, frothy or blood-tinged sputum, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND — sudden shortness of breath that wakes a patient at night), orthopnea (shortness of breath when lying flat), tachycardia, fatigue, and cyanosis

Right-Sided Heart Failure

  • Blood backs up into the venous system — causes systemic (body-wide) congestion
  • Most common cause: COPD
  • Symptoms: jugular venous distension (JVD), dependent edema, weight gain, hepatosplenomegaly (enlarged liver/spleen), and GI distress
MNEMONIC — "Left = lungs, right = body": left-sided failure backs up into the lungs (pulmonary); right-sided failure backs up into the rest of the body (systemic).

HFrEF vs. HFpEF

Normal ejection fraction (EF) is 55–65%.

HFrEF (Reduced EF) — "Systolic Heart Failure"

  • Ejection fraction under 40%
  • Reflects impaired contractile (pumping) function

HFpEF (Preserved EF) — "Diastolic Heart Failure"

  • Ejection fraction of 40–50%
  • Previously called "diastolic dysfunction"
  • The muscle can still squeeze adequately, but the ventricles can't relax and fill properly
  • Associated with older age, female sex, diabetes, obesity, and chronic hypertension

Ventricular Remodeling

In heart failure, the body inappropriately releases substances that are meant to help but end up worsening the problem — this is ventricular remodeling, and it progressively worsens heart failure over time.

Substances Inappropriately Released

  • Angiotensin II
  • Aldosterone
  • Endothelin
  • TNF-alpha
  • Catecholamines

Effects on the Heart Muscle

  • Cell death (apoptosis)
  • Hypertrophy (thickening)
  • Collagen deposits
  • Myocardial fibrosis (scarring)

Cross-reference: pharmacologic treatment aimed at interrupting this remodeling process — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, beta blockers, MRAs — is covered in full on Cardiac Pharmacology

S3 Gallop

TermDetail
S3 gallopA low-pitched heart sound heard just after S2, occurring in early diastole. In adults over 40, an S3 gallop is considered an indicator of heart failure.
