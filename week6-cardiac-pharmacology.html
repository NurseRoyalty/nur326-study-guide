Antianginal Drugs

For the underlying pathophysiology of stable angina, see Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure. Four drug categories are used for stable angina, each acting on a different piece of the heart's oxygen supply/demand balance.

How Each Category Works

  • Nitrates — decrease preload via venodilation
  • Beta blockers — decrease heart rate and contractility (pull-forward — review your drug matrix for full profile)
  • Calcium channel blockers — decrease afterload and heart rate/contractility (pull-forward — review your drug matrix for full profile)
  • Ranolazine — improves myocardial energy efficiency (the exact mechanism wasn't detailed further)

Stepped treatment approach for stable angina: start with a beta blocker or a calcium channel blocker plus a nitrate, reassess in about 3 months, and escalate treatment if needed.

Nitroglycerin FormulationDetail
Sublingual (Nitrostat)Used for an active anginal attack. Give every 5 minutes as needed, up to 3 doses total.
Transdermal patchApplied in the morning and removed in the evening, rotating to hairless application sites.
Topical ointment (Nitro-Bid)Another topical formulation used for antianginal therapy.
IV nitroglycerinICU use only. Requires a glass IV bottle and special (non-standard) IV tubing.
Long-acting oral (isosorbide)Used for prevention only, not for an active attack. Must be tapered when discontinued to avoid rebound coronary vasospasm. If breakthrough chest pain occurs, the patient should NOT take an extra isosorbide dose — use sublingual nitroglycerin instead.

Nitroglycerin's mechanism across all formulations is venodilation, which decreases preload, with some arterial dilation as well. Side effects: headache, hypotension, and reflex tachycardia (from the vasodilation itself). Rapid tolerance develops with continued use. A dangerous interaction applies across all nitrate formulations: severe hypotension can occur with sildenafil, some antihypertensive medications, and alcohol.

If chest pain is not relieved within 5 minutes of a sublingual nitroglycerin dose, the patient should call 911 — this raises concern for unstable angina or STEMI, not just stable angina.
TermDetail
Ranolazine (Ranexa)Oral only. Can prolong the QT interval, creating a risk for lethal dysrhythmias. Requires caution in patients with renal or hepatic impairment. Does NOT cause the blood pressure/heart rate drop seen with nitrates. Side effects: headache, dizziness, nausea, and constipation. It is a CYP3A4 inhibitor, so grapefruit juice and other CYP3A4-interacting drugs should be avoided.

Heart Failure Drugs

Heart failure drugs are broadly used across left- vs. right-sided and systolic (HFrEF) vs. diastolic (HFpEF) heart failure, though HFpEF tends to respond somewhat less well to these medications than HFrEF does. For the underlying pathophysiology, see Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure. The current core categories are an ACE inhibitor, ARB, or ARNI; a beta blocker; and a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA). SGLT2 inhibitors are increasingly being added as a fourth core category, projected to become standard therapy for most heart failure patients within the next few years. Diuretics, nitrates, and digitalis (digoxin) are also used.

TermDetail
ACE inhibitors & ARBsPull-forward drugs — review your drug matrix for full mechanism/side-effect detail. In heart failure, a patient is placed on only one RAS-inhibitor type at a time; ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and ARNIs are never combined in the same patient.
ARNI — sacubitril/valsartanAn angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor combination product. Currently thought to be the most effective of the three RAS-inhibitor options, because the neprilysin inhibitor component adds an extra effect on cardiac remodeling beyond what ACE inhibitors or ARBs alone provide. Newer, more expensive, and harder to access than ACE inhibitors/ARBs.

RAS Inhibitors (ACE Inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs) — Shared Effects

  • Mechanism in heart failure: decrease preload and afterload, and suppress aldosterone, which helps interrupt the pathologic cardiac remodeling process
  • Decrease mortality, particularly in patients with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF); some benefit is also seen in HFpEF, but the mortality benefit is clearest in HFrEF
  • Providers try to titrate to the highest tolerated dose
  • Shared adverse effects: an ACE-inhibitor-specific cough; all three can cause elevated potassium; some can cause hypotension

Beta Blockers in Heart Failure

  • Carvedilol is used most frequently, because it provides both beta and alpha blockade
  • In heart failure, the sympathetic nervous system becomes chronically overactivated (neurohormonal activation) as a compensatory response that ultimately worsens the disease — beta blockers counteract this
  • Help protect against dysrhythmias and help interrupt the cardiac remodeling process
  • Main side effect to monitor: hypotension — heart failure patients are asked to monitor their own blood pressure at home
TermDetail
Spironolactone (MRA)Previously learned as a potassium-sparing diuretic. In heart failure it is not primarily used for its diuretic effect — instead it helps suppress sodium and water retention, offloading/decongesting the left ventricle (management aims to keep patients as fluid-"dry" as possible, difficult since volume overload is a primary feature of heart failure itself). Associated with decreased hospitalizations and decreased deaths. Because MRAs raise potassium, kidney function must be monitored closely — elevated potassium significantly increases dysrhythmia risk, which compounds in a patient with an already "sick heart" prone to rhythm disturbances (such as AFib or V-tach).
SGLT2 inhibitors ("the -gliflozins," e.g. empagliflozin, dapagliflozin)Originally learned as diabetes medications. The exact mechanism in heart failure isn't fully understood, but it's thought to help with ventricular "unloading" through a kind of natriuresis/osmotic diuresis without the patient actually losing that volume in the usual diuretic sense, and to affect cardiac metabolism and bioenergetics. A heart failure patient without diabetes may still be placed on one for this cardioprotective effect. Shown to decrease hospital readmissions, hospital mortality, and morbidity.
Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide)Most heart failure hospital admissions are due to fluid overload (lungs and/or extremities), because the failing heart can't maintain adequate blood pressure/colloid oncotic pressure. Most heart failure patients are on some kind of diuretic — usually a loop diuretic — taken orally at home or given IV/as an IV drip when hospitalized. Biggest concerns: potassium imbalances (raising dysrhythmia risk in an already dysrhythmia-prone heart) and hypotension. Patients on digoxin who become hypokalemic from diuretic therapy are at high risk for digoxin toxicity. Despite near-universal use in hospitalized heart failure patients, diuretics provide NO survival/mortality benefit — they're used purely for symptom relief.

Inotropic drugs try to strengthen the weakened heart muscle itself. Digitalis (digoxin) is the one inotrope covered in depth below. Sympathomimetic inotropes — dopamine, dobutamine, and milrinone — are also IV-drip-only inotropes that increase contractility and cardiac output, but are complex-care-level topics not detailed further at this level.

TermDetail
Digoxin (digitalis)Derived from the foxglove plant. Historically the first-line drug for essentially all heart failure, but has fallen out of favor because it doesn't have a strong survival benefit and carries a high risk of rhythm disturbances; still used today, and may also be used to treat atrial fibrillation. Mechanism: inhibits the sodium-potassium ATPase pump within cardiac cells, causing calcium to accumulate inside the cells; increased intracellular calcium increases myocardial contractility (this is a cell-level form of calcium availability, NOT related to dietary calcium/milk intake). Also increases blood flow to the kidneys and decreases sympathetic stimulation, which helps decrease heart rate.
Digoxin toxicity — risk factorsMajor risks are cardiac dysrhythmias (increased risk of V-tach, AFib, flutter, and other rhythm problems) and digoxin toxicity itself. Higher-risk patients: older adults, women, and patients also on diuretic therapy (diuretics can cause the hypokalemia that predisposes to toxicity) — since most hospitalized heart failure patients ARE on diuretic therapy, this overlap is a major clinical concern.
Digoxin toxicity — signs & preventionSigns/symptoms: bradycardia (the hallmark sign), headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, and visual disturbances. Prevention: dose reduction to the minimum effective amount; monitoring digoxin levels weekly (similar in spirit to how INR is monitored for warfarin, rather than a peak-and-trough pattern); supplemental potassium as needed (as long as kidney function allows), since low potassium specifically increases toxicity risk. Antidote for severe toxicity: digoxin immune Fab (an antigen-binding fragment), reserved for patients who are significantly symptomatic/unstable.
Take the patient's apical pulse for a full minute before administering digoxin, and hold the dose if the pulse is below 60 bpm — then notify the provider and monitor the rhythm. Bradycardia is the hallmark sign of digoxin toxicity.

Rate & Rhythm Control Drugs

Cross-reference: for the rhythms these drugs are treating, see Cardiac Dysrhythmias

Beta blockers and calcium channel blockers (already-known pull-forward drug classes) are used here too — for example, to treat sinus tachycardia once any underlying cause (fever, volume depletion, etc.) has been addressed and ruled out as the primary fix, or for rate control (and sometimes rhythm conversion) in atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter. Beta blockers are generally tried first.

TermDetail
Amiodarone — indications & mechanismUsed for essentially any dysrhythmia — atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or ventricular dysrhythmias. Mechanism: prolongs the action potential duration and effective refractory period in all cardiac tissue, and blocks both alpha and beta receptors in the sympathetic nervous system. Effective at converting patients out of atrial fibrillation, and also effective for ventricular dysrhythmias and SVT. Broadly described as an "antidysrhythmic." Contraindicated in patients with severe bradycardia or heart block.
Amiodarone — adverse effectsAbout 75% of patients who take amiodarone experience some kind of adverse effect. It is highly lipophilic and can concentrate/accumulate in adipose tissue, especially with higher or sustained doses. Adverse effects: thyroid alterations (can cause either hypo- or hyperthyroidism, related to its iodine content — caution or avoidance is warranted with existing thyroid disease or iodine allergy, though this may be outweighed by the need to treat a life-threatening ventricular dysrhythmia); corneal deposits (light sensitivity, dry eye, visual halos); pulmonary toxicity (carries a black box warning, fatal in about 10% of cases when it occurs); hepatotoxicity; and a pro-arrhythmic effect (it can sometimes trigger a new arrhythmia rather than only treating one).
Amiodarone — interactions & monitoringSignificant interactions: with digoxin (increases digoxin toxicity risk, so the two are typically avoided together) and with warfarin (can increase INR by 50–100%). Has a very long half-life, remaining in the system for many days — if adverse effects develop, they can take two to three months to fully resolve depending on how long the patient was on the drug. Requires substantial ongoing lab monitoring.

Amiodarone — IV (Acute/Emergent)

  • Used for an emergent/acute situation, such as a very tachycardic, symptomatic AFib patient

Amiodarone — Oral (Maintenance)

  • Used for ongoing/maintenance therapy after transitioning off an IV drip
TermDetail
AtropineGiven for symptomatic sinus bradycardia only — not given to a bradycardic patient who is asymptomatic. Classified as an anticholinergic/antimuscarinic; works by blocking the vagus nerve — specifically inhibiting postganglionic acetylcholine receptors (an indirect vagolytic action). Only works if the bradycardia is vagally induced; if it's due to an underlying structural/conduction problem within the heart itself, atropine won't help — though in practice, since the exact cause often isn't immediately known, it's generally tried anyway. Given IV push only, dosed at 1 mg every 3–5 minutes, up to a maximum total of 3 mg. Works in only about 30% of cases (though it does work when it works). Adverse effects, consistent with its anticholinergic action: dry mouth, blurry vision, light sensitivity, tachycardia, flushing, and hot skin.
Never give atropine without the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring — heart rate and rhythm must be watched closely as it's given. Only give it for a truly symptomatic patient, not simply because a heart rate happens to be low on the monitor.
TermDetail
AdenosineUsed for SVT/PSVT, or sometimes sinus tachycardia, though it's generally intended for PSVT specifically (since PSVT is the faster rhythm where the P wave isn't visible). Mechanism: slows conduction through the AV node. Has an extremely short half-life, so repeat doses are often needed until the underlying problem resolves or the rhythm converts. Can cause a brief period of asystole (a short pause with no cardiac rhythm at all) as it slows AV conduction — an expected, transient effect while the drug works; because of the small risk the heart won't restart spontaneously afterward, defibrillator/shock pads are typically placed on the patient as a precaution, especially with repeat dosing. Aside from this brief rhythm pause, adenosine has relatively few other side effects of concern. Dosing: given IV push only — first dose 6 mg; if ineffective, a second dose of up to 12 mg; a third dose of another 12 mg can follow if still needed. Must be administered through a three-way stopcock, pushing the adenosine and then immediately following with a rapid saline flush — otherwise the drug won't reach systemic circulation quickly enough to be effective, since its half-life is so short.
TermDetail
DofetilideAn antidysrhythmic used to help convert atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter into a normal sinus rhythm and then maintain that normal rhythm afterward (a maintenance drug, unlike adenosine or atropine, which are acute/one-time drugs). Mechanism: selectively blocks cardiac potassium ion channels. Major risk: a high risk of causing torsades de pointes (a type of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia) as well as new-onset SVT. General side effects: headache, dizziness, and chest pain. Not given to patients who already have a prolonged QT interval, or who are on other medications that also prolong the QT interval — the QT interval is the time from the start of the Q wave to the end of the T wave on the EKG, and a prolonged/long QT makes a patient a poor candidate for dofetilide.
Torsades de pointesThe dangerous rhythm dofetilide can trigger — a fast, polymorphic form of V-tach. Dangerous and potentially deadly; CPR must be started if it occurs.
Because of the torsades de pointes risk, dofetilide is always started in the hospital while the patient is on continuous telemetry monitoring, and it is not given to patients with a prolonged QT interval or on other QT-prolonging medications.

In addition to rate/rhythm-control drugs, patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter also generally need to be on warfarin (or a similar anticoagulant) because of stroke risk — go back and review warfarin's indications and nursing care from your prior pharmacology coursework.

What does nitroglycerin do to preload, and by what mechanism?
It decreases preload via venodilation, with some arterial dilation as well.
How often, and how many times, can sublingual nitroglycerin be given for an anginal attack?
Every 5 minutes as needed, up to 3 doses total.
What should a patient do if chest pain isn't relieved within 5 minutes of sublingual nitroglycerin?
Call 911 — this raises concern for unstable angina or STEMI.
Why must isosorbide be tapered rather than stopped abruptly?
To avoid rebound coronary vasospasm.
What should a patient on isosorbide do for breakthrough chest pain, instead of taking an extra isosorbide dose?
Use sublingual nitroglycerin instead.
What is ranolazine's main dysrhythmia risk, and why does it require avoiding grapefruit juice?
It can prolong the QT interval; it's a CYP3A4 inhibitor, so grapefruit juice and other CYP3A4-interacting drugs should be avoided.
How many RAS-inhibitor drug types (ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI) can a heart failure patient be on at once?
Only one — they are never combined in the same patient.
Why is the ARNI sacubitril/valsartan thought to be more effective than an ACE inhibitor or ARB alone?
Its neprilysin inhibitor component adds an extra effect on cardiac remodeling beyond what ACE inhibitors or ARBs alone provide.
Which beta blocker is used most frequently in heart failure, and why?
Carvedilol, because it provides both beta and alpha blockade.
Is spironolactone used in heart failure mainly for its diuretic effect?
No — it helps suppress sodium and water retention to offload/decongest the left ventricle, and is associated with decreased hospitalizations and deaths.
Why does diuretic therapy in a heart failure patient raise the risk of digoxin toxicity?
Diuretics can cause hypokalemia, and low potassium increases the risk of digoxin toxicity.
Do diuretics improve survival in heart failure?
No — despite being used in nearly all hospitalized heart failure patients, diuretics provide no survival/mortality benefit; they're used for symptom relief only.
What is the cellular mechanism by which digoxin increases myocardial contractility?
It inhibits the sodium-potassium ATPase pump in cardiac cells, causing calcium to accumulate intracellularly, which increases contractility (a cell-level effect, not related to dietary calcium).
Which patients are at higher risk for digoxin toxicity?
Older adults, women, and patients also on diuretic therapy.
What is the hallmark sign of digoxin toxicity, and what should the nurse do before giving a dose?
Bradycardia is the hallmark sign; take a full 1-minute apical pulse before giving digoxin, and hold the dose if it's below 60 bpm.
What does amiodarone do to the cardiac action potential, and which receptors does it block?
It prolongs the action potential duration and effective refractory period in all cardiac tissue, and blocks both alpha and beta receptors.
Why can amiodarone cause thyroid problems?
It can cause either hypo- or hyperthyroidism, related to its iodine content.
What does amiodarone do to a patient's INR if they're also on warfarin?
It can increase INR by 50–100%.
Is atropine given for asymptomatic sinus bradycardia?
No — atropine is given for symptomatic sinus bradycardia only.
How must adenosine be administered, and why?
IV push through a three-way stopcock, immediately followed by a rapid saline flush — otherwise it won't reach systemic circulation quickly enough to work, since its half-life is so short.
