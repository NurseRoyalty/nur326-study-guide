Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure

Cardiovascular disease breaks down into three broad categories: "electrical" problems (dysrhythmias — the conduction system), "plumbing" problems (the vessels — ischemic/coronary artery disease), and "pump" problems (the heart muscle itself — heart failure). This page covers plumbing and pump; electrical is covered on its own page, Cardiac Dysrhythmias.

Coronary Artery Disease (Ischemic Heart Disease)

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is driven by atherosclerosis: plaque builds up inside the coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing blood flow to the myocardium. "Plumbing" problems in the heart aren't only blockage — the coronary arteries can also spasm, and valve disease is lumped into this same plumbing category (see Valve Disease).

Endothelial Dysfunction — the Earliest Step

  • The vessel's inner lining (endothelium) is damaged, which sets up the conditions for plaque to form there.
  • Normally, when the heart needs more oxygen, coronary arteries dilate to deliver more blood. With endothelial dysfunction, they do the opposite — they vasoconstrict instead of dilating, worsening ischemia on top of whatever plaque is already narrowing the lumen.
  • Driven by diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and smoking — a major contributor to CAD and heart attacks.

The LAD — the "Widow Maker"

  • The left anterior descending (LAD) artery supplies a large portion of the heart muscle, feeding the left ventricle — the chamber that determines perfusion to the rest of the body.
  • It's especially dangerous because it typically has no collateral circulation to fall back on — so when it clogs, there's no backup blood supply, which is why a LAD blockage carries the nickname "widow maker."

CAD is extremely common: roughly 1 in 7 deaths in the US is attributed to it, and about half the US population has dyslipidemia, a major driver.

Risk Factors for CAD

Non-Modifiable

  • Age
  • Family history
  • Gender — estrogen is cardioprotective before menopause, so risk changes after menopause
  • Ethnicity — Black, Hispanic, and Native American populations have higher risk, with a documented disparity in CAD-related deaths linked to structural racism
  • Genetics/genetic variants (SNPs)

Modifiable

  • Hypertension — roughly 2–3 times the risk
  • Smoking
  • Diabetes
  • Obesity/physical inactivity
  • Diet — a DASH-style diet is protective
  • Hyperlipidemia
  • Depression/stress/anxiety

Angina

Angina generally means chest pain, but it can also show up with a wider associated symptom complex: heartburn, palpitations, weakness, dizziness, anxiety, nausea, cold sweats, or burning sensations in the chest, shoulder, or abdomen. It's sometimes mistaken for indigestion — eating a large meal can actually be a precursor to an angina episode.

Differentiating cardiac from non-cardiac chest pain is a key skill — and within cardiac chest pain, telling stable angina apart from unstable angina (and from a full MI) matters just as much.

Chest Pain TypeHow It's Described
CardiacDiffuse, hard to pinpoint — described as pressure, tightness, or "an elephant sitting on my chest." Often radiates to the left arm, jaw, or left shoulder, with diaphoresis and pallor.
Non-cardiacFocal — the patient can point right to it ("it's right here").

Stable Angina

  • Caused by an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand
  • Triggered by exertion
  • Relieved by rest
  • Typically lasts 2–5 minutes
  • Treated with nitrates

Unstable Angina

  • Coronary blood flow is diminished but not completely blocked — a "partially open water hose" instead of stable angina's fully-open one
  • A more dangerous pattern than stable angina
  • Not reliably relieved by rest
  • The higher-risk counterpart to stable angina — a step closer to a full MI

Myocardial Infarction (MI)

MI Symptom Pattern

  • Pain not relieved by rest — this is what distinguishes MI pain from stable angina
  • Pain that radiates
  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Dyspnea
  • Diaphoresis

Women Often Present Atypically

Early CAD/MI research was done mostly in men. Women are more likely to have: hot or burning discomfort/tenderness rather than classic pressure or tightness; pain in a location other than the chest; and nonspecific symptoms like indigestion, heartburn, nausea, and fatigue. This atypical picture is a known reason MI can be missed or delayed in women.

Call 911 if chest pain is not resolved within about 5 minutes — this window is the concern point for unstable angina or a full MI.

Heart Failure: Definition & Core Physiology

Using the AHA's framing, heart failure (HF) is fundamentally the heart's inability to pump enough blood to meet the body's needs.

TermWhat It Means
Cardiac output (CO)CO = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume (SV).
PreloadThe fluid volume the heart is working with.
AfterloadThe pressure/resistance the heart has to pump against — related to blood pressure.
ContractilityHow forcefully the heart squeezes. Preload, afterload, and contractility together determine stroke volume.

The Heart Failure Hemodynamic Chain

  • Contractility decreases first
  • Which leads to increased preload — fluid backs up
  • Which leads to increased afterload

This chain is why HF's hallmark presentation involves fluid volume overload.

Cardiomyopathy — the Structural Root of Heart Failure

Cardiomyopathy is structural muscle disease of the heart that can lead to heart failure. It's usually idiopathic (no identifiable cause), but can also result from infections, toxins, myocarditis, or inherited disorders. There are three types.

TypeCauses / AssociationsKey Feature
DilatedIschemic damage, valve disease, alcohol use, peripartum (pregnancy-related) cardiomyopathy.Leads to HFrEF (reduced ejection fraction/systolic dysfunction).
HypertrophicRelated to hypertension.High risk of sudden cardiac death and arrhythmias.
RestrictiveLeast common type; often associated with amyloid deposits.The ventricle becomes stiff and non-compliant — it can't relax and fill properly; tends to cause right-sided heart failure.

Causes & Risk Factors of Heart Failure

Causes of Heart Failure

  • Repeated ischemic episodes or prior MI — damaged muscle can't pump as well
  • Papillary muscle rupture
  • Chronic hypertension
  • COPD — specifically leads to right-sided heart failure by straining the right side of the heart against high pulmonary pressures
  • Dysrhythmias
  • Valve disorders — especially mitral insufficiency and aortic stenosis
  • Pulmonary emboli

Cross-reference: dysrhythmias are a cause of heart failure listed above — see Cardiac Dysrhythmias for how they actually arise and disrupt the heart's rhythm

Stable angina by itself is generally NOT considered a risk factor for heart failure — the instructor specifically called this out as something students might mistakenly assume.

Risk Factors for Heart Failure

  • Hypertension — the single greatest risk factor
  • Diabetes
  • Having had an MI — about 22% of men and 46% of women who have an MI go on to develop heart failure
  • Age over 65 — heart failure is the most common cause of hospitalization in this age group
  • Black/African-American patients have a higher incidence
  • A strong genetic/familial link
  • COPD
  • Severe anemia
  • Congenital heart defects
  • Viral myocarditis
  • Alcohol, cocaine, or crack abuse
  • Kidney disease
  • Obesity

Marijuana — unlike alcohol, cocaine, and crack — is not currently associated with heart failure risk. The instructor called this out specifically so it isn't lumped in with the other substances above.

Left-Sided vs. Right-Sided Heart Failure

Left-Sided Heart Failure

  • Blood backs up into the lungs — causes pulmonary congestion (pulmonary edema); the LV enlarges trying to compensate
  • Most common cause: poorly controlled hypertension
  • Symptoms: cough, crackles, wheezes, frothy or blood-tinged sputum, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND — sudden shortness of breath that wakes a patient at night), orthopnea (shortness of breath when lying flat — patients often sit up in a tripod position to breathe better), tachycardia, exertional dyspnea, fatigue, cyanosis, restlessness, and confusion

Right-Sided Heart Failure

  • Blood backs up into the venous system — causes systemic (body-wide) congestion; the RV enlarges trying to compensate
  • Most common cause: COPD (specifically severe COPD)
  • Symptoms: jugular venous distension (JVD), dependent edema, weight gain, hepatosplenomegaly (enlarged liver/spleen), ascites, and poor appetite/anorexia (both from venous congestion backing up into the GI tract), and fatigue

In practice, the disease usually progresses to affect both sides eventually and patients show a mix of symptoms — but for this class and NCLEX, know how to sort a symptom into left-sided (pulmonary) vs. right-sided (systemic).

MNEMONIC — "Left = lungs, right = body": left-sided failure backs up into the lungs (pulmonary); right-sided failure backs up into the rest of the body (systemic).

HFrEF vs. HFpEF

Normal ejection fraction (EF) is 55–65%. EF is measured by echocardiogram — transthoracic (TTE) or transesophageal (TEE) — and that measurement is how a patient is sorted into HFrEF or HFpEF.

HFrEF (Reduced EF) — "Systolic Heart Failure"

  • Ejection fraction under 40%
  • Reflects impaired contractile (pumping) function

HFpEF (Preserved EF) — "Diastolic Heart Failure"

  • Ejection fraction of 40–50%
  • Previously called "diastolic dysfunction"
  • The muscle can still squeeze adequately, but the ventricles can't relax and fill properly
  • Associated with older age, female sex, diabetes, obesity, and chronic hypertension

Chronic vs. Decompensated Heart Failure

The focus of this class is chronic heart failure — a progressive disease. Within that chronic course, patients can have episodes of decompensated heart failure: new or worsening signs/symptoms, usually driven by volume overload, that send them to the ER and often lead to hospitalization. Less commonly, a patient can present with entirely new-onset heart failure.

Ventricular Remodeling

In heart failure, the body inappropriately releases substances that are meant to help but end up worsening the problem — this is ventricular remodeling, and it progressively worsens heart failure over time.

Substances Inappropriately Released

  • Angiotensin II
  • Aldosterone
  • Endothelin
  • TNF-alpha
  • Catecholamines

Effects on the Heart Muscle

  • Cell death (apoptosis)
  • Hypertrophy (thickening)
  • Collagen deposits
  • Myocardial fibrosis (scarring)

Cross-reference: pharmacologic treatment aimed at interrupting this remodeling process — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, beta blockers, MRAs — is covered in full on Cardiac Pharmacology

S3 Gallop

TermDetail
S3 gallopA low-pitched heart sound heard just after S2, occurring in early diastole. In adults over 40, an S3 gallop is considered an indicator of heart failure.

Why is the left anterior descending (LAD) artery nicknamed the "widow maker"?
It supplies a large portion of the heart muscle, so a blockage there is especially dangerous and lethal.
Name the non-modifiable risk factors for CAD.
Age, family history, gender (estrogen is cardioprotective before menopause), ethnicity (Black, Hispanic, and Native American populations have higher risk), and genetics/genetic variants (SNPs).
Name the modifiable risk factors for CAD, and how much does hypertension increase risk?
Hypertension (roughly 2–3 times the risk), smoking, diabetes, obesity/physical inactivity, diet, hyperlipidemia, and depression/stress/anxiety.
What are the defining features of stable angina?
Chest pain triggered by exertion, relieved by rest, typically lasting 2–5 minutes, and treated with nitrates.
What is the MI symptom pattern, and what one feature distinguishes it from stable angina?
Pain not relieved by rest (the distinguishing feature), pain that radiates, nausea, vomiting, dyspnea, and diaphoresis.
When should a patient call 911 for chest pain?
If chest pain is not resolved within about 5 minutes, given the concern for unstable angina or a full MI.
How does the AHA define heart failure?
The heart's inability to pump enough blood to meet the body's needs.
What is the formula for cardiac output, and what three factors determine stroke volume?
CO = HR × SV. Stroke volume is determined by preload, afterload, and contractility.
In heart failure, what is the order of the hemodynamic chain?
Contractility decreases first, which leads to increased preload (fluid backs up), which then leads to increased afterload.
What causes dilated cardiomyopathy, and what type of heart failure does it lead to?
Ischemic damage, valve disease, alcohol use, and peripartum cardiomyopathy; it leads to HFrEF (reduced ejection fraction/systolic dysfunction).
What is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy associated with, and what is its major risk?
It's related to hypertension and carries a high risk of sudden cardiac death and arrhythmias.
What are the key features of restrictive cardiomyopathy?
The least common type; the ventricle becomes stiff and non-compliant (can't relax and fill), often associated with amyloid deposits, and tends to cause right-sided heart failure.
What is the single greatest risk factor for heart failure?
Hypertension.
Is stable angina by itself considered a risk factor for heart failure?
No — the instructor specifically called this out as something students might mistakenly assume.
What is the most common cause of left-sided heart failure, and what type of congestion results?
Poorly controlled hypertension; it causes pulmonary congestion because blood backs up into the lungs.
What is the most common cause of right-sided heart failure, and what type of congestion results?
COPD; it causes systemic congestion because blood backs up into the venous system.
List the symptoms of left-sided heart failure.
Cough, crackles, wheezes, frothy or blood-tinged sputum, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND), orthopnea, tachycardia, fatigue, and cyanosis.
List the symptoms of right-sided heart failure.
Jugular venous distension (JVD), dependent edema, weight gain, hepatosplenomegaly, and GI distress.
What defines HFrEF, and what does it reflect?
An ejection fraction under 40%; it reflects impaired contractile (pumping) function — also called systolic heart failure.
What defines HFpEF, and what demographics is it associated with?
An ejection fraction of 40–50% (normal EF is 55–65%) — the muscle can still squeeze adequately but the ventricles can't relax and fill properly (also called diastolic heart failure). Associated with older age, female sex, diabetes, obesity, and chronic hypertension.
How is cardiac chest pain described, versus non-cardiac chest pain?
Cardiac: diffuse and hard to pinpoint — pressure, tightness, "an elephant sitting on my chest" — often radiating to the left arm/jaw/shoulder with diaphoresis and pallor. Non-cardiac: focal — the patient can point right to where it hurts.
What is endothelial dysfunction, mechanistically, and why does it worsen ischemia?
Damage to the vessel's inner lining that makes coronary arteries vasoconstrict instead of dilating when the heart needs more oxygen — the opposite of what should happen — worsening ischemia on top of any existing plaque. Driven by diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and smoking.
How do women often present differently than the "classic" MI picture?
Hot or burning discomfort/tenderness rather than pressure or tightness; pain not always located in the chest; and nonspecific symptoms like indigestion, heartburn, nausea, and fatigue — an atypical picture that can delay recognition.
What is "decompensated" heart failure?
An episode, within chronic heart failure, of new or worsening signs/symptoms — usually driven by volume overload — that sends the patient to the ER and often leads to hospitalization (as opposed to less-common new-onset heart failure).
Besides JVD and dependent edema, what GI-related signs point to right-sided heart failure?
Ascites and poor appetite/anorexia — both caused by venous congestion backing up into the GI tract.
