Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new — it's a map of Week 5's five topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. Two pages walk the GI tract from top to bottom (esophagus/stomach, then small intestine and below); a third page sits apart from both, treating nausea and diarrhea as symptoms that show up across many different GI diagnoses rather than belonging to one disease; and the last two pages both teach a completely different organ system (adrenal glands, then thyroid/parathyroid) using the exact same excess-vs-deficiency structure. This page shows where each idea is actually taught in full, and where it just comes back as supporting detail. No flashcards or quiz questions here — just the map.

Three Clusters, Not Five Separate Topics

Five pages is easier to hold in your head than nine, but they still aren't five unrelated diseases — two are a GI-tract progression, one is a cross-cutting symptom-treatment page, and two are a matched pair of endocrine mirror-image conditions.

ClusterTopicsThe Common Thread
The GI Tract, Top to BottomEsophageal & Gastric Disorders → Lower GI DisordersThese two move down the GI tube in order: esophagus and stomach first (dysphagia, GERD, hiatal hernia, gastritis, gastroenteritis, PUD), then small intestine and below (appendicitis, peritonitis, IBS, IBD, diverticular disease). Both pages share the "-itis" suffix pattern (inflammation) and several overlapping symptoms.
Treating GI Symptoms, Not GI DiseasesGI Symptom PharmacologyNausea/vomiting and diarrhea aren't diseases of their own — they're symptoms of nearly everything in the first cluster (and beyond). This page's antiemetics and antidiarrheals get their own page because they cut across every diagnosis rather than belonging to one.
Hormone Mirror PairsEndocrine Disorders → Thyroid & Parathyroid DisordersBoth pages teach an "excess vs. deficiency" pair for the same hormone axis — Cushing's/Addison's and SIADH/diabetes insipidus on the first page, hypothyroidism/hyperthyroidism on the second — even though the actual glands and hormones involved are completely different.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, work through the two GI pages first (upper, then lower), treat GI Symptom Pharmacology as its own quick-hit drug review, then do the two endocrine pages back to back — the mirror-pair pattern from Endocrine Disorders will make Thyroid & Parathyroid Disorders click faster.

The Five Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1Esophageal & Gastric DisordersUpper GI conditions overlap heavily in symptoms (heartburn, epigastric pain, dysphagia) and treatment — H2 blockers and PPIs decrease acid, sucralfate and antacids boost mucosal protection, and the whole category runs on the same aggressive-vs-protective-factor balance.
2Lower GI DisordersLocation and pattern are everything: RLQ pain means appendicitis, LLQ pain means diverticulitis, transmural skip lesions mean Crohn's, and continuous mucosal bleeding means ulcerative colitis. Rupture/perforation of any of these can cause peritonitis.
3GI Symptom PharmacologyThree antiemetic mechanisms (serotonin blockers, H1 antihistamines, dopamine antagonists) each carry their own specific risk — serotonin syndrome, sedation/anticholinergic effects, and EPS/NMS, respectively — plus a quick antidiarrheal review.
4Endocrine DisordersThe adrenal cortex (cortisol/aldosterone/androgens) and the ADH axis each get an excess-vs-deficiency pair: Cushing's vs. Addison's, and SIADH vs. diabetes insipidus. The adrenal medulla's pheochromocytoma is the odd one out — a single-hormone excess with no deficiency counterpart taught here.
5Thyroid & Parathyroid DisordersHypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism are another excess-vs-deficiency mirror pair, this time for thyroid hormone; the parathyroid glands are covered on the same page only because they sit physically near the thyroid, not because they share its hormone.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

Every danger callout across the five pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Esophageal & Gastric DisordersBarrett's esophagus carries a threefold risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma with no pharmacologic fix once it develops. Acute gastroenteritis's real danger is dehydration from fluid loss at both ends, not the infection itself.
Lower GI DisordersSudden, complete relief of severe appendicitis pain can mean the appendix has ruptured, not that it's getting better. Toxic megacolon — a rapid, life-threatening colon dilation — is a serious complication of ulcerative colitis.
GI Symptom PharmacologyNever give hydroxyzine IV — it can cause tissue damage, thrombosis, and gangrene (IM only). Watch for serotonin syndrome when ondansetron is combined with other serotonergic drugs. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is the rare but life-threatening worst case of dopamine-blocker-induced EPS.
Endocrine DisordersCorticosteroid therapy in Addison's disease can never be abruptly stopped — doing so can precipitate a life-threatening Addisonian crisis. Pheochromocytoma's severe hypertension risks stroke or cardiac events even though the tumor itself is usually benign. Uncontrolled diabetes insipidus can progress to fatal hypovolemic shock.
Thyroid & Parathyroid DisordersMyxedema coma (severe, prolonged, untreated hypothyroidism) and thyroid storm (severe hyperthyroidism, death within 48 hours if untreated) are mirror-image emergencies at opposite ends of the same thyroid-hormone axis.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Too much and too little are taught the same way, three times over." Cushing's/Addison's, SIADH/diabetes insipidus, and hypothyroidism/hyperthyroidism all use the identical excess-vs-deficiency structure — once you can predict Cushing's findings from "too much cortisol," you can predict all six conditions the same way.
  • "A suffix is a shortcut, not a coincidence." "-itis" means inflammation (gastritis, appendicitis, peritonitis, diverticulitis) and "-oma" typically means a benign tumor (pheochromocytoma) — both patterns hold up across this entire week and beyond it.
  • "Location plus pattern equals diagnosis." RLQ vs. LLQ pain, transmural vs. mucosal-only inflammation, and skip lesions vs. continuous involvement are the exact features that separate look-alike lower GI diagnoses from each other.
  • "The same drug can cause two unrelated problems." Lithium causes both nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and goiter/Hashimoto's risk; amiodarone causes either hypo- or hyperthyroidism — a reminder to check the med list whenever an endocrine picture doesn't add up.
  • "Symptom drugs aren't disease drugs." GI Symptom Pharmacology's antiemetics and antidiarrheals show up as supporting treatment across nearly every diagnosis in both GI pages — they're organized by symptom instead of by disease for exactly that reason.
