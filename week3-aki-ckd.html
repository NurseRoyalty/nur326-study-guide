Acute Kidney Injury & Chronic Kidney Disease

Pyelonephritis, glomerulonephritis, and the other conditions on Renal Disorders are some of the underlying causes referenced throughout this page

The Kidneys' Key Jobs

Why Kidney Injury Affects So Much of the Body

  • Maintain fluid and electrolyte homeostasis — this underlies blood pressure regulation and third-spacing.
  • Rid the body of water-soluble waste via urine (fat-soluble waste leaves via stool instead) — including many water-soluble drugs.
  • Produce erythropoietin, which stimulates red blood cell production in the bone marrow.
  • Activate vitamin D, necessary for calcium absorption and bone health.
  • Produce renin, the first step of the renin-angiotensin system that helps regulate blood pressure.

Renal blood flow is about 1 liter per minute — roughly 20% of cardiac output — carried by an organ that's only about 0.5% of body mass. The kidneys are extremely dependent on perfusion, which is exactly why they're so vulnerable to sudden injury.

TRICK: Every manifestation on this page traces back to one of these five jobs failing. Can't manage fluid/lytes → edema and electrolyte imbalances. Can't rid the body of waste → azotemia/uremia. Can't make erythropoietin → anemia. Can't activate vitamin D → weak bones (renal osteodystrophy). Before memorizing the manifestation lists below, try predicting them from this list first.

GFR — The Indicator of Kidney Function

GFRWhat It Means
Greater than 90Normal.
About 25% of normal (~25–30 mL/hr)Renal insufficiency — the body can compensate for a while, but the longer this state continues, the more likely it progresses to a chronic disease state.

You don't need to memorize every GFR cutoff, but do know that greater than 90 is normal and roughly 25% of that marks insufficiency — GFR is the shared indicator used for both AKI and CKD staging below.

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

What's Happening

  • Usually the result of an ischemic injury from volume loss — the intravascular space loses volume and the kidneys stop getting the perfusion they need (remember: 1 liter/minute through an organ that's 0.5% of body mass).
  • Common causes: acute blood loss, third-spacing (as in sepsis), or toxin/drug exposure (including an overdose state).
  • The body's inflammatory response to the injury is meant to help, but often makes things worse — it increases vascular changes and kidney cell death.
  • AKI can happen within hours and is potentially reversible — the kidneys can heal over time depending on how severe and how prolonged the injury was.
ClassificationWhere the Problem IsExample Causes
Prerenal (most common)Before blood reaches the kidney — a volume problem.Blood loss during surgery, dehydration.

Postrenal (least common)An obstruction downstream of the kidney.A blockage such as a tumor (see the urinary obstruction section on the Renal Disorders page).
TRICK: Think of it like a traffic jam relative to the kidney. Prerenal = the block is before the kidney (not enough blood arrives). Intrarenal = the damage is inside the kidney itself. Postrenal = the block is after the kidney (urine can't get out).
Finding
Oliguria — less than 400 mL in 24 hours (roughly less than 30 mL/hour). Onset typically begins one day after a hypotensive event (surgery, blood loss, dehydration) and lasts one to three weeks.
Fluid volume excess, since filtration isn't happening properly.
Metabolic acidosis — the kidneys are heavily involved in acid-base balance.
Hyponatremia or hyperkalemia — electrolyte management is impaired.
Azotemia/uremia — waste products accumulate in the blood instead of being filtered out.
Neurologic changes, especially once severe.

Treatment Principles

  • Address the cause: replace volume for a prerenal problem; look for an antidote or flush the system for an intrarenal toxin/overdose problem.
  • Treat the manifestations as they appear — correct electrolyte imbalances, manage fluid shifts, and avoid any additional nephrotoxic drugs or medications.
  • Monitor labs closely and watch for progression toward chronic kidney disease.

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

Staging & Progression

  • CKD can be caused by AKI, but more often develops from ongoing chronic conditions. Staging is based on GFR, the same indicator used for AKI. Stages 1–2 are usually asymptomatic; hypertension appears with moderate GFR decline; the rest of the classic manifestations appear as GFR drops further.
  • Stage 5 is considered uremic — uremia is the presence of urea (an excess amino acid/protein metabolism end-product) in the blood, essentially meaning the body can no longer make and excrete its own urine.
  • There is no drug that reverses CKD. Management for mild-to-moderate stages focuses on cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk reduction — controlling whatever chronic condition is driving it (diabetes, hypertension, or an unresolved AKI).
Cause of End-Stage Renal DiseaseShare of Cases
Diabetes~50%
Hypertension~30%
Other (glomerulonephritis, unresolved AKI, etc.)~20%

The best treatment for CKD is prevention — controlling the diabetes and hypertension that cause the large majority of cases. Other risk factors: a family history of CKD, other vascular disease (CAD, hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis), age over 60, male sex, Black/African American race, smoking (causes endothelial damage), and obesity.

The Cyclical Pathogenesis

  • Whatever reduces blood flow to kidney cells starts a self-worsening cycle. Two findings are the biggest indicators and drivers of worsening CKD: proteinuria (a leaky, damaged GBM lets protein through, and that protein accumulating in the interstitial space further activates inflammation) and increased angiotensin II (a powerful vasoconstrictor that drives hypertension and further damages the glomerulus).
  • This is why blood pressure control and monitoring the urine for protein are such consistent priorities in CKD care.

Manifestations by Body System

Fluid & ElectrolyteEdema, third-spacing, hyperkalemia (a major concern in both CKD and AKI), hyperphosphatemia, hypermagnesemia, metabolic acidosis.
NeurologicFatigue, headaches, sleep disturbances, and — in severe cases — encephalopathy and mental status changes (azotemia crossing the blood-brain barrier). Anxiety and depression are also common, though less emphasized.
CardiovascularHypertension (from angiotensin II), which can progress to heart failure and coronary artery disease; also pericarditis and peripheral artery disease.

GIAnorexia, nausea/vomiting, and it can contribute to gastritis or GI bleeding.
IntegumentaryItching, redness, and dry, scaly skin — toxins building up in the skin can even produce a coating called uremic frost.
HematologicChronic anemia from lost erythropoietin production — hemoglobin can fall as low as 5–6 g/dL, but because the decline is so slow, patients often have fewer symptoms than someone who lost the same amount of blood acutely.
MusculoskeletalRenal osteodystrophy — weakened, more fracture-prone bones from the loss of activated vitamin D (think of hip fractures in older CKD patients).

Pharmacotherapy for CKD

There is no drug that fixes the kidneys or reverses CKD — treatment slows progression and manages complications. Blood pressure goal: systolic under 140, diastolic under 90 (not lowered too aggressively, since CKD kidneys are used to a higher perfusion pressure) — typically an ACE inhibitor or an ARB, never both together. Cholesterol goal: under 200, usually with a statin.

ComplicationTreatment
Volume OverloadA diuretic (usually furosemide) plus a lower-sodium diet (around 2 g/day).
HyperkalemiaA diuretic to lower potassium; other agents such as Kayexalate; dialysis at more severe stages also corrects this.
Metabolic AcidosisSodium bicarbonate — an alkalinizing agent.
Hyperphosphatemia / Renal OsteodystrophyCalcium carbonate (binds phosphate — calcium and phosphate move inversely, like a teeter-totter) and calcitriol (activated vitamin D, since the kidney can no longer activate it itself).

DrugDetail
Sodium BicarbonateOral, alkalinizing agent — lowers pH and raises bicarb levels. Goal bicarb 18–20; started when bicarb is under 15. Slows CKD progression, helps prevent bone loss, improves nutritional status. Can cause bloating; otherwise safe.
Calcium Carbonate (Tums)Not a true phosphate binder, but binds phosphate to lower it and reduce mortality. Take with meals. Can cause hypercalcemia — monitor both calcium and phosphate levels.

Erythropoietin black-box warning: know it and use it appropriately — overcorrecting hemoglobin raises the risk of stroke, MI, and thromboembolism (see the cross-reference below).

Renal Dosing — Why It Matters

  • The kidneys are the primary filter for most medications. When kidney function drops significantly, drug elimination slows and doses/frequency often need adjusting downward — this is called renal dosing.
  • Common examples: digoxin, diabetic medications (glyburide, metformin), vancomycin (its own dedicated renal dosing protocol), and opioids.
Opioids in severe CKD: a patient with severe, poorly-filtering CKD will retain opioids far longer than normal, risking significant respiratory depression. This is the classic "most concerning medication" answer for a CKD patient in pain.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What are the kidneys' five key jobs?
Fluid/electrolyte homeostasis, ridding the body of water-soluble waste, producing erythropoietin, activating vitamin D, and producing renin.
What GFR is considered normal, and what marks renal insufficiency?
Greater than 90 is normal; about 25% of that (roughly 25–30 mL/hr) marks insufficiency.
What are the three classifications of AKI, and what does each mean?
Prerenal (a volume problem before the kidney, most common), intrarenal (direct damage inside the kidney, e.g. acute tubular necrosis), and postrenal (an obstruction after the kidney, least common).
What defines oliguria in AKI, and when does it typically begin?
Less than 400 mL in 24 hours (about less than 30 mL/hour), typically beginning one day after a hypotensive event.
Is AKI usually reversible?
Yes — the kidneys can heal over time depending on how severe and how prolonged the injury was, though prevention is still the goal.
What two conditions cause about 80% of end-stage renal disease combined?
Diabetes (~50%) and hypertension (~30%).
What are the two biggest drivers/indicators of worsening chronic kidney disease?
Proteinuria and increased angiotensin II.
Why does CKD cause chronic anemia, and how is it different from acute blood-loss anemia?
The kidneys can no longer produce erythropoietin. Because the hemoglobin drop happens so slowly, patients often have fewer symptoms than someone who lost the same amount of blood suddenly.
What is renal osteodystrophy, and what causes it?
Weakened, fracture-prone bones from the kidneys' loss of the ability to activate vitamin D, which impairs calcium absorption.
Why is calcium carbonate given for high phosphate levels?
Calcium and phosphate move inversely — giving calcium binds and lowers phosphate.
What's the black-box warning concern with erythropoietin?
Overcorrecting hemoglobin (giving it when hemoglobin is already above 10 g/dL) raises the risk of stroke, MI, and thromboembolism.
Why are opioids especially dangerous in a patient with severe CKD?
The kidneys aren't filtering effectively, so opioids are retained much longer than normal — raising the risk of significant respiratory depression.
