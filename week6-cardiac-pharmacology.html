Antianginal Drugs

For the underlying pathophysiology of stable angina, see Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure. Four drug categories are used for stable angina, each acting on a different piece of the heart's oxygen supply/demand balance.

How Each Category Works

  • Nitrates — decrease preload via venodilation
  • Beta blockers — decrease heart rate and contractility (pull-forward — review your drug matrix for full profile)
  • Calcium channel blockers — decrease afterload and heart rate/contractility (pull-forward — review your drug matrix for full profile)
  • Ranolazine — improves myocardial energy efficiency (the exact mechanism wasn't detailed further)

Stepped treatment approach for stable angina: start with a beta blocker or a calcium channel blocker plus a nitrate, reassess in about 3 months, and escalate treatment if needed.

Nitroglycerin FormulationDetail

Topical ointment (Nitro-Bid)Another topical formulation used for antianginal therapy.
IV nitroglycerinICU use only. Requires a glass IV bottle and special (non-standard) IV tubing.
Long-acting oral (isosorbide)Used for prevention only, not for an active attack. Must be tapered when discontinued to avoid rebound coronary vasospasm. If breakthrough chest pain occurs, the patient should NOT take an extra isosorbide dose — use sublingual nitroglycerin instead.

Nitroglycerin — Mechanism & Watch-Fors

  • Mechanism: venodilation (↓ preload), with some arterial dilation too — across every formulation above
  • Side effects: headache, hypotension, reflex tachycardia (from the vasodilation itself)
  • Tolerance: develops rapidly with continued use
  • Dangerous interaction: severe hypotension with sildenafil, some antihypertensives, or alcohol
MNEMONIC — the nitrate "3 H's": Headache, Hypotension, reflex tacHycardia. All three come from the same vasodilating mechanism that treats the angina in the first place.
If chest pain is not relieved within 5 minutes of a sublingual nitroglycerin dose, the patient should call 911 — this raises concern for unstable angina or STEMI, not just stable angina.

Ranolazine (Ranexa) the exception

  • Oral only — the one antianginal here with no injectable/topical form
  • Key risk: can prolong the QT interval → risk of lethal dysrhythmias
  • Unlike the others: does NOT drop blood pressure or heart rate the way nitrates do
  • Use caution with renal or hepatic impairment
  • Side effects: headache, dizziness, nausea, constipation
  • CYP3A4 inhibitor — avoid grapefruit juice and other CYP3A4-interacting drugs

Heart Failure Drugs

Heart failure drugs are broadly used across left- vs. right-sided and systolic (HFrEF) vs. diastolic (HFpEF) heart failure, though HFpEF tends to respond somewhat less well to these medications than HFrEF does. For the underlying pathophysiology, see Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure.

THE 4 PILLARS OF HFrEF THERAPY: modern heart failure treatment rests on four drug classes, each doing a different job. Diuretics, nitrates, and digoxin are added on top of these four as needed — they aren't part of the core pillars.

1 — RAS Inhibitor ACEI / ARB / ARNI

The "unloader" — ↓ preload & afterload, suppresses aldosterone, interrupts remodeling. Only ONE of the three at a time, never combined.

2 — Beta Blocker carvedilol preferred

The "calmer" — counteracts the chronic sympathetic overactivation that drives remodeling.

3 — MRA spironolactone

The "de-congester" — suppresses sodium/water retention to offload the left ventricle (not primarily acting as a diuretic here).

4 — SGLT2 Inhibitor the "-gliflozins"

The newest pillar — helps "unload" the ventricle and supports cardiac metabolism, even in patients without diabetes.

Pillar 1 — RAS Inhibitors (ACE Inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs)

  • ACE inhibitors & ARBs are pull-forward drugs — review your drug matrix for full mechanism/side-effect detail.
  • ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan): an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor combo, thought to be the most effective of the three because the neprilysin component adds an extra anti-remodeling effect beyond ACEI/ARB alone. Newer, more expensive, and harder to access.
  • A patient is on only one of the three at a time — never combined.
  • Mortality benefit is clearest in HFrEF; some benefit is seen in HFpEF too.
  • Titrated to the highest tolerated dose.
  • Watch for: an ACE-inhibitor-specific cough; elevated potassium (all three); hypotension (some).
MNEMONIC: ACE inhibitors end in "‑pril" and cause the classic dry cough; ARBs end in "‑sartan" and generally don't. If a patient can't tolerate an ACE inhibitor's cough, an ARB is the usual swap.

Pillar 2 — Beta Blockers in Heart Failure

  • Carvedilol is used most frequently, because it provides both beta AND alpha blockade.
  • In heart failure, the sympathetic nervous system becomes chronically overactivated (neurohormonal activation) as a compensatory response that ultimately worsens the disease — beta blockers counteract this.
  • Help protect against dysrhythmias and interrupt the remodeling process.
  • Watch for: hypotension — patients are asked to monitor their own BP at home.

Pillar 3 — Spironolactone MRA

  • Previously learned as a potassium-sparing diuretic — but here it's not primarily used for that diuretic effect.
  • Suppresses sodium/water retention to offload the LV; goal is keeping patients as fluid-"dry" as possible.
  • ↓ hospitalizations and ↓ deaths.
  • Watch for: hyperkalemia — monitor kidney function closely, since high K⁺ raises dysrhythmia risk in an already "sick heart."

Pillar 4 — SGLT2 Inhibitors the "‑gliflozins"

  • e.g. empagliflozin, dapagliflozin — originally diabetes medications.
  • Exact HF mechanism isn't fully understood: thought to "unload" the ventricle via a natriuresis/osmotic-diuresis-like effect and to support cardiac metabolism.
  • Can be used even in HF patients without diabetes, for the cardioprotective effect alone.
  • ↓ hospital readmissions, mortality, and morbidity.

Loop Diuretics e.g. furosemide — Symptom Relief, Not Survival

  • Most HF hospital admissions are from fluid overload — the failing heart can't maintain adequate BP/oncotic pressure.
  • Most HF patients are on one, usually a loop diuretic — oral at home, or IV/IV drip when hospitalized.
  • Biggest concerns: potassium imbalance (↑ dysrhythmia risk) and hypotension.
  • A patient on digoxin who becomes hypokalemic from a diuretic is at high risk for digoxin toxicity — these two drugs are a classic risky combination.
  • Despite near-universal use, diuretics give NO survival benefit — symptom relief only.

Inotropic drugs try to strengthen the weakened heart muscle itself. Digitalis (digoxin) is the one inotrope covered in depth below. Sympathomimetic inotropes — dopamine, dobutamine, and milrinone — are also IV-drip-only inotropes that increase contractility and cardiac output, but are complex-care-level topics not detailed further at this level.

Digoxin (Digitalis) from the foxglove plant

  • Historically first-line for nearly all heart failure — fallen out of favor (weak survival benefit, high rhythm-disturbance risk) but still used, including for AFib.
  • Mechanism: inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump in cardiac cells → calcium accumulates intracellularly → ↑ contractility (a cell-level calcium effect — NOT related to dietary calcium/milk intake).
  • Also ↑ renal blood flow and ↓ sympathetic stimulation, which helps ↓ heart rate.

Digoxin Toxicity — Who's at Risk

  • Major risks: cardiac dysrhythmias (V-tach, AFib, flutter, and more) and toxicity itself.
  • Higher risk: older adults, women, and patients also on diuretics (diuretic-induced hypokalemia predisposes to toxicity — and most hospitalized HF patients ARE on a diuretic).

Digoxin Toxicity — Signs & Prevention

  • Signs: bradycardia (the hallmark), headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, visual disturbances.
  • Prevent: lowest effective dose; weekly digoxin levels (like INR monitoring, not peak/trough); supplement potassium as kidney function allows.
  • Antidote for severe toxicity: digoxin immune Fab, for significantly symptomatic/unstable patients.
MNEMONIC — digoxin toxicity is "SLOW & BLURRY": SLOW (bradycardia — the hallmark sign) and BLURRY (visual disturbances), plus headache, dizziness, confusion, and nausea along the way.
Take the patient's apical pulse for a full minute before administering digoxin, and hold the dose if the pulse is below 60 bpm — then notify the provider and monitor the rhythm. Bradycardia is the hallmark sign of digoxin toxicity.

Rate & Rhythm Control Drugs

Cross-reference: for the rhythms these drugs are treating, see Cardiac Dysrhythmias

Beta blockers and calcium channel blockers (already-known pull-forward drug classes) are used here too — for example, to treat sinus tachycardia once any underlying cause (fever, volume depletion, etc.) has been addressed and ruled out as the primary fix, or for rate control (and sometimes rhythm conversion) in atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter. Beta blockers are generally tried first.

MNEMONIC: Atropine speeds up a heart that's too SLOW. Adenosine briefly STOPS a heart that's racing too fast and narrow (PSVT). Amiodarone and dofetilide are the "big guns" for AFib/flutter conversion and maintenance.

Amiodarone the broad-spectrum antidysrhythmic

  • Used for: essentially any dysrhythmia — AFib, atrial flutter, ventricular dysrhythmias, and SVT. Effective at converting AFib to sinus rhythm.
  • Mechanism: prolongs the action potential duration and effective refractory period in all cardiac tissue; blocks both alpha and beta sympathetic receptors.
  • Contraindicated in severe bradycardia or heart block.
  • Forms: IV for an acute/emergent situation (e.g. a very tachycardic, symptomatic AFib patient); oral for ongoing maintenance after transitioning off an IV drip.
  • Interactions: with digoxin (↑ digoxin toxicity risk — typically avoided together); with warfarin (can ↑ INR by 50–100%).
  • Half-life: very long — remains in the system for days, so adverse effects can take 2–3 months to fully resolve after stopping. Requires substantial ongoing lab monitoring.
MNEMONIC — amiodarone "TELLS" you where it can hurt: Thyroid (hypo- or hyperthyroidism, from its iodine content), Eyes (corneal deposits — light sensitivity, dry eye, halos), Lungs (pulmonary toxicity — black box warning, ~10% fatal when it occurs), Liver (hepatotoxicity). It can also be pro-arrhythmic — sometimes triggering a new arrhythmia instead of only treating one. About 75% of patients on amiodarone experience some adverse effect, and it's highly lipophilic, accumulating in adipose tissue.

Atropine for slow & symptomatic

  • Given for symptomatic sinus bradycardia only — never for an asymptomatic slow rate.
  • Anticholinergic/antimuscarinic — blocks the vagus nerve (vagolytic action).
  • Only works if bradycardia is vagally induced (though it's often tried regardless, since the cause isn't always known immediately) — works in only about 30% of cases.
  • Dosing: IV push only, 1 mg every 3–5 min, max 3 mg total.
  • Side effects (anticholinergic): dry mouth, blurry vision, light sensitivity, tachycardia, flushing, hot skin.

Adenosine for fast & narrow

  • Used for SVT/PSVT (occasionally sinus tach), but really intended for PSVT.
  • Mechanism: slows AV node conduction.
  • Extremely short half-life — repeat doses often needed.
  • Causes a brief, expected pause in rhythm (transient asystole) as it works — shock pads are typically placed as a precaution.
  • Dosing: IV push only — 6 mg, then up to 12 mg, then another 12 mg if still needed.
  • Must be given through a three-way stopcock immediately followed by a rapid saline flush, or it won't reach circulation fast enough to work.
Never give atropine without the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring — heart rate and rhythm must be watched closely as it's given. Only give it for a truly symptomatic patient, not simply because a heart rate happens to be low on the monitor.

Dofetilide convert & maintain

  • Converts AFib or atrial flutter into normal sinus rhythm and then maintains it — a maintenance drug, unlike acute/one-time adenosine or atropine.
  • Mechanism: selectively blocks cardiac potassium ion channels.
  • Major risk: torsades de pointes (a fast, polymorphic form of V-tach — dangerous and potentially deadly; start CPR if it occurs) as well as new-onset SVT.
  • Side effects: headache, dizziness, chest pain.
  • Not given to patients with an already-prolonged QT interval or on other QT-prolonging medications.
Because of the torsades de pointes risk, dofetilide is always started in the hospital while the patient is on continuous telemetry monitoring, and it is not given to patients with a prolonged QT interval or on other QT-prolonging medications.

QT-prolonger tie-together: ranolazine (antianginal) and dofetilide (antidysrhythmic) both carry QT-prolongation risk — worth remembering as a pair, since combining QT-prolonging drugs compounds the torsades de pointes risk.

In addition to rate/rhythm-control drugs, patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter also generally need to be on warfarin (or a similar anticoagulant) because of stroke risk — go back and review warfarin's indications and nursing care from your prior pharmacology coursework.

What does nitroglycerin do to preload, and by what mechanism?
It decreases preload via venodilation, with some arterial dilation as well.
How often, and how many times, can sublingual nitroglycerin be given for an anginal attack?
Every 5 minutes as needed, up to 3 doses total.
What should a patient do if chest pain isn't relieved within 5 minutes of sublingual nitroglycerin?
Call 911 — this raises concern for unstable angina or STEMI.
Why must isosorbide be tapered rather than stopped abruptly?
To avoid rebound coronary vasospasm.
What should a patient on isosorbide do for breakthrough chest pain, instead of taking an extra isosorbide dose?
Use sublingual nitroglycerin instead.
What is ranolazine's main dysrhythmia risk, and why does it require avoiding grapefruit juice?
It can prolong the QT interval; it's a CYP3A4 inhibitor, so grapefruit juice and other CYP3A4-interacting drugs should be avoided.
How many RAS-inhibitor drug types (ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI) can a heart failure patient be on at once?
Only one — they are never combined in the same patient.
Why is the ARNI sacubitril/valsartan thought to be more effective than an ACE inhibitor or ARB alone?
Its neprilysin inhibitor component adds an extra effect on cardiac remodeling beyond what ACE inhibitors or ARBs alone provide.
Which beta blocker is used most frequently in heart failure, and why?
Carvedilol, because it provides both beta and alpha blockade.
Is spironolactone used in heart failure mainly for its diuretic effect?
No — it helps suppress sodium and water retention to offload/decongest the left ventricle, and is associated with decreased hospitalizations and deaths.
Why does diuretic therapy in a heart failure patient raise the risk of digoxin toxicity?
Diuretics can cause hypokalemia, and low potassium increases the risk of digoxin toxicity.
Do diuretics improve survival in heart failure?
No — despite being used in nearly all hospitalized heart failure patients, diuretics provide no survival/mortality benefit; they're used for symptom relief only.
What is the cellular mechanism by which digoxin increases myocardial contractility?
It inhibits the sodium-potassium ATPase pump in cardiac cells, causing calcium to accumulate intracellularly, which increases contractility (a cell-level effect, not related to dietary calcium).
Which patients are at higher risk for digoxin toxicity?
Older adults, women, and patients also on diuretic therapy.
What is the hallmark sign of digoxin toxicity, and what should the nurse do before giving a dose?
Bradycardia is the hallmark sign; take a full 1-minute apical pulse before giving digoxin, and hold the dose if it's below 60 bpm.
What does amiodarone do to the cardiac action potential, and which receptors does it block?
It prolongs the action potential duration and effective refractory period in all cardiac tissue, and blocks both alpha and beta receptors.
Why can amiodarone cause thyroid problems?
It can cause either hypo- or hyperthyroidism, related to its iodine content.
What does amiodarone do to a patient's INR if they're also on warfarin?
It can increase INR by 50–100%.
Is atropine given for asymptomatic sinus bradycardia?
No — atropine is given for symptomatic sinus bradycardia only.
How must adenosine be administered, and why?
IV push through a three-way stopcock, immediately followed by a rapid saline flush — otherwise it won't reach systemic circulation quickly enough to work, since its half-life is so short.
Name the "4 pillars" of HFrEF drug therapy.
A RAS inhibitor (ACE inhibitor, ARB, or ARNI — only one at a time), a beta blocker (carvedilol preferred), an MRA (spironolactone), and an SGLT2 inhibitor.
What are the "3 H's" of nitrate side effects?
Headache, Hypotension, and reflex tacHycardia — all from the same vasodilating mechanism that treats the angina.
What does the "TELLS" mnemonic cover for amiodarone?
Thyroid dysfunction, Eyes (corneal deposits), Lungs (pulmonary toxicity — black box warning), Liver (hepatotoxicity) — plus a pro-arrhythmic effect. About 75% of patients on amiodarone get some adverse effect.
For a symptomatic, very fast, narrow-QRS rhythm with no visible P wave (PSVT), which drug is the go-to? What about a symptomatic, slow sinus rhythm?
Adenosine for PSVT (briefly blocks AV node conduction). Atropine for symptomatic sinus bradycardia (blocks vagal slowing).
Which two cardiac drugs on this page share a QT-prolongation risk?
Ranolazine (antianginal) and dofetilide (antidysrhythmic) — combining QT-prolonging drugs compounds the risk of torsades de pointes.
