Liver Anatomy & Key Cells

TermDetail
Size & LocationThe largest organ in the body — weighs about 3 pounds and receives 5–7% of total cardiac output. Sits in the right epigastric region, enclosed in a fibrous capsule, divided into left and right lobes.
LobulesThe functional units of the liver. Made up of hepatocytes (liver cells) arranged around a central vein.
HepatocytesThe liver's main working cells. One of the few cell types in the body that can regenerate — if the remaining liver tissue is healthy, lost liver tissue can regrow.
Kupffer CellsLine the liver's inner capillaries (sinusoids). Responsible for removing bacteria and toxins from the blood — the liver's built-in blood filter.

What the Liver Actually Does

Understanding these five jobs is what makes the rest of Week 4 make sense — every major finding in hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver failure is really just "one of these five jobs stopped happening."

The Liver's Five Jobs

  • Metabolizes and stores fat, carbohydrate, protein, vitamins, and minerals.
  • Blood volume reservoir — distends and compresses to hold or release blood based on circulating volume.
  • Blood filter — Kupffer cells purify the blood; the liver also removes bilirubin, a byproduct of hemoglobin breakdown.
  • Makes clotting factors, including prothrombin and fibrinogen.
  • Metabolizes and detoxifies drugs.
MNEMONIC: When the liver fails, work backward through its five jobs to predict the labs — no metabolism (glucose/protein problems), no reservoir/filter function (bilirubin and toxins build up), no clotting factors (bleeding), no drug metabolism (drugs accumulate). Every abnormal value on the next table is just one of these five jobs not happening anymore.

Portal Circulation

How Blood Reaches the Liver

  • The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood to the liver from the stomach, intestines, spleen, and pancreas — the lower digestive organs.
  • This blood is rich in nutrients absorbed from the intestinal tract, which is exactly why the liver has so much to do with metabolism — digested nutrients go straight to the liver before reaching the rest of the body.
  • This is also where the first-pass effect comes from — an oral drug passes through the portal circulation and liver before reaching systemic circulation, which is why oral doses are often higher than IV doses of the same drug.

Bilirubin is also part of the liver workup, but it gets its own section below since it's the key to understanding jaundice.

Jaundice (Icterus)

Jaundice and icterus are the same thing — a yellowish discoloration of the skin and deep tissues caused by elevated bilirubin. It usually isn't visible until bilirubin reaches about 2–2.5 mg/dL (normal is roughly 0.6–1 mg/dL).

Conjugated (Direct) Bilirubin — ~30%

  • Bilirubin the liver has already processed.
  • Elevated when conjugated bilirubin can't get OUT of the liver — a bile duct obstruction (gallstones).
  • The liver is doing its job; the bile just can't flow out.

Unconjugated (Indirect) Bilirubin — ~70%

  • Bilirubin the liver has NOT yet processed.
  • Elevated with bilirubin overproduction (hemolysis) OR impaired liver function — the liver can't conjugate it in the first place.
  • Some liver conditions and hepatitis cases elevate both types at once.
ClassificationWhat's Actually HappeningBilirubin Pattern
Hemolytic JaundiceToo many red blood cells are being broken down (excess RBCs, internal bleeding, hemolysis).Unconjugated (indirect) — bilirubin overproduction outpaces the liver's ability to conjugate it.
Hepatocellular JaundiceThe liver itself isn't working — it can't take up or conjugate bilirubin from the blood.Unconjugated (indirect) — the liver never gets the chance to conjugate it.
Obstructive JaundiceNot actually a liver problem — bile can't flow OUT of the liver, most often from gallstones.Conjugated (direct) — the liver already did its job; the bile is just blocked.

Recognizing Jaundice at the Bedside

Clinical Signs

  • Yellowish hue to skin and deep tissues — rarely as bright as it looks on TV. The sclera of the eyes is the best place to look, especially in a darker-skinned patient.
  • Palms, soles, and mucous membranes can also show discoloration.
  • Dark amber urine that foams when shaken — conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and backs up into the blood, then the urine.
  • Stool can be normal or clay-colored — bile salts normally give stool its brown color; if bile flow is obstructed, that pigment never reaches the stool.
  • Pruritus (itching) — backed-up bile salts get deposited in the skin. Sometimes localized to palms/soles, sometimes generalized.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What are the liver's five major jobs?
Metabolize/store nutrients, act as a blood volume reservoir, filter the blood (including bilirubin removal), make clotting factors, and metabolize/detoxify drugs.
What are Kupffer cells and what do they do?
Cells lining the liver's sinusoids (inner capillaries) that remove bacteria and toxins from the blood.
What is unique about hepatocytes compared to most other cells in the body?
They can regenerate — if the remaining liver is healthy, lost liver tissue can regrow.
What does the hepatic portal vein carry, and from where?
Deoxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the stomach, intestines, spleen, and pancreas to the liver.
With liver failure, which direction do liver enzymes (AST/ALT/Alk Phos) and ammonia trend?
Both go UP.
With liver failure, which direction do total protein, albumin, and PT trend?
Total protein and albumin go DOWN; PT goes UP (prolonged) — the liver isn't making enough protein or clotting factors.
At approximately what bilirubin level does jaundice become visible?
About 2–2.5 mg/dL (normal is roughly 0.6–1 mg/dL).
What's the difference between conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin?
Conjugated (direct, ~30%) has already been processed by the liver. Unconjugated (indirect, ~70%) has not.
A patient has elevated CONJUGATED (direct) bilirubin. What does that point to?
An obstruction — the liver is conjugating bilirubin fine, but it can't get OUT (classically a gallstone blocking the bile duct).
A patient has elevated UNCONJUGATED (indirect) bilirubin. What two categories of cause does that point to?
Bilirubin overproduction (hemolysis) OR impaired liver function — either way, the liver hasn't conjugated it.
Where is the best place to assess for jaundice, especially in a darker-skinned patient?
The sclera of the eyes.
Why does obstructed bile flow cause both dark urine AND clay-colored stool?
Conjugated bilirubin backs up into the blood and spills into the urine (dark, foamy). Bile salts never reach the intestines to color the stool brown, so it turns clay-colored instead.
