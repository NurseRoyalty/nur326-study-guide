Hypertension & Hyperlipidemia (HTN/HLD)

NUR 325 review, covering: cholesterol and lipoprotein basics, diagnosing hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, lipid-lowering pharmacology, blood pressure regulation (the RAS system), hypertension classification and complications, and antihypertensive pharmacology.

Cholesterol Basics — Lipoproteins & Where Cholesterol Comes From

Cholesterol is essential — it's part of every cell membrane, a building block for hormones (estrogen, testosterone, cortisol) and vitamin D, a key ingredient in bile acid, and it helps the skin retain water. It's highly insoluble, so it has to be transported into cells by binding cell-surface receptors (mostly on arterial muscle cells).

SourceDetail
Exogenous CholesterolFrom diet — only about a quarter of the body's cholesterol. The 2015 USDA Dietary Guidelines dropped the old 300 mg/day dietary limit after the AHA/ACC found cholesterol intake itself isn't a nutrient of concern for overconsumption.
Endogenous CholesterolMade by the liver — about three-quarters of the body's cholesterol, via the HMG-CoA reductase pathway. The liver responds to diet: more dietary cholesterol only minimally raises levels, but more dietary saturated fat can raise cholesterol by 15–25% — so the real dietary target is limiting saturated/trans fat, not cholesterol itself.

Liver cells have LDL receptors. LDL from the blood binds these receptors, and HMG-CoA reductase uses it to produce cholesterol. This is a negative feedback loop: high LDL/cholesterol turns down HMG-CoA reductase production; if cholesterol production is blocked, the liver responds by making more LDL receptors to pull more LDL out of the blood.

LipoproteinDetail
HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein)The "good" cholesterol — more protein, less cholesterol. Higher HDL decreases risk of adverse cardiovascular events.
LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein)The "bad" cholesterol — less protein, more cholesterol ("L for lousy," or "L for lower"). Increases atherosclerosis risk.
VLDL (Very-Low-Density Lipoprotein)Mostly triglycerides, so it isn't measured directly — high triglycerides suggest high VLDL, which also raises heart disease risk.

Diagnosing Hyperlipidemia — Screening, Targets & Familial Hypercholesterolemia

Hypercholesterolemia (too much cholesterol) is also called hyperlipidemia (high lipid levels, the term you'll hear most) or dyslipidemia (a lipid problem generally). Diagnosed via a fasting blood draw (fasting overnight — a recent meal can skew results); high-lipid serum looks pink, opaque, and cloudy compared to normal, clear, yellowish serum. There usually aren't symptoms until it's severe, so routine screening in adults and high-risk children is how it's caught early.

MeasureTarget
Total CholesterolBelow 200. Calculated as HDL + LDL + triglycerides ÷ 5.
HDLAbove 55 (females), above 45 (males); above 60 is optimal for both.
LDLBelow 130 generally, below 100 for higher-risk patients (diabetes, other risk factors), and as low as below 70 for the highest-risk patients.
TriglyceridesBelow 160.

NCLEX-style exams provide normal lab ranges as a reference — you don't need to memorize exact numbers, but you should recognize what an abnormal cholesterol panel looks like.

Familial Hypercholesterolemia

  • A genetically-linked, familial pattern of high cholesterol caused by a defect in liver LDL receptors — the liver can't efficiently pull LDL out of the blood, so cholesterol stays elevated despite diet, exercise, and a healthy lifestyle.
  • Suspect it in children or people in their early 20s with high cholesterol, especially with a strong family pattern.
  • Inheriting the genetic variant from both parents can cause severe hypercholesterolemia in young children, including heart disease or heart attacks.
  • Because the defect is in the LDL receptors themselves, this group responds poorly to medications that work by increasing LDL receptors (like statins).

General (non-familial) risk factors: age, family history, tobacco use, hypertension, diabetes, physical inactivity, and a poor diet. Age and family history can't be modified — the goal is addressing what can be controlled to lower overall cardiovascular risk.

Atherosclerosis — How Plaque Forms

Atherosclerosis is a buildup of cholesterol in the vessels. Arteriosclerosis — thickening/hardening of the arterial wall — is often used interchangeably, since the arteries (which carry oxygenated blood to tissues) are the vessels of greatest concern.

The Plaque-Formation Sequence

  • 1. Endothelial injury. The endothelium (the vessel's inner lining) is damaged — by cigarette/tobacco use, chronic hemodynamic wall stress (hypertension), or hyperglycemia (long-term high blood sugar).
  • 2. Increased permeability. The normally tightly-bunched endothelial cells loosen, letting LDL molecules pass under the vessel wall.
  • 3. Inflammation & foam cells. Macrophages arrive and engulf the trapped lipids, becoming foam cells that embed in the vessel wall and appear as fatty streaks.
  • 4. Plaque development. Inflammatory mediators, accumulated lipids, and foam cells form a lipid core that expands into the vessel and can impede blood flow.

There's no simple blood test for atherosclerosis itself — confirming it invasively (fluoroscopy with injectable dye) is highly invasive. As a proxy, clinicians check C-reactive protein (CRP), a nonspecific marker of systemic inflammation released by the liver during the acute inflammatory phase. An elevated CRP suggests increased risk but isn't a guarantee of atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis — Consequences, Blockage Severity & Related Terminology

Large plaques with a large lipid core are prone to rupture. A rupture triggers platelet aggregation and clot (thrombus) formation, which can block the vessel entirely — anything downstream loses oxygen and nutrients. In the heart, that's a myocardial infarction; in the brain, a stroke. Older, more stable plaques have more collagen/fiber forming a protective cap, making them less likely to rupture.

BlockageClinical Picture
30–90% BlockageBlood can still get through; the patient may be asymptomatic, especially if sedentary or if the blockage is in a smaller vessel.
~99% BlockageSevere flow constriction — typically chest pain (angina) with minimal exertion, like getting up and walking to a chair. Needs immediate intervention; this is life-threatening.
Overlapping terminology: coronary heart disease (CHD), coronary artery disease (CAD), ischemic heart disease, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) all describe the same underlying problem — plaque buildup in the coronary arteries limiting oxygen delivery to the heart.

Hyperlipidemia Pharmacology — Statins (HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors)

Before medication, clinicians try 6 months of diet and physical activity modification (unless the patient already has a history of MI, stroke, or other atherosclerosis-related organ damage). If that isn't enough, statins are first-line. Statins block HMG-CoA reductase, so the liver makes less cholesterol — the liver responds by building more LDL receptors, which pull more LDL out of the blood. The cholesterol reduction itself isn't the goal; getting LDL out of circulation is.

Statins reduce fatal and non-fatal atherosclerotic cardiovascular events (heart attacks, strokes), reduce disability after a non-fatal stroke, and are used for both primary prevention (before any event) and secondary prevention (after one, to reduce recurrence/mortality). Benefit groups include: clinical ASCVD (history of MI, angina, coronary revascularization, stroke/TIA, or peripheral arterial disease); primary LDL ≥ 190; adults 40–75 with diabetes and LDL 70–189; and adults 40–75 without ASCVD/diabetes but LDL 70–189 with an elevated 10-year ASCVD risk score. Effects are not permanent — the drug must be taken continuously (or until lifestyle changes bring cholesterol down on their own) to keep working. It typically takes about two weeks to see effect, since the liver needs time to build new LDL receptors.

TermDetail
Drugs (know these)Atorvastatin (the prototype), simvastatin, rosuvastatin.
Typical EffectLDL decreases ~21–63%, HDL increases ~5–22%, triglycerides decrease ~6–43%.
Dosing TimingTake at night — the liver makes more cholesterol at night, so this is when the drug is most effective. Required for simvastatin and rosuvastatin (shorter half-life); atorvastatin has a longer half-life so timing matters less, but night dosing is still typically recommended for consistency.
InteractionsAvoid grapefruit — it interferes with statin metabolism and raises statin levels. Avoid alcohol (liver risk). Avoid other drugs that raise myopathy/rhabdomyolysis risk (azoles, HIV protease inhibitors, macrolides, cyclosporine, niacin).

Adverse reactions: myopathy (muscle weakness — ask patients about this), rhabdomyolysis (muscle fiber breakdown whose byproducts can cause acute kidney failure), and hepatotoxicity (liver damage with a year or more of treatment — monitor AST/ALT). Baseline labs before starting: fasting lipid panel, AST/ALT (liver function), and creatine kinase (CK, a muscle-breakdown marker). Patients with familial hypercholesterolemia (defective LDL receptors) don't respond as well to statins, since the drug's mechanism depends on the liver being able to build more of those receptors.

Hyperlipidemia Pharmacology — Ezetimibe & Secondary Causes

Ezetimibe (Zetia) — Cholesterol Absorption Inhibitor

  • Blocks the small intestine's ability to absorb cholesterol, from both dietary intake and bile.
  • A second-line therapy to statins — sometimes given in combination (a combined statin + ezetimibe pill exists, though it's typically expensive); combination therapy produces a greater LDL decrease than either drug alone.
Secondary CauseDetail
DietEspecially saturated/trans fat intake.
MedicationsDiuretics, glucocorticoids, cyclosporine, and amiodarone can raise cholesterol.
Disease — Bile Duct ObstructionCholesterol is normally secreted into bile; a blocked/obstructed bile duct raises cholesterol levels.
Altered MetabolismHypothyroidism, obesity, and pregnancy can all raise LDL and triglycerides.

Other second-line agents you don't need to know in detail: bile acid sequestrants, niacin (sometimes combined with statins), fibric acid derivatives, and fish oil (available OTC — worth asking patients about). Statins remain the gold standard, alongside diet and exercise modification and smoking cessation.

Blood Pressure Regulation — The RAS System & Other Mechanisms

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAS) is the primary hormonal driver of blood pressure, with two endpoints: sodium/water retention and vasoconstriction. It's a normal, appropriate physiologic response to a drop in blood pressure/volume — but it can also be pathologically overactive.

The RAS Cascade

  • 1. Decreased renal perfusion (low blood volume/pressure) triggers the kidney to release renin.
  • 2. Renin converts angiotensinogen (released by the liver) into angiotensin I.
  • 3. Angiotensin I travels to the lungs, where ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) converts it to angiotensin II — a potent vasoconstrictor.
  • 4. Angiotensin II acts on the adrenal glands to release aldosterone, which causes the kidney (nephron) to retain sodium and water — "salt sucks," water follows salt — raising blood volume and blood pressure.

Chronic stress, hypersensitivity to angiotensin II, or being a high renin-secretor can push this system into an inappropriate, pathologic overactivation. Other blood-pressure mechanisms: arterial baroreceptors (carotid sinus, aorta, left ventricle) sense pressure and adjust heart rate and vaso-tone; vascular autoregulation keeps tissue perfusion consistent by adjusting arteriole resistance/diameter based on the mean arterial pressure (MAP — the number in parentheses on a blood pressure reading).

Hypertension Basics — Classification, Primary & Secondary Causes

Hypertension = high (hyper-) blood pressure (tension). It affects about 1 in 3 people in the US and is often called the "silent killer" because it's usually asymptomatic. The 2019 AHA guidelines lowered treatment thresholds after evidence showed waiting too long led to worse outcomes.

Category (2019 AHA)SystolicDiastolic
NormalBelow 120 andBelow 80
Elevated120–129 andBelow 80
Hypertension Stage 1130–139 or80–89
Hypertension Stage 2Above 140 orAbove 90
Hypertensive CrisisAbove 180 and/orAbove 120

Watch the "and" vs. "or": Normal and Elevated require both numbers to qualify. Stage 1 and Stage 2 only need one number to qualify — e.g., a systolic of 120 (which alone reads as Elevated) paired with a diastolic of 85 makes the patient Stage 1, because the diastolic number alone meets that threshold.

Primary (Essential) Hypertension

  • ~90–95% of cases; no single known cause — usually idiopathic, from a complex mix of genetics and environment affecting the sympathetic nervous system, the RAS system, and natriuretic peptides.
  • Risk factors: smoking, excess sodium, sedentary lifestyle, hyperlipidemia, stress, family history/genetics, obesity, age (over 55 for males, over 60 for females), insulin resistance, high alcohol intake.

Secondary Hypertension

  • Has a known, identifiable cause — renal disease, adrenal cortical or medullary tumors (pheochromocytoma), or drug-induced (oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, antihistamines, cocaine, amphetamines).
  • Treated by treating/removing the underlying cause.

Hypertension — Long-Term Complications & Hypertensive Crisis

Hypertension is usually asymptomatic until there's end-organ damage — this is why it's screened for routinely rather than waiting for symptoms.

SystemLong-Term Damage
HeartLeft ventricular hypertrophy (from the increased workload), increased myocardial oxygen demand, accelerated atherosclerosis, increased risk of aortic aneurysm.
KidneysA primary cause of end-stage renal disease.
BrainHigher risk of stroke, aneurysm, and hemorrhage.
EyesRetinopathy, blindness.
Lower ExtremitiesGangrene, intermittent claudication.
Hypertensive crisis (systolic ≥ 180 and/or diastolic ≥ 120) splits into two severities. Hypertensive urgency: no signs of end-organ damage — treat with oral medication, lowering blood pressure gradually over hours to days. Hypertensive emergency: uncontrolled blood pressure with end-organ damage (headache, blurry vision, heart dysfunction, stroke, brain hemorrhage, chest pain, acute coronary syndrome — even unresponsiveness or seizures). This is treated aggressively with IV medication (commonly labetalol), lowering blood pressure over minutes to hours — the risk of over-correcting is outweighed by the ongoing organ damage.

Antihypertensive Pharmacology — Diuretics

Diuretics lower blood pressure by increasing urinary output, decreasing circulating volume, and decreasing arterial resistance — decreasing that volume decreases cardiac output. They're inexpensive and commonly first-line.

ClassDetail
Thiazide Diuretics
(hydrochlorothiazide/HCTZ)		First-line for mild hypertension. Works on the distal convoluted tubule, inhibiting sodium/potassium/chloride reabsorption and relaxing arterioles (decreasing peripheral vascular resistance). PO only. Side effects: hypokalemia, orthostatic hypotension, may worsen renal insufficiency, hyperuricemia (caution with gout), can raise glucose/cholesterol/triglycerides.
Loop Diuretics
(furosemide/Lasix)		Inhibits sodium reabsorption at the loop of Henle — a much stronger diuretic effect than thiazides. Also used for fluid volume overload unrelated to blood pressure (common in the ICU), and works even with preexisting renal disease. PO or IV. Side effects: hypokalemia plus other electrolyte losses (hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, hypochloremia), dehydration, hypotension, and ototoxicity (usually transient) — give IV doses slowly to reduce that risk.
Potassium-Sparing Diuretics
(spironolactone/Aldactone)		An aldosterone antagonist — blocks aldosterone's action, so sodium/water are excreted but potassium is retained (spared). A mild diuretic on its own, often added to a thiazide/loop regimen to boost effect without worsening hypokalemia. PO only. Side effect: hyperkalemia. Endocrine side effects: deepened voice, impotence, irregular menstrual cycles, gynecomastia, hirsutism.

Nursing considerations across diuretics: monitor potassium levels closely (critical for cardiac rhythm — both hypo- and hyperkalemia can cause dysrhythmias); with thiazides/loops, consider potassium supplementation and potassium-rich foods (avocado, spinach, watermelon, beans, tomatoes, sweet potatoes, and beets all have more potassium per serving than a banana); with loop diuretics, monitor daily weights and teach patients to report low urine output.

Antihypertensive Pharmacology — Sympatholytics

This class works by blocking the sympathetic nervous system, decreasing vasoconstriction and peripheral vascular resistance.

ClassDetail
Beta Blockers
(metoprolol, propranolol, carvedilol)		Suffix "-olol." Decrease heart force, heart rate, and renin secretion; increase nitric oxide (vasodilation). Beta-1 receptors are cardioselective (heart — "1 heart"); beta-2 receptors are in the lungs ("2 lungs"). Metoprolol is selective (beta-1 only); propranolol is non-selective (beta-1 and beta-2 — avoid in asthma/COPD); carvedilol blocks alpha and beta receptors. PO or IV.
Alpha-2 Agonists
(clonidine)		Centrally acting — decreases sympathetic outflow to both alpha and beta receptors, causing bradycardia, decreased cardiac output, and vasodilation. Not first-line (high side-effect profile); given PO or transdermal (patch). Also used for opioid withdrawal and ADHD. Side effect: drowsiness (advise taking at night, especially the first dose).
Alpha-1 Blockers
(doxazosin)		Directly blocks the sympathetic nervous system, decreasing peripheral vascular resistance (venous and arterial dilation). Not first-line. Side effects: hypotension, dizziness. (Tamsulosin/Flomax is also an alpha-1 blocker, but it's used for benign prostatic hyperplasia, not hypertension.)
Never stop beta blockers or clonidine abruptly — doing so can cause rebound hypertension, a critical, dangerous rise in blood pressure with high risk for stroke or death. Wean the dose down gradually instead. Also: always take blood pressure and heart rate immediately before giving a beta blocker (not a reading from hours earlier) — hold the dose and contact the provider if heart rate is below 60 or systolic blood pressure is below 100.

Other notes: beta blockers can mask hypoglycemia (tachycardia is a common hypoglycemia symptom, but a beta blocker keeps heart rate normal) — use cautiously in diabetes. Beta blockers are also used for angina, arrhythmias, heart failure, migraines, and short-term situational anxiety.

Antihypertensive Pharmacology — RAS Blockers, Calcium Channel Blockers & Vasodilators

ClassDetail
ACE Inhibitors
(captopril, lisinopril)		Suffix "-pril." Block angiotensin-converting enzyme, so angiotensin II (a vasoconstrictor) and aldosterone (water retention) are both reduced. First-line for hypertension, also used in heart failure; slows left ventricular hypertrophy progression; renal-protective, often chosen for patients with diabetes. PO only. Not for use in pregnancy. Often paired with a thiazide diuretic.
ARBs
(losartan)		Suffix "-sartan." Block angiotensin II's action at its receptor (rather than blocking its production) — vasodilation, decreased peripheral vascular resistance, increased sodium/water excretion, without affecting heart rate. Well tolerated overall. PO only. Not for use in pregnancy — patients of childbearing age need contraception. Not given together with an ACE inhibitor (either/or) — an ACE is typically tried first; if not tolerated (usually because of the cough), switch to an ARB.
Renin Inhibitor
(aliskiren)		Directly inhibits renin, causing vasodilation, decreased blood volume, decreased sympathetic activation. PO only. Long half-life — takes several weeks for full effect. Not for use in pregnancy; watch for hyperkalemia if combined with an ACE inhibitor.
Calcium Channel Blockers
(nifedipine, amlodipine, nicardipine, verapamil, diltiazem)		Block calcium channels, causing vasodilation of arterial (not venous) smooth muscle. Nifedipine/amlodipine/nicardipine are used mainly for hypertension (nicardipine IV drip for hypertensive crisis); verapamil/diltiazem are used more for cardiac rhythm disorders, though still technically antihypertensives. PO or IV. Side effects: orthostatic hypotension, peripheral edema (sometimes managed by adding a diuretic). Well tolerated in older adults and in African American/Black patients, with lower angioedema risk than ACE inhibitors.
Vasodilator
(hydralazine)		Works directly on arterial and venous smooth muscle, causing relaxation and decreasing systemic/peripheral vascular resistance. PO or IV (IV for emergencies or when PO isn't tolerated). Side effects: hypotension, dizziness, headache, tachycardia, edema, dyspnea, GI upset. Combined with isosorbide dinitrate (as the pill BiDil) for heart failure, especially in African American/Black patients, with strong mortality benefit.
ACE inhibitors carry a risk of angioedema — swelling of the deep skin layers, around the eyes/lips, and critically the larynx, which can close the airway. This is rare overall (0.1–0.7%) but occurs more often in African American/Black patients (~5.5%). It's life-threatening and a reason to avoid future ACE inhibitor use — watch closely for it if starting an ARB in a patient with a prior ACE-related angioedema history.

ACE inhibitor side effects to also expect: first-dose hypotension (a 15–20% BP drop in the first 6–8 hours — advise taking the first dose at home, not before driving or operating machinery), a dry, persistent, non-productive cough (a common reason patients ask to switch drugs), dizziness, rash, and — specific to captopril — neutropenia (monitor WBC count every 2 weeks for the first 3 months, then periodically). Both ACE inhibitors and ARBs carry hyperkalemia risk, especially with potassium supplements.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the difference between HDL, LDL, and VLDL?
HDL is "good" cholesterol (more protein, decreases cardiovascular risk — want it higher). LDL is "bad" cholesterol (more cholesterol, less protein, increases atherosclerosis risk — want it lower). VLDL is mostly triglycerides and isn't measured directly, but high triglycerides suggest high VLDL and increased heart disease risk.
What are the four steps of atherosclerotic plaque formation?
1) Endothelial injury (smoking, hypertension, hyperglycemia). 2) Increased permeability lets LDL under the vessel wall. 3) Macrophages engulf the lipids, becoming foam cells (fatty streaks). 4) Inflammation and lipid accumulation build a plaque with a lipid core, which can rupture.
How do statins work, and what are their key adverse effects?
Statins block HMG-CoA reductase, so the liver makes less cholesterol and responds by building more LDL receptors, pulling LDL out of the blood. Key adverse effects: myopathy, rhabdomyolysis (can cause acute kidney failure), and hepatotoxicity.
What is familial hypercholesterolemia, and why is it hard to treat with statins?
A genetic defect in liver LDL receptors that causes high cholesterol despite diet/exercise/healthy lifestyle. It's hard to treat with statins because statins work by prompting the liver to build more LDL receptors — but this population already has a defect in making them.
Walk through the RAS cascade from trigger to blood pressure increase.
Decreased renal perfusion → kidney releases renin → renin converts angiotensinogen (from the liver) to angiotensin I → ACE (in the lungs) converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II (a vasoconstrictor) → angiotensin II triggers aldosterone release → aldosterone causes sodium/water retention → blood pressure rises.
A patient's blood pressure is 122/86. Why does the "and/or" logic in the AHA categories matter here?
The systolic (122) alone would read as Elevated, but the diastolic (86) meets the Stage 1 threshold (80-89) on its own — Stage 1 only needs ONE number to qualify ("or"), so this patient is Hypertension Stage 1, not Elevated.
What's the difference between primary and secondary hypertension?
Primary (essential) hypertension has no single known cause (~90-95% of cases) — a mix of genetics and environment. Secondary hypertension has a known, identifiable cause (renal disease, adrenal tumors, certain drugs) and is treated by treating that underlying cause.
What's the difference between hypertensive urgency and hypertensive emergency?
Both are BP ≥180/120. Urgency: no end-organ damage — treat with oral medication, lowering BP gradually over hours to days. Emergency: end-organ damage present (headache, vision changes, stroke, chest pain) — treat aggressively with IV medication, lowering BP over minutes to hours.
What's the key side-effect difference between thiazide/loop diuretics and potassium-sparing diuretics?
Thiazide and loop diuretics both cause potassium LOSS (hypokalemia) — a cardiac rhythm risk. Potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone) retain potassium instead, risking hyperkalemia — often added to a thiazide/loop regimen specifically to offset that potassium loss.
Why would a nurse avoid giving propranolol (a non-selective beta blocker) to a patient with asthma or COPD?
Propranolol blocks both beta-1 (heart) and beta-2 (lung) receptors. Blocking beta-2 receptors in the lungs can worsen bronchoconstriction. A cardioselective beta blocker like metoprolol (beta-1 only) is preferred in patients with lung disease.
What's the key difference between ACE inhibitors and ARBs, including their most notable side effects?
ACE inhibitors block the conversion of angiotensin I to II (causing a common dry cough and rare but serious angioedema risk). ARBs block angiotensin II at its receptor instead, avoiding that cough — better tolerated overall, though angioedema risk still exists (just without the racial disparity seen with ACE inhibitors). Never given together.
What's a hallmark side effect of calcium channel blockers, and why does it happen?
Peripheral edema — because calcium channel blockers cause vasodilation primarily in arterioles (not veins), fluid can pool in the periphery. Sometimes managed by adding a diuretic alongside the CCB.
