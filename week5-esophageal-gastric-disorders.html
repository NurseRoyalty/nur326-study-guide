Esophageal & Gastric Disorders

These conditions are grouped together because they're all "upper GI" disorders — involving the esophagus and stomach — and they share overlapping symptoms (heartburn, epigastric pain, dysphagia) and overlapping pharmacologic treatments.

Dysphagia

Dysphagia means difficulty swallowing. It typically affects swallowing solids first, then progresses to also affect liquids.

Mechanical Causes

  • Stenosis
  • Strictures
  • Diverticula
  • Tumors — physically block the esophagus

Neuromuscular Causes

  • Stroke — the esophagus loses proper innervation
  • Achalasia — the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) can't open properly

Patients who have been intubated or have a tracheostomy are also a common population with dysphagia; it can either resolve or progress. LES = lower esophageal sphincter, the sphincter connecting the esophagus to the stomach — this term recurs throughout this page.

GERD (Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease)

In GERD, the LES doesn't close properly, allowing highly acidic stomach contents to reflux backward into the esophagus, causing heartburn. It occurs from things that either weaken LES closure strength or increase abdominal pressure.

Contributing Factors

  • Fatty foods
  • Spicy foods
  • Tomato-based foods
  • Citrus
  • Caffeine
  • Large amounts of alcohol
  • Cigarette smoking
  • Certain sleep positions
  • Obesity
  • Pregnancy
  • Certain pharmacologic agents

If severe or chronic, GERD can also cause dental problems (tooth decay, gingivitis), ear problems, and throat problems (hoarseness, chronic sore throat, throat clearing, laryngitis).

Complications of Chronic GERD

  • Esophageal ulcerations
  • Scarring and strictures within the esophagus
  • Barrett's esophagus — the most severe complication; abnormal, metaplastic (precancerous) cell changes develop in the esophageal lining
Barrett's esophagus carries a threefold risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma, with very low survival if it develops — providers monitor for it in long-term GERD patients. There is no real pharmacologic treatment for existing strictures or Barrett's esophagus itself; management focuses on preventing these complications by treating the underlying GERD and avoiding triggers.

Hiatal Hernia

A hiatal hernia is a defect in the diaphragm that allows part of the stomach to pass upward into the thoracic (chest) cavity.

Sliding Hernia

  • The peritoneum stays intact, which restrains/limits the size of the hernia
  • Smaller, more common, and less severe

Paraesophageal ("Rolling") Hernia

  • Part of the stomach pushes through the diaphragm and stays there permanently, above the diaphragm
  • The peritoneal membrane becomes thin/defective, forming a true sac
  • More severe than a sliding hernia

A patient can also have a mixed type (sliding + paraesophageal together); the most complex/severe forms are classified as type 3 or type 4 hiatal hernia.

Associated Factors

  • Aging — the older you get, the more likely
  • Injury/damage that weakens the diaphragm muscles
  • Repeatedly putting too much pressure on the muscles around the stomach (chronic severe coughing, vomiting, constipation/straining to have a bowel movement)
  • Obesity

The cause is not fully understood, but age, obesity, and smoking are the high-risk factors overall.

Presentation

  • Many patients are asymptomatic.
  • Others have belching, dysphagia, or chest/epigastric pain that manifests just like GERD — because the LES sits above where it's supposed to be (weaker there), or with a paraesophageal hernia part of the stomach permanently sits above the diaphragm.
  • GERD and hiatal hernia commonly coexist in the same patient.

Treatment

  • Mostly conservative: small frequent meals, avoid lying down after eating
  • Avoid things that increase abdominal pressure (tight clothing, abdominal support)
  • Weight control for those who are obese
  • Pharmacologic therapy for GERD/esophagitis symptoms
  • Surgery is sometimes required when conservative treatment isn't well-adhered to or isn't effective

Gastritis

Gastritis is a non-specific inflammatory condition of the stomach — the "-itis" suffix means inflammation, a suffix worth watching for across this week's GI conditions.

Acute Gastritis

  • Temporary inflammation of the stomach lining only (intestines not affected)
  • Generally lasts 2 to 10 days
  • Related to irritating substances, drugs, or infectious agents: alcohol, NSAIDs, H. pylori, stomach viruses

Chronic Gastritis

  • A progressive disorder with chronic inflammation of the stomach lining
  • Can last weeks to years
  • Autoimmune (parietal cells attacked by the immune system) or caused by chronic H. pylori infection
  • Can lead to peptic ulcer disease, bleeding ulcers, anemia, and increased risk of gastric cancer

NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis; prostaglandins normally stimulate protective mucus secretion in the stomach and inhibit gastric acid production, so NSAIDs remove that protection and increase acid.

TermDetail
H. pylori (Helicobacter pylori)A gram-negative spiral bacterium that thrives in an acidic environment. Initially often asymptomatic, but if it overgrows/overtakes normal gut flora it can cause chronic gastritis and peptic ulcer disease, and increases stomach cancer risk. Transmitted person-to-person via saliva, fecal matter, or vomit, or from contaminated food/water.

Symptoms (Acute or Chronic)

  • Vomiting
  • Epigastric/stomach burning
  • Upset stomach
  • Loss of appetite
  • Severe cases: vomiting blood or black stool
  • Classic finding: postprandial discomfort — symptoms appear soon after eating

Acute Gastroenteritis

Acute gastroenteritis involves inflammation of both the stomach and the small intestine — unlike gastritis, which is stomach only.

Causes

  • Stomach viruses (norovirus, rotavirus) — the classic "stomach flu"/food-poisoning picture
  • Bacterial infections (E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter)
  • Parasitic infections

It usually lasts 1 to 3 days, but can last as long as 10 days.

Symptoms

  • Watery diarrhea (possibly bloody if bacterial)
  • Abdominal pain
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Fever
  • Malaise
The biggest concern is fluid volume deficit/dehydration, since the patient is losing fluid from both ends.

Acute gastroenteritis is usually managed supportively — antibiotics/antivirals aren't typically needed, and antivirals wouldn't work anyway. If symptoms last more than 48–72 hours, the patient should see a provider to prevent dehydration, and antibiotics may be needed if a bacterial cause is suspected.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

PUD is a disorder involving ulceration of the upper GI tract: it can occur in the esophagus (esophageal ulcer), the stomach (gastric ulcer), or the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine (a duodenal ulcer, technically a type of peptic ulcer).

Aggressive Factors

  • H. pylori infection
  • NSAID use
  • Excess gastric acid/pepsin secretion
  • Smoking

Protective/Defensive Factors

  • Mucus secretion
  • Bicarbonate secretion
  • Adequate blood flow
  • Prostaglandins

PUD develops when aggressive factors overwhelm the body's defenses.

Risk Factors

  • H. pylori infection — a major risk factor
  • Frequent/chronic NSAID or aspirin use
  • Heavy alcohol use
  • Smoking
  • Family history

Stress does NOT directly cause peptic ulcers — this outdated belief has been re-thought. However, stress can definitely worsen an existing ulcer, because the stress response (HPA axis / sympathetic nervous system activation) increases gastric acid secretion.

Pathophysiology

  • Mucosal damage (from acid or NSAIDs) triggers an immune response
  • Histamine is secreted, which increases acid and pepsin secretion, causing further damage
  • This causes vasodilation (bringing more white blood cells and blood flow to promote healing), which also causes edema
  • If blood vessels near the ulcer are destroyed by the acid/pepsin damage, this causes bleeding ulcers
TermDetail
Duodenal ulcerThe most common location for peptic ulcers overall. Can occur at any age but often in earlier adulthood.
Gastric ulcerTends to occur later in life (around age 50–70), more associated with chronic risk factors — long-term NSAID use, chronic corticosteroid use, anticoagulant use, or other serious systemic illness.

Gastric Ulcer Pain

  • Burning, cramping, or gas-like pain in the epigastrium or back
  • Occurs 1–2 hours after eating, as the stomach empties and the ulcer becomes more irritated

Duodenal Ulcer Pain

  • Burning, cramping, or gas-like pain in the epigastrium or back
  • Occurs 2–4 hours after eating, once food has passed through to that area
MNEMONIC — HOP (Complications of PUD): Hemorrhage (the stomach and intestines are highly vascularized, so bleeding can be severe), Obstruction (ulcer scar tissue and strictures can bond/connect areas and obstruct the intestines), Perforation (a severe ulcer can erode through all the layers of the stomach/intestinal wall, which can cause peritonitis).

Pharmacology for GERD & Peptic Ulcer Disease

Treatment works by either decreasing aggressive factors or increasing protective factors.

Decrease Aggressive Factors

  • H. pylori treatment (antibiotics + gastric acid inhibitor)
  • H2 receptor blockers
  • Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)

Increase Protective Factors

  • Sucralfate
  • Antacids
TermDetail
H. pylori treatmentCombination antibiotic therapy plus a gastric acid inhibitor, given over about 10–14 days. A confirmed H. pylori test (a breath test) is always done before treating, to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use/resistance. This regimen can cost around $200 with up to 12 pills a day, so patient adherence is often poor.
H2 receptor blockers (H2 antagonists)Block histamine (H2) receptors in the stomach's parietal cells, reducing gastric acid secretion by about 60–70% and increasing stomach pH. Key drugs: famotidine (most commonly used now) and cimetidine (an older agent — ranitidine has been pulled from the market). Can be given PO or IV; give at least 1 hour apart from antacids. Used for GERD, PUD, and also given preventively as ulcer prophylaxis in hospitalized/critically ill patients to reduce aspiration pneumonia risk.
Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)The "-prazole" drugs: omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole. Bind to and irreversibly inhibit the H+/K+ ATPase enzyme (the "proton pump"), blocking gastric acid secretion. Work better than H2 blockers. Available OTC or by prescription; used for short-term treatment of PUD and GERD.
SucralfateA mucosal protectant — sucrose-based with aluminum hydroxide; forms a sticky, thick protective gel/barrier in the stomach. FDA-approved for duodenal ulcers, and also helps gastric ulcers and chronic gastritis. Given PO only (tablet or suspension), taken before meals. Minimal side effects, though it may cause constipation.
AntacidsNeutralize stomach acid by about 50%. Used for PUD and GERD symptoms, stress ulcers, heartburn, and indigestion (though some patients get no relief). There is often an intense rebound effect — heartburn returns rapidly and severely after stopping antacids.

H2 Blockers — Safety Notes

  • Generally very well tolerated
  • Can cause CNS/alertness changes in elderly patients
  • Give IV slowly — can cause bradycardia
  • Cimetidine is a CYP450 inhibitor with significant drug interactions (warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline)
  • Famotidine has much less of this interaction risk, which is why famotidine is generally preferred over cimetidine

PPIs — Long-Term Use Considerations

  • Short-term use is relatively safe
  • Long-term use (years) has been linked to increased risk of pneumonia, bone loss/hip fractures, and stomach cancer
  • For patients with real PUD or Barrett's esophagus risk, providers often judge the benefits to outweigh these risks and keep patients on PPIs long-term with monitoring

Sucralfate can decrease absorption of other drugs, so other medications should be taken separately, about 2 hours apart. Antacids also cause chelation with other drugs (binding/interfering with their absorption), so they should be separated from other medications by 1–2 hours.

What are the two main causes of dysphagia?
Mechanical (a structural blockage — stenosis, strictures, diverticula, or tumors) and neuromuscular (stroke, or achalasia).
What is achalasia?
A condition where the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) can't open properly.
How do proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) reduce stomach acid?
They bind to and irreversibly inhibit the H+/K+ ATPase enzyme (the "proton pump"), blocking gastric acid secretion.
Why is Barrett's esophagus considered dangerous?
It involves abnormal, precancerous (metaplastic) cell changes in the esophageal lining and carries a threefold risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma, with very low survival if it develops.
What distinguishes a sliding hiatal hernia from a paraesophageal hernia?
In a sliding hernia the peritoneum stays intact, limiting hernia size (smaller, more common, less severe). In a paraesophageal hernia, part of the stomach stays permanently above the diaphragm and the peritoneum forms a true, thin/defective sac (more severe).
Why should other medications be taken about 2 hours apart from sucralfate?
Sucralfate can decrease the absorption of other drugs.
How does acute gastroenteritis differ from gastritis?
Gastroenteritis involves inflammation of both the stomach and the small intestine; gastritis involves the stomach lining only.
Does stress directly cause peptic ulcers?
No — that's an outdated belief. Stress can, however, worsen an existing ulcer by increasing gastric acid secretion through the stress response.
About how many hours after eating does gastric ulcer pain typically occur?
1–2 hours after eating, as the stomach empties and the ulcer becomes more irritated.
About how many hours after eating does duodenal ulcer pain typically occur?
2–4 hours after eating, once food has passed through to that area.
What does the mnemonic HOP stand for in PUD complications?
Hemorrhage, Obstruction, Perforation.
Why is famotidine generally preferred over cimetidine?
Cimetidine is a CYP450 inhibitor with significant drug interactions (warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline); famotidine carries much less of this interaction risk.
What is happening mechanically in GERD?
The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) doesn't close properly, allowing highly acidic stomach contents to reflux backward into the esophagus — from things that weaken LES closure strength or increase abdominal pressure.
Define pyrosis and dyspepsia.
Pyrosis is the medical term for heartburn (a burning sensation); dyspepsia means indigestion.
Why do NSAIDs increase the risk of gastritis and peptic ulcers?
NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis; prostaglandins normally stimulate protective mucus secretion in the stomach and inhibit gastric acid production, so blocking them removes that protection and increases acid.
What kind of organism is H. pylori, and how is it transmitted?
A gram-negative spiral bacterium that thrives in an acidic environment; transmitted person-to-person via saliva, fecal matter, or vomit, or from contaminated food/water.
What are the four protective/defensive factors that guard against peptic ulcer disease?
Mucus secretion, bicarbonate secretion, adequate blood flow, and prostaglandins.
What happens when a patient stops taking antacids?
There is often an intense rebound effect — heartburn returns rapidly and severely.
What is the main side effect difference between aluminum-based and magnesium-based antacids?
Aluminum-based (and calcium-based) antacids tend to cause constipation; magnesium-based antacids tend to cause diarrhea. Combined aluminum + magnesium products are more balanced.
What is the biggest concern with acute gastroenteritis, and why?
Fluid volume deficit/dehydration — the patient is losing fluid from both vomiting and diarrhea at once.
