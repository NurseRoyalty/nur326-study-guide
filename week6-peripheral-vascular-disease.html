Peripheral Vascular Disease

Peripheral vascular disease (PVD) is an umbrella term covering two conditions: peripheral artery disease (PAD) and peripheral venous disease (chronic venous insufficiency). They share some risk factors and general treatment principles, but the underlying problem — and the physical findings — point in opposite directions, which is what most of this page is built around.

Cross-reference: atherosclerosis in the lower extremities is the same underlying disease process as coronary artery disease, just occurring in a different vascular bed — see Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure

Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD) — Risk Factors

Risk Factors

  • Smoking — the single biggest risk factor
  • Uncontrolled diabetes
  • High cholesterol
  • Existing heart disease or history of stroke
  • Age over 50
  • Affects men and women roughly equally
Smoking is the single biggest risk factor for PAD: more than 80% of PAD patients are current or former smokers, and smoking doubles the risk of amputation.

Other Causes of Arterial Occlusion

Besides atherosclerosis itself, a few other mechanisms can occlude an artery.

CauseWhat It IsWhy It Matters
Thrombus formationA blood clot forms within the artery and blocks flow.Direct mechanical blockage of arterial flow.
Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease)An autoimmune condition strongly associated with smokers.Causes permanent arterial occlusion.
Vasospasm (Raynaud's phenomenon)Sudden constriction/spasm of small arteries.Associated with scleroderma and pulmonary hypertension, with a genetic predisposition component.

Clinical Manifestations of PAD

The hallmark symptom is pain, typically in the calf or buttock, classically described as intermittent claudication — exertional cramping pain that is relieved by rest. It's sometimes called "angina of the lower extremity" because the mechanism (inadequate blood flow to working muscle) parallels cardiac angina. The femoral artery is the most common site involved.

Other Manifestations

  • Non-healing wounds
  • Diminished sensation
  • Trophic skin changes — skin becomes shiny and thick, with hair loss on the affected limb
  • Diminished or absent pulses
  • Pallor when the limb is elevated
  • Cyanosis
  • Dependent rubor — a deep red color when the limb is hanging down
  • Erectile dysfunction — can occur when pelvic/lower-extremity arterial flow is affected
MNEMONIC — The 5 P's (significant arterial compromise): Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesias, and Paralysis (weakness).

Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI)

ABI is the diagnostic test used for PAD: it compares blood pressure at the ankle to blood pressure at the arm.

ABI ValueInterpretation
~1.0–1.4Normal ratio.
0.5 or belowSevere arterial disease.

Arterial vs. Venous Disease

PAD (arterial) and chronic venous insufficiency (venous) are contrasted repeatedly by the same features — pain quality, skin changes, and where ulcers form.

PAD (Arterial)

  • Intermittent claudication — exertional cramping, relieved by rest
  • Pallor with limb elevation; dependent rubor when limb hangs down
  • Diminished or absent pulses
  • Skin: shiny, thick, with hair loss (trophic changes)
  • Ulcers typically form at the toes or the top (dorsum) of the foot

Chronic Venous Insufficiency (Venous)

  • Aching and tiredness in the legs — NOT burning or heaviness
  • Edema
  • Skin: leathery, dark/tan discoloration, flaking
  • Varicose veins commonly associated
  • Stasis ulcers typically form at the ankles

Chronic Venous Insufficiency

Caused by dysfunction of the venous walls and valves, leading to venous stasis — blood pooling in the legs instead of returning efficiently to the heart. It's described as affecting roughly 40% of the U.S. population.

Symptoms

  • Edema
  • Aching and tiredness in the legs
  • Leathery, dark/tan skin discoloration
  • Stasis ulcers — typically form at the ankles
  • Flaking skin

Varicose veins are noted as a precursor to, or commonly associated finding with, chronic venous insufficiency.

Treatment — Shared Between PAD and PVD

General Treatment

  • Smoking cessation — emphasized repeatedly as the single most important intervention
  • Increased physical activity — encourages the body to build collateral circulation around blocked vessels
  • Weight loss
  • Stress reduction
  • Controlling diabetes and hypertension

For severe arterial occlusion, procedural options include bypass surgery, balloon angioplasty, or stent placement.

Pharmacology for Intermittent Claudication

DrugDetail
Cilostazol (Pletal)Works as both a platelet inhibitor and a vasodilator. Has CYP450 drug interactions. Side effects: headache, dizziness, diarrhea, palpitations, and peripheral edema.
PentoxifyllineWorks by increasing blood flow and oxygen delivery to the affected tissue. Side effects: nausea, vomiting, and dizziness. Dosed TID (three times a day).

Most patients with PAD are also placed on a statin plus an antiplatelet or anticoagulant agent — examples given: aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin, and apixaban.
