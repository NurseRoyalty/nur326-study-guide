Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome

NUR 325 review, covering: glucose regulation and the insulin-deficiency cascade, diagnosing and classifying diabetes, acute and chronic complications, insulin pharmacology and administration, oral/injectable antidiabetic agents, obesity, and metabolic syndrome.

Glucose Regulation Basics — Carbs, the Liver & the Pancreas

Carbohydrates are broken down into glucose, raising blood glucose; insulin is secreted in response and lowers it back to baseline. The liver regulates glucose storage and release: glycogenesis converts excess blood glucose into stored glycogen, and glycogenolysis breaks glycogen back down into glucose when blood sugar is low.

The pancreas is both an exocrine gland (secretes digestive enzymes — amylase, lipase — through the pancreatic duct, not the bloodstream) and an endocrine gland (secretes hormones directly into the blood). The islets of Langerhans are the pancreas's endocrine tissue — only 1–2% of the pancreas by mass, but they receive about 20% of its blood flow.

Islet CellHormone & Effect
Alpha CellsSecrete glucagon in response to low blood sugar — triggers glycogenolysis, raising blood glucose.
Beta CellsSecrete insulin (and amylin) — lowers blood glucose by promoting cellular glucose uptake in skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and adipose tissue, and promotes glucose storage as glycogen in the liver. Also inhibits fat breakdown in adipose tissue.

Glucose counter-regulation is the body's defense against hypoglycemia — a negative feedback loop where insulin (lowers glucose) works against a group of counter-regulatory hormones that raise it: glucagon, epinephrine, glucocorticoids, and growth hormone. Together they balance glucose release during eating, glucose release during fasting, and ongoing storage/release.

Insulin Deficiency — The Self-Feeding Cascade

When there isn't enough insulin, glucose can't get into cells even though blood glucose is high (hyperglycemia) — cells are starving despite the excess sugar circulating around them. The body compensates with two other processes, both of which cause their own problems.

Lipolysis (Fat Breakdown)

  • Fat is broken down for energy instead of glucose, releasing free fatty acids.
  • Unused free fatty acids convert to cholesterol/phospholipids (contributing to atherosclerosis) or are broken down to acetyl CoA, which the liver turns into acetoacetic acid → ketone bodies.
  • Rising ketones cause ketosis, which can progress to severe metabolic acidosis and coma.

Proteolysis (Protein Breakdown)

  • Protein synthesis stops and protein catabolism increases, releasing amino acids the cells use for energy.
  • Results in muscle wasting, multiple organ dysfunction, amino acidemia, and an increased BUN.
  • More common in type 1 diabetes (complete insulin deficiency) than type 2.

Osmotic diuresis & dehydration: excess blood glucose raises serum osmolality, pulling water out of cells and into the bloodstream (intracellular dehydration). Once blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold (~200 mg/dL), the kidneys can't reabsorb it all, causing glycosuria — glucose spills into urine and pulls water and electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, phosphorus) with it. This further raises serum osmolality (more cellular dehydration) and lowers the glomerular filtration rate (more glucose accumulates in the blood) — a self-feeding, life-threatening cycle of hyperglycemia, cellular dehydration, electrolyte depletion, and metabolic crisis. Glycosuria and ketones can both be detected on a urine dipstick.

Diabetes — Symptoms & Diagnostic Criteria

Diabetes is characterized by hyperglycemia from an absolute or relative insulin deficiency. Clinically, this presents as the classic three P's: polyuria (excessive urination, from osmotic diuresis), polydipsia (excessive thirst, from dehydration/increased serum osmolality), and polyphagia (increased hunger, from fat/protein breakdown and cellular starvation).

A1c reflects average blood glucose over the last 2–3 months (the percentage of hemoglobin with glucose attached) — a better long-term picture than a single fasting glucose value. The three main types of diabetes are type 1, type 2, and gestational (during pregnancy); other rare forms exist but aren't covered here.

Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes

Type 1 Diabetes

  • Most common pediatric chronic disease, usually diagnosed around age 12; adult-onset type 1 exists but is often misdiagnosed as type 2.
  • Usually autoimmune (T cells destroy the beta cells that make insulin), sometimes idiopathic; genetic predisposition + environmental triggers; slowly progressive.
  • Beta cells cannot regenerate — once destroyed, they're gone. About 80–90% of beta cells must be destroyed before insulin secretion noticeably declines.
  • Complete lack of endogenous insulin. ~5–10% of U.S. diabetes cases. Previously called insulin-dependent or juvenile-onset diabetes.
  • Symptoms: the three P's, plus weight loss/fatigue more than in type 2.

Type 2 Diabetes

  • Attributed to genetic and environmental factors; strongly associated with long-term obesity.
  • Primarily insulin resistance (cells/insulin receptors lose sensitivity, so glucose can't enter despite insulin being present), with possible decreased insulin secretion as beta cells become exhausted over time.
  • Risk factors: age, obesity, hypertension, poor diet, physical inactivity, family history.
  • Symptoms are vague/nonspecific — recurrent infections, visual changes, prolonged wound healing, fatigue. The three P's are less common than in type 1.
  • ADA recommends screening all adults ≥ 35 regardless of risk factors (earlier if risk factors present).

In type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance triggers compensatory hyperinsulinemia (the body makes more insulin to try to overcome the resistance) — those high insulin levels stimulate lipogenesis (fat creation) and inhibit fat breakdown. The liver, sensing starving cells, also increases hepatic glucose production — but because of the resistance, cells still can't use that glucose effectively.

Acute Complications — DKA, HHNS & Hypoglycemia

ComplicationDetail
Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)Most common acute complication in type 1 diabetes. Severe hyperglycemia (blood sugar in the 400–600s), acidosis from ketone bodies, ketonuria, and possible coma. Can be fatal — treated as an emergency.
Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic Syndrome (HHNS)Primarily in type 2 diabetes. Extremely high hyperglycemia (blood sugar in the 600–800s) causing severe osmolality-driven fluid shifts. Results from a less profound insulin deficiency than DKA, but still causes major fluid derangement.
HypoglycemiaOccurs in both types — typically blood sugar below ~55–60 mg/dL, usually from too much insulin/medication relative to food intake. A patient chronically running high (e.g., ~250 mg/dL) can feel symptomatic at a much higher glucose, like 120 mg/dL, since their body is adapted to a higher baseline.
Hypoglycemia is a true emergency. Signs: cold sweats, pallor, tachycardia, hunger, restlessness, anxiety — and if untreated, it can progress to seizures or coma. If a patient shows these symptoms, check the blood sugar immediately — it's easy to confirm and easy to treat.

Chronic Complications — Microvascular Disease

Chronic complications result from poorly controlled diabetes — mainly chronic hyperglycemia, which produces advanced glycation end products, free fatty acids, ketone bodies, and abnormal amino acid pathways that damage tissue. Microvascular damage affects small capillaries (eyes, kidneys, nerves), causing hypoxemia and ischemia in surrounding tissue — hypothesized to result from capillary membrane thickening.

ComplicationDetail
Diabetic NeuropathyMost common microvascular complication. Progressive peripheral nervous system disorder — ischemia, inflammation, and demyelination cause delayed nerve conduction, sensory/motor loss (pain, temperature, vibration sensation), and muscle weakness, especially in feet/legs. Decreased sensation + impaired wound healing + vascular disease can lead to unnoticed injury, infection, and amputation.
Diabetic RetinopathyLeading cause of blindness worldwide; common in both types. Risk rises with duration of diabetes and poor glucose control, and with comorbidities like hypertension/dyslipidemia. Damaged retinal vessels → hemorrhage and RBC aggregation from relative hypoxemia. Also raises risk of cataracts and glaucoma. Intensive glucose control delays onset; regular eye screening is a key teaching point.
Diabetic NephropathyDiabetes is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease/end-stage renal disease worldwide. Hyperglycemia injures the glomeruli, progressively thickening the glomerular basement membrane and allowing protein to leak into urine (microalbuminuria) — the first sign of diabetic kidney dysfunction. As it progresses: hypoproteinemia, fluid overload, generalized edema, and hypertension. Early diagnosis, BP control, and glucose control all help prevent progression.

Chronic Complications — Macrovascular Disease & Infection Risk

Macrovascular disease involves lesions in large/medium arteries — common in type 2 diabetes (but possible in type 1). Thought to result from oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, altered mineral metabolism, and increased cytokine production. About one-third of people with type 2 diabetes develop a macrovascular complication: coronary artery disease, heart attack, stroke, heart failure, or peripheral artery disease — all raising morbidity/mortality.

Diabetes and infection are a dangerous combination — people with diabetes are more prone to cystitis, enteric infections, skin/soft tissue infections, appendicitis, pneumonia, and ear infections, due to immune dysfunction, decreased blood supply, increased nutrient availability for pathogens, and impaired sensation. Infections in diabetic patients can also become serious more quickly, making screening and patient education important.

Insulin Pharmacology — Types, Onset, Peak & Duration

The overall goal of diabetes pharmacology is to prevent hyperglycemia and reduce long-term microvascular/macrovascular complications, by maintaining tight glycemic control (target blood glucose ~80–140 mg/dL, A1c < 6.5%) and healthy lipid levels. Exogenous insulin replaces the endogenous hormone the body isn't making (or isn't making enough of). All type 1 diabetics require insulin; some type 2 diabetics do too, while others are managed on oral agents alone.

Insulin Administration, Regimens & Technology

Insulin is dosed in units, not milligrams or milliliters, and must be drawn up with an insulin-specific syringe — mixing up a standard syringe with an insulin syringe is a known, serious medication error (1 mL of U-100 insulin = 100 units). Most insulin is given subcutaneously (back of the arm, abdomen, thighs — anywhere with adequate fat), with regular insulin as the exception (may also be IV/IM).

Before giving any insulin, get a current blood sugar — not one from an hour ago or earlier in the day, but immediately before the dose (finger-stick/FSBG). This is one of the most important safety rules in diabetes nursing care.
Regimen / DeviceDetail
Sliding ScaleRegular insulin given with meals and at bedtime (charted as AC and HS) — dose is based on the blood glucose reading at that moment, per a provider-defined scale (e.g., 2 units for BG 150–200, 8 units for BG 303). Often combined with a basal dose of longer-acting insulin.
Insulin PumpsComputerized devices delivering a continuous basal infusion of regular insulin, plus patient-triggered bolus doses calculated from carbohydrate intake. Mostly used in type 1, but also in type 2. Many still require a finger-stick before bolusing.
Continuous Glucose Monitors (CGM)Sensor-based, often paired to a phone, giving real-time glucose readings without repeated finger-sticks.

Certified diabetes educators are a valuable resource for teaching patients — especially children — about insulin regimens like sliding scale.

Insulin Complications — Hypoglycemia, Somogyi Effect & Dawn Phenomenon

Signs of hypoglycemia from too much insulin: tachycardia, sweating, nervousness, headache, drowsiness, and — if severe — decreased level of consciousness. Causes beyond a straightforward insulin overdose: skipped/reduced meals, GI illness (vomiting/diarrhea), excess alcohol (raises blood sugar first, then drops it as it's metabolized), sudden new exercise, and childbirth/postpartum. Treatment: if the patient is awake and able to swallow, give oral carbohydrates (15–20 g simple carbohydrate — e.g., 4–6 oz fruit juice, glucose tablets — not candy); if not, give parenteral glucagon (IM/subcutaneous) or, in the hospital, IV dextrose.

Somogyi Effect

  • Rebound hyperglycemia in the morning caused by excessive insulin at bedtime, which triggers nocturnal hypoglycemia.
  • The body senses the low blood sugar and releases stored glucose (counter-regulatory response) — overshooting into hyperglycemia. Severe cases can trigger ketosis.
  • Usually a sign of poor diabetes management (too much basal/bedtime insulin) — needs patient education and dose adjustment.

Dawn Phenomenon

  • Hyperglycemia in the early morning from the body's natural release of cortisol and growth hormone.
  • Cortisol raises blood sugar — a normal physiologic pattern, not an insulin-dosing error.
  • Usually less extreme than the Somogyi effect.

Other insulin complications: rare allergic reactions (less common now that insulin is chemically synthesized), and lipodystrophy/lipoatrophy — skin depression, hardening, or discoloration at injection sites from repeated use of the same spot. Patient teaching: monitor glucose as prescribed, carry a simple-carbohydrate source at all times, rotate injection sites (avoid red/irritated areas), and know that insulin vials are good for about 28–30 days at room temperature before needing refrigeration.

Oral & Injectable Antidiabetic Agents

ClassMechanism & Notes
Sulfonylureas
(glipizide, glyburide)		Suffix "-ide." First-line oral agent. Closes potassium-ATP channels in beta cells, stimulating insulin secretion (requires some remaining beta cell function — type 2 only), increases insulin sensitivity, and reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis. Side effect: hypoglycemia. Interacts with alcohol, NSAIDs, cimetidine (Tagamet), and sulfa antibiotics — all potentiate hypoglycemia risk. Avoid in pregnancy.
Biguanides
(metformin)		First-line for type 2 diabetes (FDA-approved 1994). Stops hepatic conversion of glycogen to glucose and enhances glucose uptake/use by muscle. Does not stimulate insulin release, so it doesn't typically cause hypoglycemia on its own. Slow onset (peaks 2–4 weeks). Side effects: GI upset (bloating, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) — give 30 minutes before meals to help absorption and reduce GI discomfort. Renally cleared — avoid with kidney disease (acidosis risk with elevated creatinine) or elevated ALT; avoid with heart failure or heavy alcohol use. Also used for PCOS.
DPP-4 Inhibitors ("Gliptins")
(linagliptin, saxagliptin, sitagliptin)		Suffix "-gliptin." Inhibits DPP-4, the enzyme that breaks down incretin hormones (GLP-1, gastric inhibitory peptide) — letting them last longer, which increases insulin synthesis, reduces glucagon release, decreases hepatic glucose production, slows digestion, and decreases appetite. Side effects: UTI, headache, GI upset, flu-like symptoms, increased pancreatitis risk. Low hypoglycemia risk. Used with lifestyle changes, alone or combined with metformin.
GLP-1 Receptor Agonists
(dulaglutide, exenatide, semaglutide)		Suffix "-tide." Act on the incretin (GLP-1) receptor: stimulate insulin secretion, reduce postprandial glucagon, slow gastric emptying, increase satiety. Usually adjunct therapy with metformin/other agents; mostly subcutaneous (some oral forms exist). Side effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, injection site reactions, upper respiratory infection, weight loss, headache. Avoid with a history of pancreatitis. Black box warning: risk of thyroid C-cell tumors — contraindicated with a history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2. Not recommended with chronic kidney disease.
SGLT2 Inhibitors
(dapagliflozin)		Suffix "-flozin." Blocks the kidney's SGLT2 transporters, which normally reabsorb ~90% of filtered glucose — blocked glucose is excreted in urine instead, lowering blood glucose. Oral, type 2 only. Side effects: increased UTI and fungal/yeast infection risk (more sugar in urine feeds pathogens; more common in females due to shorter urethral length), hypotension, fainting, dizziness, fatigue. Avoid with end-stage/severe renal disease (for diabetes management — the drug can't work without adequate kidney function). Some agents also carry cardioprotective and renal-protective benefits, and are used adjunctively in early/moderate CKD and heart failure even outside diabetes management.
GlucagonThe hypoglycemia antidote. Oral tablet preferred for an awake, swallowing patient; IM/IV/subcutaneous for decreased level of consciousness. Activates liver glucagon receptors, triggering glycogenolysis. Short half-life (25–35 min) — may need repeat dosing. Check blood sugar before giving and again ~15 minutes after to confirm effect.
GLP-1 = Gila monster: GLP-1 receptor agonists (the "-tide" drugs) were derived from a peptide in Gila monster saliva — after clinicians noticed people bitten by the venomous lizard became hypoglycemic. Gila starts with G, just like GLP-1.

Other considerations: steroids raise blood sugar — a diabetic patient started on steroids (e.g., for a sinus infection) may need their insulin regimen increased. Illness raises blood sugar (stress response) and can push a patient toward DKA/HHNS if not managed — check blood sugar more often when sick, keep taking oral antidiabetic medication if possible even with GI upset, and adjust insulin as needed. New/unplanned exercise can increase hypoglycemia risk. Meal planning approaches include the healthy plate method and carb counting (often paired with insulin pump bolus dosing).

Obesity — Adipose Tissue, Distribution & Measurement

About 74% of U.S. adults ≥ 20 are overweight or obese (42% obese, 10% severely obese) — a costly problem (over $147 billion in obesity-related medical costs in 2008) associated with increased risk across most major disease categories. Adipose tissue is normal and needed — it provides insulation, mechanical support, energy reserve, and (via adipokines) contributes to immune function. Adipocytes are fat-storing cells that increase in number and hypertrophy (grow larger) with excess fat storage.

Fat LocationDetail
Subcutaneous (Peripheral)Just under the skin — the "pinchable" fat. Considered the healthier storage type; less inflammatory.
VisceralStored between abdominal organs (e.g., the omentum). More hormonally active than subcutaneous fat — associated with impaired lipid/glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, increased cancer risk, and cardiovascular disease.

Android obesity ("apple" shape, more common in males) concentrates fat in the abdomen — more likely visceral fat, carrying higher risk of coronary artery disease, hypertension, stroke, and diabetes. Gynoid obesity ("pear" shape, more common in females) stores fat elsewhere on the body, without that same elevated cardiometabolic risk. Waist circumference is used as a cardiovascular risk marker: > 35 in for women, > 40 in for men.

MeasureCalculation / Threshold
BMI (adults)Weight (kg) ÷ height² (m), or weight (lb) ÷ height² (in) × 703. Obesity = BMI > 30 kg/m².
BMI (children)Obesity = BMI ≥ the age/sex-specific 95th percentile on growth charts.
Body Fat %A more precise gauge than BMI alone (e.g., accounts for a muscular person with a high BMI but low body fat). Ideal: women ≤ 30%, men ≤ 25%.

Obesity is the fifth leading cause of death in the U.S. and a risk factor for type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, hypertension, GERD, arthritis, infection, and sleep apnea. It's polygenic (multiple genes involved — e.g., leptin gene mutations) and multifactorial: genetics, environment (including "obesogens" — endocrine-disrupting environmental chemicals), secondary conditions (Cushing syndrome, PCOS, hypothyroidism), excess caloric intake, sedentary lifestyle, age, socioeconomic status, cultural eating patterns, and smoking cessation (associated with weight gain) all interact.

Obesity — Adipokines, GI Hormones & Pharmacology

Adipose tissue functions as an endocrine organ, secreting adipokines — cell-signaling proteins regulating appetite, energy expenditure, lipid storage, insulin secretion/sensitivity, and coagulation.

SignalDetail
AdiponectinA "good" adipokine — inversely related to body fat (decreases as adiposity increases, especially visceral fat; resistance can also develop). Increases energy expenditure, improves insulin sensitivity, has anti-inflammatory and anti-atherosclerotic effects. Low levels → insulin resistance, inflammation, hyperlipidemia, and higher risk of type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease.
LeptinA "good" adipokine made by adipocytes — rises with fat mass (positive relationship). Signals satiety and increases energy expenditure after eating; low levels (fasting) stimulate intake and reduce expenditure. With obesity, chronically high leptin leads to leptin resistance — losing its appetite-suppressing effect and promoting overeating, weight gain, hyperglycemia/hyperinsulinemia, hyperlipidemia, and inflammation.
Ghrelin, GLP-1 & Peptide YYGI-derived hormones that normally promote satiety/fullness (ghrelin also rises with fasting). All three are decreased in obesity, contributing to reduced satiety signaling.
Cholecystokinin (CCK)Stimulates gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme release; increases satiety, reduces food intake, slows gastric emptying. Research on its level in obesity is mixed (traditionally decreased, some newer studies show increased).

Obesity also causes a chronic low-grade inflammatory state — immune cells infiltrate adipose tissue and release cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-6) that circulate systemically, contributing to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome.

Orlistat — Pharmacologic Treatment for Obesity

  • Over-the-counter. Binds/blocks gastric and pancreatic enzymes, reducing dietary fat absorption by up to 30%.
  • Black box warning: liver injury — avoid in patients with existing liver disease.
  • Side effects (from blocked fat absorption): oily spotting, flatulence, fecal incontinence — advise reducing fat intake to < 30% of daily calories to help. Also decreases fat-soluble vitamin absorption (A, D, E, beta-carotene) — patients need a multivitamin.
  • Takes about 3 months to see effect; minimally effective without diet/exercise; significant weight rebound after stopping — it's a long-term treatment, not a short course.
  • Drug therapy is generally reserved for BMI > 30, or > 27 with risk factors (after ~6 months of unsuccessful diet/exercise alone). Other FDA-approved obesity medications exist (phentermine, liraglutide, naltrexone/bupropion) but aren't detailed here. Bariatric surgery (gastric bypass, gastric banding, liposuction) is another option, weighed by individual risk/benefit.

Metabolic Syndrome

Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of risk factors that together substantially raise the risk of stroke and cardiovascular disease. A patient is considered to have metabolic syndrome with three or more of: elevated waist circumference (> 40 in men / > 35 in women), elevated triglycerides, low HDL, hypertension, and elevated fasting glucose.

Management treats each component individually (antihypertensives for high blood pressure, lipid-lowering agents for high triglycerides, antidiabetic therapy for high glucose) alongside counseling on weight loss, exercise, healthy diet, and smoking cessation. Social determinants of health — poverty, caregiving demands, food access — make these lifestyle changes genuinely harder for many patients, and should be considered rather than assumed to be simple choices. Small, incremental, sustainable changes are more effective long-term than large sweeping ones.

Walk through what happens in the body with insulin deficiency — the three main downstream processes.
1) Hyperglycemia (glucose can't enter cells). 2) Lipolysis — fat breakdown → free fatty acids → ketone bodies → ketosis → possible metabolic acidosis/coma. 3) Proteolysis — protein breakdown → amino acids used for energy → muscle wasting. Also: osmotic diuresis from the hyperglycemia pulls water/electrolytes into urine, worsening dehydration and further raising serum osmolality — a self-feeding cycle.
What are the three P's of hyperglycemia, and what causes each?
Polyuria (excessive urination, from osmotic diuresis), polydipsia (excessive thirst, from dehydration/increased osmolality), and polyphagia (increased hunger, from fat/protein breakdown and cellular starvation).
What's the core pathophysiologic difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes?
Type 1: autoimmune destruction of beta cells → complete lack of endogenous insulin. Type 2: primarily insulin resistance (cells don't respond to insulin normally), with possible decreased secretion over time from beta cell exhaustion — insulin is often still present, just not used effectively.
What's the difference between DKA and HHNS?
DKA: most common in type 1, severe hyperglycemia (400-600s) with ketone-driven acidosis and ketonuria, can lead to coma. HHNS: primarily type 2, even higher hyperglycemia (600-800s) driven by extreme serum osmolality and major fluid shifts, from a less profound insulin deficiency than DKA.
What's the difference between diabetic microvascular and macrovascular complications, and name one example of each.
Microvascular = small capillary damage (eyes, kidneys, nerves) — e.g., diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, or neuropathy. Macrovascular = large/medium artery damage — e.g., coronary artery disease, stroke, or peripheral artery disease.
Rank rapid-acting, short-acting, intermediate-acting, and long-acting insulin by onset speed, and name one example of each.
Rapid (lispro, onset 15 min) → short (regular, 30-60 min) → intermediate (NPH, 2-4 hr) → long (glargine/Lantus, ~70 min onset but no peak, lasts 24 hr). Glargine is never mixed with other insulins.
A nurse is drawing up regular insulin and NPH in the same syringe. What's the correct order, and why?
Draw the clear (regular/rapid) insulin first, then the cloudy (NPH) insulin second — "clear before cloudy." This prevents contaminating the short-acting vial with longer-acting insulin, which could blunt a fast correction dose used later.
What is the single most important safety step before giving any dose of insulin?
Get a CURRENT blood sugar reading — checked immediately before the dose, not from an hour ago or earlier in the day.
What's the difference between the Somogyi effect and the dawn phenomenon?
Somogyi effect: rebound morning hyperglycemia caused by too much bedtime insulin driving nocturnal hypoglycemia, which triggers a counter-regulatory glucose release — a sign of insulin mismanagement. Dawn phenomenon: morning hyperglycemia from the body's normal early-morning cortisol/growth hormone release — not a dosing error, and usually less extreme.
Why does metformin need to be held around IV contrast administration, and why doesn't it typically cause hypoglycemia?
Metformin is renally cleared, and IV contrast is nephrotoxic — combining them risks acidosis, so metformin is held for about 48 hours around contrast. It doesn't typically cause hypoglycemia because it works by reducing hepatic glucose production and improving glucose uptake — it doesn't stimulate insulin secretion the way sulfonylureas do.
What's the difference between android and gynoid obesity, and which carries more cardiometabolic risk?
Android ("apple" shape, more common in males) stores fat in the abdomen — more likely visceral fat, carrying higher risk of coronary artery disease, hypertension, stroke, and diabetes. Gynoid ("pear" shape, more common in females) stores fat elsewhere, without that same elevated risk.
A patient meets 3 of the 5 metabolic syndrome criteria. What are the 5 criteria, and how is metabolic syndrome managed?
Elevated waist circumference (>40 in men / >35 in women), elevated triglycerides, low HDL, hypertension, and elevated fasting glucose. Managed by treating each component individually (antihypertensives, lipid-lowering agents, antidiabetic therapy) plus counseling on weight loss, exercise, diet, and smoking cessation — ideally through small, sustainable steps.
