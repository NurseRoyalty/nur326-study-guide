Week 6

Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new — it's a map of Week 6's five topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. The instructor's own opening framework for the whole week was that cardiovascular disease breaks into three kinds of problems: "electrical" (the conduction system), "plumbing" (the vessels), and "pump" (the heart muscle itself). That framework turns out to organize this entire week, including the two topics that don't fit neatly into just one of the three, and the pharmacology page that cuts across all of them. No flashcards or quiz questions here — just the map.

The Instructor's Own Framework, Applied to All Five Topics

Five pages is easier to hold in your head than the eleven separate pre-lecture voiceovers they were built from, but they still aren't five unrelated diseases — every one of them is really a plumbing problem, a pump problem, an electrical problem, or some combination.

CategoryTopicsThe Common Thread
Plumbing & Pump — The Heart ItselfIschemic Heart Disease & Heart FailureThis single page covers both halves of the instructor's framework for the heart itself: coronary artery disease is a plumbing problem (narrowed vessels, reduced blood flow), and heart failure is a pump problem (the muscle can't move enough blood). They're combined into one page because ischemic damage from the "plumbing" half is one of the direct causes of the "pump" half.
ElectricalCardiac DysrhythmiasThe heart's conduction system — from normal sinus rhythm all the way to V-fib. Explicitly kept separate from the plumbing/pump content, even though ischemia and heart failure are both named causes of specific dysrhythmias.
Plumbing & Pump, Beyond the Coronary ArteriesPeripheral Vascular Disease → Valve DiseasePeripheral vascular disease is the exact same atherosclerotic plumbing process as coronary artery disease, just in the leg vessels instead of the coronary arteries. Valve disease is a different kind of pump-adjacent problem — not muscle weakness, but a mechanical gatekeeper failing to open or close properly, which can itself cause or worsen heart failure.
Cutting Across All ThreeCardiac PharmacologyNitrates and ranolazine treat plumbing problems (angina); heart failure drugs treat pump problems; amiodarone/atropine/adenosine/dofetilide treat electrical problems. This page exists because the drugs don't sort neatly into just one of the other four pages.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, work through Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure first (it's the foundation the other pages keep referencing), then Cardiac Dysrhythmias, then the two "beyond the coronary arteries" pages (Peripheral Vascular Disease, then Valve Disease), and finish with Cardiac Pharmacology once you already know what each drug is actually treating.

The Five Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart FailureCAD narrows coronary vessels until angina or MI results; heart failure follows a predictable chain (contractility down → preload up → afterload up) and splits cleanly into left-vs-right (which body compartment congests) and HFrEF-vs-HFpEF (which physical problem — squeezing or filling — is broken).
2Cardiac DysrhythmiasEvery rhythm answers the same four questions (rate, regularity, P wave, QRS width) — and QRS width is the single biggest fork in the road, splitting the whole list into narrow-QRS (supraventricular) and wide-QRS (ventricular, the deadlier half) rhythms.
3Peripheral Vascular DiseasePAD (arterial) and chronic venous insufficiency (venous) are near-perfect opposites on every feature that matters — pain quality, skin appearance, and where ulcers form — even though both fall under the same "PVD" umbrella term.
4Valve DiseaseEvery valve problem is either stenosis (too tight) or regurgitation (too floppy), and the left-sided valves get the teaching focus because the left heart's higher pressure makes their failures more consequential — plus a full section on infective endocarditis and its embolic complications.
5Cardiac PharmacologyThree drug groups (antianginals, heart failure drugs, rate/rhythm-control drugs) built around the same plumbing/pump/electrical split as the rest of the week, plus a running distinction between genuinely new drugs and "pull-forward" drugs you already know from a prior pharm course.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

Every danger callout across the five pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart FailureCall 911 if chest pain isn't resolved within about 5 minutes — the concern point for unstable angina or a full MI.
Cardiac DysrhythmiasV-tach and V-fib are deadly rhythms requiring an immediate pulse check — CPR immediately if pulseless. Sustained V-tach (>10–15 sec) is a rapid-response emergency even with a pulse.
Peripheral Vascular DiseaseSmoking is the single biggest risk factor for PAD, doubling amputation risk — smoking cessation is the single most important intervention for both PAD and chronic venous insufficiency.
Valve DiseaseProsthetic-valve patients require prophylactic antibiotics before dental procedures. Roughly 40% of infective endocarditis patients develop neurologic/embolic stroke-type symptoms.
Cardiac PharmacologyHold digoxin and notify the provider if the 1-minute apical pulse is under 60 bpm — bradycardia is the hallmark toxicity sign. Never give atropine without continuous cardiac monitoring. Dofetilide is always started in-hospital on telemetry due to torsades de pointes risk.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Wide QRS means it's coming from the ventricle, and that's the deadlier half." Every narrow-QRS rhythm on Cardiac Dysrhythmias is manageable or even benign (PACs, sinus arrhythmia); the moment the QRS goes wide (PVCs, V-tach, V-fib), the stakes jump — the same logic that makes the "widow maker" LAD blockage and severe aortic stenosis dangerous: a large territory failing at once rather than a small, tolerable disruption.
  • "Potassium is the thread connecting dysrhythmias to nearly every heart failure drug." High or low potassium can trigger a dysrhythmia on its own, and half of the Cardiac Pharmacology drug list (MRAs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNIs, loop diuretics, digoxin) either raises or lowers potassium as a side effect — which is why "check the potassium" is such a recurring nursing action this week.
  • "Left-sided problems get more teaching attention because the left heart works under more pressure." Left-sided heart failure, left-sided valve disease (aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation) — the pattern holds across two completely different topics for the same underlying reason.
  • "The same disease process shows up in more than one vascular bed." Atherosclerosis is the root cause of both coronary artery disease and peripheral artery disease — same risk factors, same mechanism, different vessels and different symptoms.
  • "New drug vs. pull-forward drug" is worth tracking on every pharm page. Cardiac Pharmacology is explicit about which drugs are genuinely new this week (nitrates, ranolazine, ARNI, SGLT2 inhibitors, digoxin, amiodarone, atropine, adenosine, dofetilide) and which ones you're expected to already know cold — don't let a familiar drug name convince you there's nothing new to learn about its cardiac-specific role.
