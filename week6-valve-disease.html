Valve Disease

The heart has four valves, and their job is to keep blood moving forward in one direction. Valve disease happens when a valve's leaflets stop working properly — either they get too tight and restrict forward flow, or they get too floppy and let blood leak backward. This topic covers valve anatomy, the two general categories of valve dysfunction, the two specific conditions the lecture details (aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation), and infective endocarditis.

Valve Anatomy & Blood Flow

Blood flows through the four heart valves in a fixed order.

MNEMONIC: "TP-MA" — tricuspid → pulmonic → mitral → aortic. Also remembered as "tricycle before bicycle": the first two valves in the order (tricuspid, pulmonic) each have three leaflets like a tricycle's three wheels, and the pattern shifts at the mitral valve, which has only two leaflets like a bicycle's two wheels.
ValveLeafletsClassification
Tricuspid3Atrioventricular (AV) valve
Pulmonic3Semilunar (ventricular) valve
Mitral2Atrioventricular (AV) valve
Aortic3Semilunar (ventricular) valve

The tricuspid and mitral valves are grouped together as the AV valves; the pulmonic and aortic valves are grouped together as the semilunar (ventricular) valves. The tricuspid and aortic valves specifically open during systole.

General mechanisms of valve damage

  • Normal wear and tear over time
  • Calcium deposits building up on the leaflets
  • Problems with the valve annulus (the ring the leaflets attach to)
  • Leaflet malcoaptation — the leaflets don't fully open or fully close as they should
  • Endocarditis/infection
  • Clot formation
  • Papillary muscle rupture — applies only to the AV valves (tricuspid and mitral), since only they are anchored by papillary muscles/chordae tendineae

Two Categories of Valve Dysfunction

Stenosis

  • The leaflets become too tight or stiff.
  • This restricts forward flow through the valve.
  • Most commonly seen in the aortic valve.

Regurgitation

  • The leaflets become floppy or loose and don't close properly.
  • This allows blood to flow backward instead of moving forward.
  • Most commonly seen in the mitral valve.

The lecture focuses specifically on left-sided valve disease, because the left side of the heart works against much higher pressure than the right side — this makes left-sided valve problems generally more clinically significant.

Aortic Stenosis & Mitral Regurgitation

Aortic stenosis

  • Classic triad: syncope, lightheadedness, and chest pain.
  • The chest pain occurs because the coronary arteries — which branch off just past the aortic valve — don't receive adequate forward blood flow when the valve is stenotic.
  • If it becomes severe, it can progress to pulmonary edema, with crackles and cough.

Mitral regurgitation

  • Causes fatigue from reduced forward cardiac output — blood is regurgitating backward instead of moving forward efficiently.
  • Causes pulmonary symptoms/dyspnea from the backward flow of blood into the left atrium and then into the pulmonary veins/lungs.

Diagnosis

TermDetail
TTE (transthoracic echocardiogram)The standard/"regular" echocardiogram used to diagnose valve disease.
TEE (transesophageal echocardiogram)A more invasive alternative to a TTE that provides a closer, more detailed view.

Infective Endocarditis

Vegetations are infective masses that form on the valve leaflets. They impair the valve's ability to close properly and can also break off and embolize — travel elsewhere in the body.

TermDetail
Most commonly affected valveThe tricuspid valve — it's the first valve blood encounters when it enters the heart. This makes it especially relevant to IV drug users, since bloodstream infections introduced via IV injection travel through the venous system and reach the tricuspid valve first.
Common causative organismsStaphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis.
Risk groupsPatients with prosthetic (artificial) heart valves, and IV drug users.
SymptomsFever, chills, weight loss, inability to eat, muscle and joint pain, and heart murmurs (which can be difficult to hear/auscultate).
Patients with prosthetic heart valves require prophylactic antibiotics before dental procedures. Dental work is a known source of transient bacteremia, which puts a prosthetic valve at risk for infection.

Septic emboli

  • Septic emboli that break off from vegetations on the tricuspid valve (right-sided) travel to the lungs.
  • Septic emboli that break off from a left-sided valve travel systemically — to the brain, coronary arteries, spleen, kidney, or bowel.
Roughly 40% of infective endocarditis patients develop neurologic or embolic stroke-type symptoms. Meningitis, seizures, encephalopathy, and brain abscess are also possible complications.

Classic peripheral embolic/immunologic signs of infective endocarditis:

SignWhat It Looks/Feels Like
PetechiaeSmall red/purple pinpoint spots.
Splinter hemorrhagesThin, dark red-brown lines under the nail beds.
Janeway lesionsNon-tender, flat spots on the palms or soles.
Osler's nodesTender, raised nodules on the fingertips. A helpful distinction: Osler's nodes are tender, while Janeway lesions are not.
Roth spotsSpots seen on the retina during an eye exam.

Rheumatic Heart Disease

Another named cause of valve damage, originating from a rheumatic illness in childhood. Noted as especially relevant background for students who may go on to work in cardiovascular ICU or progressive/step-down cardiac care settings.

Treatment

Valve replacement

  • The treatment for significant valve disease.
  • When infection is involved, any active bloodstream infection must be cleared with antibiotics before and after surgery.
