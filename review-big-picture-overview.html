Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new — it's a map of the other 11 Pathopharm Review pages, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. A handful of big ideas (a few key hormones, immune function, glucose, electrolytes) keep showing up across different diseases and drug classes. This page shows you where each idea is actually taught in full, and where it just comes back as a supporting detail. No flashcards or quiz questions here — just the map.

Eleven pages is a lot to hold in your head at once. Most of them naturally fall into one of five groups — knowing the group can matter more than memorizing all eleven titles.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

Every danger callout across all 11 pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
HTN/HLDHypertensive emergency (BP ≥180/120 + end-organ damage) needs IV meds now. Never stop beta blockers/clonidine abruptly — rebound hypertension. ACE inhibitors can cause angioedema, which can close the airway.
Diabetes/Metabolic SyndromeHypoglycemia is a true emergency — check blood sugar immediately. Always get a current blood sugar before giving insulin, not one from earlier.
Coagulation/DVTPush protamine slowly — too fast causes profound hypotension; watch for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Phlegmasia cerulea dolens is a true emergency that can progress to amputation.
Blood Cells/CBC InterpretationNeutropenia — treat any infection as potentially life-threatening; use neutropenic precautions.
Fluid/ElectrolytesCorrect sodium imbalances slowly — too fast risks permanent neurologic damage. Never IV push undiluted potassium — can cause fatal ventricular fibrillation.
Pain/SeizuresNever give aspirin to a child with a viral illness — Reye syndrome. Acetaminophen max 4 g/24 hr (2 g if liver disease/alcohol use). Opioid respiratory rate < 10 needs immediate assessment. Status epilepticus is a true emergency. Never abruptly stop an antiepileptic drug.
Dementia, Depression & SUDSerotonin syndrome risk when combining serotonergic drugs. TCA overdose can be fatal — screen suicide risk first. MAOI + tyramine-rich food → hypertensive crisis. Never combine a benzodiazepine with another CNS depressant. Benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening — unlike opioid withdrawal.
GI, GU, Skin & HypersensitivityAmphotericin B is high-alert — nephrotoxic and cardiotoxic. Anaphylaxis is life-threatening. Epinephrine concentration mix-ups (1:1,000 vs. 1:10,000) can be fatal. Stop a blood transfusion immediately at the first sign of a reaction.
Stress & SteroidsIncrease steroid dosing during illness or major stress (sick-day dosing). Never stop long-term corticosteroids abruptly — risk of adrenal crisis.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Never stop this abruptly" shows up three separate times — beta blockers/clonidine (HTN/HLD), antiepileptic drugs (Pain/Seizures), and corticosteroids (Stress & Steroids). Different drugs, same underlying principle: the drug has been suppressing or replacing one of the body's own regulatory systems, and yanking it away doesn't give that system time to restart.
  • "Get a current number immediately before dosing" appears for both blood sugar before insulin (Diabetes/Metabolic Syndrome) and blood pressure/heart rate before a beta blocker (HTN/HLD) — the same instinct to verify the current state rather than trust an older reading.
  • "This can look mild at first but escalates fast" is really the same warning behind anaphylaxis, a transfusion reaction, ACE inhibitor angioedema, hypertensive crisis, and status epilepticus — five different diseases teaching the same nursing reflex: recognize early, escalate immediately.
