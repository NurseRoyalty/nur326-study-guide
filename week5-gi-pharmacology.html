GI Symptom Pharmacology

These drug classes treat nausea/vomiting and diarrhea as symptoms that show up across many different GI conditions, rather than belonging to any single disease — that's why they get their own page here instead of being folded into one disorder-specific page.

Antiemetics: Serotonin Blockers

TermDetail
Ondansetron (Zofran)A serotonin (5-HT3) receptor blocker. Blocks serotonin receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the brain, which affects the afferent vagal nerves in the stomach and small intestine. Can be given PO or IV. Originally developed for chemotherapy- and radiation-induced nausea/vomiting, but now used broadly for any type of nausea.

Adverse Reactions

  • Mild headache, diarrhea, or dizziness.
  • Some patients also experience constipation.
  • In general, ondansetron is very well tolerated.
Serotonin syndrome risk: because ondansetron affects serotonin, be aware of other serotonergic drugs — SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs, buspirone, and tramadol. A single dose of ondansetron is generally fine, but if a patient is taking ondansetron together with one of these other serotonergic drugs on a daily basis, that combination needs closer monitoring, especially on the first dose.

Antiemetics: Antihistamines

TermDetail
Dimenhydrinate (Dramamine)H1 receptor blocker. Blocks H1 receptors specifically within the inner ear, which is why it's also commonly used for motion sickness and dizziness — that same mechanism also helps with nausea.
Hydroxyzine (Vistaril)H1 receptor blocker. Same inner-ear H1 mechanism as dimenhydrinate, used for motion sickness, dizziness, and nausea.

These are H1 receptor blockers — not H2 receptor blockers. That's a common mix-up point worth calling out explicitly: H2 blockers are covered on the Esophageal & Gastric Disorders page for a completely different purpose.

Sedation and Anticholinergic Effects

  • Most of these drugs cause significant sedation and drowsiness, similar to other H1 blockers like diphenhydramine (Benadryl).
  • They also have anticholinergic effects.
  • Because of sedation and anticholinergic effects, use caution in the elderly — fall risk.
MNEMONIC: Anticholinergic toxidrome — "Mad as a hatter" (CNS/mental status changes), "blind as a bat" (blurred vision), "dry as a bone" (dry mucous membranes/secretions), "red as a beet" (flushing), "hot as a hare" (hyperthermia).
Never give hydroxyzine IV. It can be given IM, but IV administration can cause significant tissue damage, thrombosis, and gangrene.

Antiemetics: Dopamine Antagonists / Prokinetics

TermDetail
Metoclopramide (Reglan)Blocks dopamine receptors, increases the tone of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), and increases peristalsis in both the stomach and intestines. Can also sedate patients.

Antiemetic Use

  • Nausea/vomiting associated with chemotherapy, radiation, and opioids/opiates.

Prokinetic Use

  • GI motility issues in patients with decreased peristalsis — for example, patients with cystic fibrosis.
  • Patients waking up from a long surgery whose bowel sounds/peristalsis haven't returned yet — metoclopramide can help "turn that peristalsis back on."

There is a rare but severe side effect of metoclopramide: extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) — covered in the next section. EPS with metoclopramide is more common when it's given in combination with antipsychotic medications, or with regular/prolonged metoclopramide use.

Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

EPS are drug-induced movement disorders, most classically associated with antipsychotic medications, but also seen with metoclopramide (especially with regular use or in combination with antipsychotics).

TermDetail
AkathisiaRestlessness — the patient feels a constant desire/urge to move.
Acute dystoniaInvoluntary muscle contractions.
ParkinsonismSymptoms that look and feel like Parkinson's disease — tremors and rigid muscles in the limbs are common symptoms.
Tardive dyskinesiaA late-onset EPS finding that develops with progression/prolonged exposure — repetitive, involuntary facial movements such as tongue twisting, chewing motions, lip smacking, cheek puffing, and grimacing.
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is the worst-case EPS-related complication — very rare, but very life-threatening. The first signs are rigid muscles and fever, followed by drowsiness and confusion, which can ultimately progress to seizures. Metoclopramide can cause this because it works on dopamine, the same mechanism responsible for EPS with antipsychotic medications.

Antidiarrheal Drug Therapy — A Quick Review

Mechanism

  • These drugs work by decreasing intestinal peristalsis, which reduces intestinal fluid output/effluent — essentially slowing the gut down or drawing water out.

Main Concerns

  • Drowsiness.
  • Constipation.
  • Antidiarrheal combination drugs that contain atropine can cause bradycardia — atropine is anticholinergic, and it also has a drying effect on secretions. Use caution with driving/activities requiring alertness on these drugs.

PO or IV. It was originally developed for chemotherapy- and radiation-induced nausea/vomiting, but is now used broadly for any type of nausea.
