Part 1 — Liver Anatomy, Function & Jaundice

Liver Anatomy & Key Cells

TermDetail
Size & LocationThe largest organ in the body — about 3 pounds, receiving 5–7% of total cardiac output. Sits in the right epigastric region, enclosed in a fibrous capsule, divided into left and right lobes.
LobulesThe functional units of the liver — hepatocytes arranged around a central vein.
HepatocytesThe liver's main working cells. One of the few cell types in the body that can regenerate.
Kupffer CellsLine the liver's inner capillaries (sinusoids) — remove bacteria and toxins from the blood.

What the Liver Actually Does

The Liver's Five Jobs

  • Metabolizes and stores fat, carbohydrate, protein, vitamins, and minerals.
  • Blood volume reservoir — distends and compresses to hold or release blood based on circulating volume.
  • Blood filter — Kupffer cells purify the blood; the liver also removes bilirubin, a byproduct of hemoglobin breakdown.
  • Makes clotting factors, including prothrombin and fibrinogen.
  • Metabolizes and detoxifies drugs.
MNEMONIC: When the liver fails, work backward through its five jobs to predict the labs — no metabolism (glucose/protein problems), no reservoir/filter function (bilirubin and toxins build up), no clotting factors (bleeding), no drug metabolism (drugs accumulate). Nearly every abnormal value in this whole week traces back to one of these five jobs not happening anymore.

Portal Circulation

  • The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood to the liver from the stomach, intestines, spleen, and pancreas.
  • This blood is rich in nutrients absorbed from the intestinal tract — exactly why digested nutrients go straight to the liver before reaching the rest of the body.
  • This is also where the first-pass effect comes from — an oral drug passes through the portal circulation and liver before reaching systemic circulation, which is why oral doses are often higher than IV doses of the same drug.

First-pass effect and drug metabolism basics are covered in full on Pharmacology Foundations

Jaundice (Icterus)

Jaundice and icterus are the same thing — a yellowish discoloration from elevated bilirubin, usually visible around 2–2.5 mg/dL (normal roughly 0.6–1 mg/dL).

Conjugated (Direct) — ~30%

  • Already processed by the liver.
  • Elevated when it can't get OUT (bile duct obstruction, e.g. gallstones).

Unconjugated (Indirect) — ~70%

  • Not yet processed.
  • Elevated with overproduction (hemolysis) OR impaired liver function.
ClassificationWhat's HappeningBilirubin Pattern
Hemolytic JaundiceExcess RBC breakdown.Unconjugated
Hepatocellular JaundiceThe liver itself can't take up/conjugate bilirubin.Unconjugated
Obstructive JaundiceBile can't flow OUT — usually gallstones.Conjugated

Recognizing Jaundice at the Bedside

  • The sclera of the eyes is the best place to look, especially in a darker-skinned patient.
  • Dark, foamy urine (conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble); clay-colored stool if bile flow is obstructed.
  • Pruritus from backed-up bile salts depositing in the skin.

Part 2 — Viral Hepatitis (A, B & C)

The Basics & Pathogenesis

  • A systemic virus that mainly inflames the liver. This course focuses on hepatitis A, B, and C (HAV/HBV/HCV).
  • Viral infection triggers an immune response — inflammatory mediators cause infected cells to lyse, causing edema and swelling, which drives tissue hypoxia and, if severe, hepatocyte death.
  • Many cases are asymptomatic. When symptoms occur, they progress the same way regardless of strain — only transmission route, chronicity risk, and treatment differ.
PhaseTimingWhat Happens
Prodromal~2 weeks after exposureNonspecific symptoms (fatigue, anorexia, malaise, nausea, headache); most transmissible phase.
Icteric1–2 weeks after prodromal; lasts 2–6 weeksJaundice, dark urine, possibly clay stools, enlarged tender liver.
Recovery2–12 weeks after jaundice onsetSymptoms diminish; liver can stay enlarged/tender even as symptoms fade.

Possible Complications

  • Chronic hepatitis — the virus never fully clears.
  • Cirrhosis — permanent scarring (Part 3).
  • Liver cancer — B and C are both linked to hepatocellular carcinoma.
  • Fulminant viral hepatitis — a severe case progressing to acute liver failure.
Hepatitis AHepatitis BHepatitis C
TransmissionFecal-oralIV drug use, sexual contactSame as B; also mother-to-fetal
OnsetAcute, mildInsidious, 60–180 daysInsidious, more symptomatic

VaccineYes (2 doses)Yes (3-dose series)None
MNEMONIC: Hepatitis A is the "food and water" one (fecal-oral, acute, never chronic, has a vaccine). B and C are "blood and body fluid" ones (needles/sex, insidious, can go chronic) — B about 10% chronic, C about 80%.
StrainApproach
Hepatitis B (high-risk, chronic)Interferons + nucleoside analogs (entecavir, tenofovir); prolonged, expensive, high relapse rate — no great treatment exists.
Hepatitis CDirect-acting antivirals (DAAs) — can essentially eliminate the virus in most patients.
Acetaminophen with hepatitis: stay under 2 g/day (versus the usual 4 g/day) — avoid entirely in serious, advanced liver disease.

Part 3 — Cirrhosis & Liver Failure

Hepatitis vs. Cirrhosis

  • Hepatitis = inflammation — reversible.
  • Cirrhosis = irreversible fibrotic scarring, about 26% mortality, develops slowly over years.
Causes of Cirrhosis
Hepatitis B and C
Excessive alcohol intake
NAFLD / NASH
Autoimmune conditions, hereditary metabolic conditions, idiopathic

Fibrosis develops when structural injury (toxin or virus) triggers leukocyte infiltration and inflammatory mediators; chaotic fibrosis obstructs biliary channels and blood flow (jaundice, portal hypertension), and disrupted hepatocyte regeneration forms abnormal, poorly-perfused tissue — more scarring, more hypoxia, eventual liver failure.

Alcoholic Liver Disease StageWhat's Happening
1. Alcoholic Fatty LiverMild, asymptomatic, reversible if drinking stops.
2. Alcoholic SteatohepatitisIrreversible — anorexia, nausea, jaundice, edema can appear.
3. Alcoholic CirrhosisNecrosis, fibrosis, permanent scarring.
Late-Stage FindingWhy It Happens
JaundiceBilirubin build-up.
Peripheral Edema & AscitesLow albumin → low oncotic pressure; worsened by portal hypertension.
Bleeding TendencyNo clotting factors; portal hypertension bursts small veins too.
Spider AngiomataVascular skin lesions specific to cirrhosis.
Esophageal & Anorectal VaricesDistended veins from portal hypertension — can rupture.
Hepatic EncephalopathyLoss of detox function — see below.
MNEMONIC: every late-stage cirrhosis finding traces back to one of the liver's five jobs failing — no albumin/clotting → edema/ascites/bleeding; no bilirubin clearance → jaundice; no detox → hepatic encephalopathy; and portal hypertension adds varices on top.

Portal hypertension: fibrosis blocks free portal blood flow — like paper towels stuffed in a pipe. Asymptomatic until complications: variceal hemorrhage, ascites, gut bacteria translocation/peritonitis risk, hepatorenal syndrome, heart failure.

Variceal hemorrhage: a burst esophageal or anorectal varix is a true, high-mortality emergency — no drug reverses the underlying portal hypertension or fibrosis.
TermDetail
Hepatic EncephalopathyAmmonia crosses the blood-brain barrier and builds up. Diagnosed by level of consciousness, never by ammonia level alone. Occurs in ~30–45% of cirrhosis patients.

Cirrhosis

  • Slow, insidious onset over years.
  • Chronic fibrosis (alcohol, hepatitis, NAFLD).

Acute (Fulminant) Liver Failure

  • Sudden onset, not related to fibrosis.
  • Most often acetaminophen overdose; antidote is acetylcysteine.
DrugDetail
Lactulose (first-line)Colonic bacteria metabolize it into acids that draw water in (stooling) and trap ammonia as ammonium for excretion. Monitor potassium.
Rifaximin (second-line)Reduces ammonia-producing gut bacteria. Watch for C. diff.

Part 4 — Gallbladder & Pancreatic Disorders

These two organ systems share a root problem: a gallstone blocking the common bile duct can cause cholecystitis in the gallbladder AND back up pancreatic enzymes into the pancreas at the same time.

Cholelithiasis (Gallstones)

TermDetail
Why They FormImpaired metabolism of cholesterol, bilirubin, and bile acids — they saturate and aggregate into stones.
Stone TypesCholesterol (~70%, most common); pigmented; mixed pigmented (black, hard).
MNEMONIC: "Fair, fat, and 40" — the classic gallstone patient profile.

Biliary Colic

  • Epigastric/RUQ pain radiating to the back, classically 30 minutes to a couple hours after a fatty meal.
  • Most gallstones confined to the gallbladder are asymptomatic — pain starts when a stone lodges in the cystic or common duct.
FindingMechanism
JaundiceStone blocks the common bile duct.
Dark, Foamy UrineConjugated bilirubin backs up into blood and urine.
Clay-Colored StoolsNo bilirubin reaching the intestines.
SteatorrheaNo bile salts reaching the duodenum — fat malabsorption.
Bleeding TendencyNo vitamin K absorption without bile.

Cholecystitis

  • Gallbladder inflammation, usually from a lodged stone — edematous, hyperemic, distended gallbladder.
  • Fever, leukocytosis, rebound tenderness, guarding; increased bilirubin and alk phos.
Cholecystitis can progress to ischemia, necrosis, and perforation if wall pressure isn't relieved — a surgical priority.
TermDetail
IV Ketorolac (Toradol)First-line pain control (NSAID); opioids added if needed.
AnticholinergicsDecrease gallbladder secretion and smooth-muscle spasm.
CholecystectomyDefinitive treatment for both cholelithiasis and cholecystitis.

Acute Pancreatitis

Most often caused by a gallstone obstructing the common bile duct, blocking pancreatic enzyme outflow — trapped enzymes auto-digest the pancreas.

EnzymeEffect When Trapped
TrypsinEdema, necrosis, hemorrhage.
ElastaseHemorrhage.
Phospholipase AFat necrosis.
KallikreinEdema, vascular permeability, pain, shock risk.
LipaseFat necrosis.

Clinical Presentation

  • Severe, sudden-onset epigastric/mid-abdominal pain radiating to the back, with guarding.
  • Nausea/vomiting, abdominal distension, fever; severe cases can progress to hypovolemic shock.
  • Amylase rises with organ damage; glucose can go either direction; elevated WBC.
MNEMONIC: Gray Turner sign (flank bruising) and Cullen sign (belly-button bruising) = hemorrhagic pancreatitis. Cullen = belly button: vampires (and "the Cullens") don't have belly buttons. Both are severe, uncommon — not required to diagnose pancreatitis.

Pseudocyst

  • Fluid-filled cavity OUTSIDE the pancreas.
  • Can perforate and cause peritonitis.

Pancreatic Abscess

  • Infected fluid cavity INSIDE the pancreas.
  • Very high fever, leukocytosis.

Other complications: pleural effusion, hypovolemic shock, and tetany from hypocalcemia (lipase-generated free fatty acids bind calcium).

Chronic Pancreatitis

TermDetail
Most Common CauseAlcohol abuse — damages both acinar cells (digestive enzymes) and islet cells (glucose/insulin).
PresentationRecurring acute attacks plus ongoing pancreatic dysfunction between them; pain is the biggest complaint.
Other ManifestationsWeight loss (fat malabsorption); diabetes (islet cell loss).

Diabetes mellitus itself is covered in full on Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome; this page covers only the pancreas's exocrine dysfunction

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer. Three cards from each of the four parts above — a whole-week spot check.

What are the liver's five major jobs?
Metabolize/store nutrients, act as a blood volume reservoir, filter the blood (bilirubin removal), make clotting factors, and metabolize/detoxify drugs.
A patient has elevated CONJUGATED (direct) bilirubin. What does that point to?
An obstruction — the liver conjugated it fine, but it can't get OUT (classically a gallstone).
Where is the best place to assess for jaundice, especially in a darker-skinned patient?
The sclera of the eyes.
What percentage of hepatitis B infections become chronic? Hepatitis C?
About 10% for hepatitis B; about 80% for hepatitis C.
What's the daily acetaminophen limit for a patient with hepatitis, versus normal?
Under 2 grams/day with hepatitis, versus the usual 4 grams/day.
What drug class has made hepatitis C essentially curable?
Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs).
What's the key difference between hepatitis and cirrhosis?
Hepatitis is inflammation — reversible. Cirrhosis is fibrotic scarring — irreversible.
What causes hepatic encephalopathy, and what is it primarily diagnosed by?
Ammonia building up in the brain (the liver can't clear it). Diagnosed by level of consciousness — never by ammonia level alone.
What is the most common cause of acute (fulminant) liver failure?
Acetaminophen (Tylenol) overdose.
What makes cholecystitis more dangerous than simple cholelithiasis?
Pressure from the inflamed, distended gallbladder wall decreases blood flow, risking ischemia, necrosis, and perforation.
What is the most common cause of acute pancreatitis?
A gallstone obstructing the common bile duct, blocking pancreatic enzyme outflow.
What is the most common cause of CHRONIC pancreatitis, and what two problems does the resulting damage cause?
Alcohol abuse. Islet cell loss causes diabetes; loss of digestive enzymes causes fat malabsorption/weight loss.
