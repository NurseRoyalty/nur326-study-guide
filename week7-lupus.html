Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (Lupus)

Lupus is the last topic of the very long musculoskeletal slide deck. It is an abnormal immune reaction of the body against its own cells: a complex autoimmune disease in which autoantibodies (mainly ANA) attack the body's own DNA, forming immune complexes that circulate and land in almost any organ, where the inflammation they trigger destroys tissue. This page covers the types of lupus, how the disease works, who gets it and why, what patients look like (hallmark signs and an organ-by-organ list), the course and prognosis, flare warning signs and triggers, pharmacotherapy, and a comparison of lupus with rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Cross-reference: the RA-versus-lupus comparison below pairs with the RA lecture, and hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil) was first covered with RA. See Rheumatoid Arthritis

What Is Lupus?

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

  • The main type of lupus, and the one the lecture covers for the rest of the deck.
  • It is what people in general mean when they say "lupus" in the community.
  • "SLE" and "lupus" are used interchangeably; they all refer to the same disease process.
  • Systemic: it can involve many body systems.

Skin-only lupus

  • There is a type of lupus that affects only the skin.
  • The lecture only names it; it is not the version being taught.
  • The focus is the systemic version (SLE).

Definition

  • Lupus is an abnormal immune reaction of the body against its own cells, so it is an autoimmune disease.
  • The lecture calls it a particularly complex autoimmune disease.
  • It involves the production of autoantibodies.
TermDetail
Major autoantibodyANA = anti-nuclear antibody. This is the major autoantibody produced in lupus.
TestingAn ANA level can be drawn from plasma.
Target of the antibodiesThe body's own DNA. The antibodies are activated against the body's own DNA to form immune complexes.
Immune complexesThey circulate around the body and can target any major organ system: heart, skin, kidneys, brain.
ResultThe immune complexes trigger an inflammatory response that destroys whatever organ tissue they landed in.

How Lupus Damages the Body

The chain of events, in the instructor's own logic: the disease is "truly activated by the body's own DNA," and because DNA is everywhere in the body, the resulting immune complexes can land almost anywhere.

Step by step

  • 1. The immune system produces autoantibodies; the major one is ANA (anti-nuclear antibody).
  • 2. These antibodies are activated against the body's own DNA and form immune complexes.
  • 3. The immune complexes circulate around the body.
  • 4. They land in an organ (any major organ system can be targeted: heart, skin, kidneys, brain).
  • 5. They trigger an inflammatory response in that organ.
  • 6. That inflammatory response destroys the tissue where the complexes deposited.

Where RA complexes go

  • In RA, the immune complexes were deposited only in the synovial fluid.

Where lupus complexes go

  • In lupus they can deposit anywhere, because there is DNA everywhere and the complexes can just land somewhere.
  • The most common area is the kidney: the kidneys are very commonly affected when complexes deposit in the glomeruli.
  • The instructor stresses that the glomeruli are so important and can be pretty fragile.

Who Gets Lupus? Risk Factors

Risk factorWhat the lecture says
GeneticsThere is definitely an inherited genetic component.
SexFemales are much more likely to get lupus than males.
AgeTypically affects people aged 20 to 40, so it is a disease process of younger people.
RaceMost commonly affects Black and African American people.
Sun exposureAn environmental trigger thought to trigger a lupus flare-up; it can also be a predisposing factor.
HormonesHormones have a significant influence on the immune system and seem to be an important factor in developing the disease (see the two examples below).
Estrogen-containing oral contraceptivesWomen who take oral birth control that contains estrogen have a 50% higher risk of experiencing the disease.
Early menarcheWomen who started menstruating before age 10 have double the risk.
SmokingSmoking releases multiple toxins; the instructor says it is essentially a risk factor for everything.
Antibiotic allergiesIt is thought there is some relation to having a lot of allergies to certain antibiotics.

Quick picture of the typical patient (from the lecture's list): a younger (20 to 40) female, Black or African American, with a family (genetic) tendency; sun, estrogen-containing birth control, early menarche (before 10), smoking, and many antibiotic allergies all add to the risk. The instructor notes there is "definitely some kind of hormonal interplay."

Raynaud's-type changes

What happens and how it is treated

  • In the distal capillary regions, the small distal capillaries constrict and reduce blood flow.
  • It can be very painful.
  • It is usually triggered by cold.
  • Treatment: calcium channel blockers.

Organ-by-organ list: lupus can affect every organ system

The instructor warns that the list of manifestations is huge precisely because lupus can affect every single organ system.

Organ / systemPossible manifestations
BrainHeadache, dizziness, seizures, stroke.
LungsPleuritis, pleural effusion.
HeartMyocarditis, endocarditis.
KidneysAbout half of patients with lupus develop kidney complications. These can lead to permanent kidney damage and the need for dialysis or kidney transplant.
Blood vesselsVasculitis.
BloodAnemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia.
JointsArthritis.
Kidney involvement is the big one. The kidneys are the most common site of immune-complex deposition (the glomeruli), about half of lupus patients develop kidney complications, and the damage can be permanent, requiring dialysis or a kidney transplant. The instructor's example: Selena Gomez had to have a kidney transplant because of her battle with lupus and its effect on her kidneys.

Prognosis and Clinical Course

Poor prognosis

  • People with severe multi-system organ involvement (more than one organ involved) tend to have a very poor prognosis.

Good prognosis

  • The majority of people have mild disease.
  • They typically have a good prognosis and good survival outcomes with close monitoring and treatment.
  • The systems are usually not all affected at the same time; involvement can happen at different times.
  • Lupus has flare-ups, similar to how gout had flares.
  • The clinical course is exacerbations and remissions.

Flares: Warning Signs and Triggers

A flare is an acute exacerbation of the symptoms. The instructor's key point: try to recognize the warning signs and avoid the triggers.

Warning signs of a flare

  • More severe fatigue
  • Pain
  • Headache
  • The person just becomes more symptomatic in general, with systemic symptoms

Triggers to avoid

  • Sunlight exposure
  • Having an infection
  • Abruptly stopping a medicine
  • Stress

Pharmacotherapy

The goal is to control symptoms, and the choice of drug depends on which systems are involved.

Drug / classWhen and why it is used
NSAIDsFor headache, musculoskeletal symptoms, pleuritis, and pericarditis: anti-inflammatories like NSAIDs.
Steroids, high doseThese patients often do need steroids. High dose if there is severe kidney disease or a lot of CNS involvement.
Steroids, low doseLow dose if the problem is something like arthritis.
Hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil)Discussed earlier with RA. It helps with a lot of the skin and musculoskeletal problems and can prevent kidney and CNS organ damage. The instructor says you will see a lot of people with lupus on hydroxychloroquine.
Biologics and immunosuppressivesUsed if there is multi-organ involvement or if there is concern about progression.

RA vs. Lupus

The instructor points out a lot of similarities between RA and lupus, and a worksheet on Canvas helps you fill out the distinctions between the two.

FeatureRheumatoid arthritis (RA)Lupus (SLE)
Autoimmune?YesYes
Systemic?Yes: multiple body systems can be involvedYes: multiple body systems can be involved
Where immune complexes depositOnly in the synovial fluidAnywhere, because there is DNA everywhere (kidney glomeruli most common)
Main area involvedReally only the jointsAny kind of organ can be involved
Systemic fatigue and malaiseYes (in addition to the joint disease)Yes: fatigue is the most common symptom
Drug overlapHydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil) was discussed with RA and comes up again for lupus.

The difference to hold onto: in RA it is really only the joints (plus the systemic fatigue and malaise), while in lupus (SLE) any kind of organ can be involved.

What are the two kinds of lupus mentioned, and which one does the lecture focus on?
A type of lupus that affects only the skin, and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The lecture focuses on SLE, which is what people mean by "lupus" in the community; "SLE" and "lupus" are used interchangeably.
Define lupus.
An abnormal immune reaction of the body against its own cells: a particularly complex autoimmune disease that involves production of autoantibodies.
What is the major autoantibody in lupus, and how is it measured?
ANA (anti-nuclear antibody). An ANA level can be drawn from plasma.
Explain how lupus damages organs, step by step.
Autoantibodies (ANA) are activated against the body's own DNA and form immune complexes. The complexes circulate around the body and can target any major organ system (heart, skin, kidneys, brain). They trigger an inflammatory response that destroys the organ tissue they landed in.
Why can lupus affect so many organs, and where is it most common?
There is DNA everywhere, so the immune complexes can land anywhere. The most common area is the kidney, when complexes deposit in the glomeruli, which are important and can be fairly fragile.
Where do immune complexes deposit in RA compared with lupus?
In RA they were deposited only in the synovial fluid. In lupus they can deposit anywhere.
Who typically gets lupus?
People with an inherited genetic component; females much more than males; typically ages 20 to 40 (a younger disease); most commonly Black and African American people.
Give the two hormone-related risk statistics for lupus.
Women taking estrogen-containing oral contraceptives have a 50% higher risk; women who started menstruating before age 10 have double the risk.
What other risk factors does the lecture list for lupus besides genetics, sex, age, race, and hormones?
Sun exposure (an environmental trigger and predisposing factor), smoking (releases multiple toxins), and a relation to having a lot of allergies to certain antibiotics.
What is the most common symptom of lupus?
Fatigue, which can be extreme and very debilitating.
What is the butterfly rash?
One of the hallmark signs of lupus: a bilateral, red, irritating rash that spreads across the face like butterfly wings.
Besides fatigue and the butterfly rash, what other general signs do lupus patients show?
Photosensitivity (sensitivity to light and sunshine), edema (usually in the legs or around the eyes), fever, and unusual hair loss.
Describe the Raynaud's-type change in lupus and its treatment.
The distal small capillaries constrict and reduce blood flow; it can be very painful and is usually triggered by cold. It is treated with calcium channel blockers.
List the lupus manifestations by organ: brain, lungs, heart, blood vessels, blood, joints.
Brain: headache, dizziness, seizures, stroke. Lungs: pleuritis, pleural effusion. Heart: myocarditis, endocarditis. Blood vessels: vasculitis. Blood: anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia. Joints: arthritis.
How often is the kidney involved in lupus, and what can result?
About half of patients with lupus develop kidney complications. These can lead to permanent kidney damage and the need for dialysis or a kidney transplant (the lecture's example: Selena Gomez's kidney transplant).
What is the prognosis of lupus, and what is its clinical course?
Severe multi-system (more than one organ) involvement carries a very poor prognosis; most people have mild disease with a good prognosis and survival outcomes with close monitoring and treatment. The course is exacerbations (flares) and remissions, and the systems usually are not all affected at the same time.
What are the warning signs of a lupus flare, and what are the triggers to avoid?
Warning signs: more severe fatigue, pain, headache, and generally more symptomatic with systemic symptoms. Triggers: sunlight exposure, infection, abruptly stopping a medicine, and stress. The key is to recognize the warning signs and avoid the triggers.
How are NSAIDs and steroids used in lupus?
NSAIDs (anti-inflammatories) for headache, musculoskeletal symptoms, pleuritis, and pericarditis. Steroids are often needed: high dose for severe kidney disease or a lot of CNS involvement, low dose for something like arthritis.
What does hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil) do in lupus, and what else is used for multi-organ disease?
It helps with a lot of the skin and musculoskeletal problems and can prevent kidney and CNS organ damage, so many lupus patients are on it. Biologics and immunosuppressives are used if there is multi-organ involvement or concern about progression.
How are RA and lupus alike, and what is the major difference?
Both are autoimmune and systemic (multiple body systems can be involved). The major difference: RA is really only in the joints (with systemic fatigue and malaise), while in lupus (SLE) any kind of organ can be involved.
