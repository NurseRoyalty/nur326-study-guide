Intro to Pathophysiology

What Pathophysiology Is

Breaking Down the Word

  • Physiology is normal functioning — how the body works when there aren't any problems, at the molecular, cellular, and whole-organ-system level.
  • Pathology is the study of disease and injury — what abnormal processes are happening to disrupt the body from normal functioning.
  • Pathophysiology combines the two: the mechanisms of disease — how and why changes in body structure and function happen, and how those changes produce the signs and symptoms of a given disease.

Disease is a disruption in homeostasis — "homeo" (same) + "stasis" (balance/stability). This disruption can be physical (e.g., heart failure — the heart can't pump adequately, so the body doesn't get the blood/oxygen it needs), mental (e.g., chronic stress wearing down the body's compensatory mechanisms over time), or social (e.g., chronic social isolation, which is itself linked to disease).

Homeostasis & Compensation

The Body's Steady State

  • The body operates best within a narrow normal range for any given process — heart rate (typically 60–100 bpm) and temperature (around 98.5°F, with some individual variation) are two easy examples.
  • When something pushes a value outside that normal range, the body activates processes to bring it back toward homeostasis.
  • Disease manifests when the disruption is prolonged, or exceeds the body's ability to compensate.
DirectionCompensatory Response
Temperature RisesBlood vessels dilate (flushed/red skin) and sweat glands secrete fluid — both mechanisms lose heat to the environment, cooling the body back toward normal.
Temperature DropsBlood vessels constrict (pale skin) and sweat glands stop producing fluid, conserving heat. Shivering — involuntary muscle contraction — generates additional heat.

Causes of Disease — Intrinsic & Extrinsic Factors

TermDetail
Intrinsic FactorsCome from inside the body: genetics/genetic mutations, immune status (immunocompromised patients are more susceptible to disease), age, sex, autoimmune disorders (the body attacking itself), and over- or under-production of hormones/chemicals (e.g., insulin underproduction in type 1 diabetes, thyroid hormone imbalance).
Extrinsic FactorsCome from outside the body: pathogens (bacteria, viruses, parasites), environmental exposures (toxins, pollutants), lifestyle/behaviors (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol), and stressors that overwhelm the body's defenses.

The Epidemiologic Triangle (Epi-Triad)

  • Pathogen (agent): the causative factor responsible for disease — a bacteria, virus, or parasite.
  • Host: typically the human — susceptibility depends on genetics, age, and immune status.
  • Environment: the external conditions that influence how the pathogen and host interact (e.g., some bacteria can't survive heat, which is why certain foods are cooked to specific temperatures).
  • Disease risk emerges when all three components come together.

Identifying & Describing Disease

TermDetail
SignsObjective data — what can be seen, heard, or touched (e.g., wheezes on auscultation, a strong or weak pulse).
SymptomsSubjective data — what the patient reports (e.g., pain, nausea, dizziness).
OccurrenceHow often and when signs/symptoms happen (e.g., indigestion that occurs only after meals).
DiagnosisPutting a name to the disease (e.g., pneumonia, heart failure, hypertension).
EtiologyThe cause of the disease — not always known.
PrognosisThe expected disease course or outcome — the likelihood of returning to baseline.

Working up a symptom means asking detailed, specific questions. For a cough: is it productive (and if so, what does the sputum look like)? When does it happen — morning, after eating, with a choking/aspiration risk? Is it acute or chronic? Related to allergies, smoking, or medication? These details point toward the underlying disease process and the body systems involved.

Disease Onset & Course

TermDetail
ExposureContact with the agent or pathogen that sets off the disease cascade — a chemical, temperature, or organism exposure.
Sudden OnsetSigns/symptoms start quickly (e.g., a myocardial infarction, an asthma attack).
Insidious OnsetSlow and gradual — patients often adjust to symptoms until the disease has progressed significantly before it's caught (e.g., a slow GI bleed).
Latent OnsetThe disease process is present but inactive, and can become active later (e.g., the chickenpox virus lying dormant before reactivating as shingles).
ProdromalEarly, nonspecific signs that a disease/condition is beginning (e.g., chills or a mild headache before a cold or flu fully sets in).
ManifestationsThe signs and symptoms related to the disorder.
RemissionThe disease is no longer active.
ConvalescenceRecovering from the disease, back toward baseline.
TermDetail
IdiopathicThe cause of the disease is unknown (e.g., idiopathic cardiomyopathy — thickened heart muscle with no confirmed cause).
IatrogenicCaused by the healthcare field itself — a treatment, drug, or action (or inaction) caused the disease or complication (e.g., a colon perforation during a colonoscopy, a hospital-acquired infection, a medication error).
ExacerbationA worsening of disease — often an acute decline in a chronic condition (e.g., an asthma attack triggered by allergen exposure in a patient with chronic asthma).

Prefixes & Suffixes Worth Knowing

TermMeaning
Hypo-Under, below, low (e.g., hypotensive = low blood pressure).
Hyper-Above, over, high (e.g., hypertensive = high blood pressure).
-PeniaA lack of or deficiency (e.g., leukopenia = low white blood cell count).
-CytosisAn increase, usually referring to cells (e.g., leukocytosis = excess white blood cells, commonly seen with infection).
-OsisA process or condition — production, increase, invasion, or infection, generally (e.g., osmosis = movement of fluid through a cell membrane).
-ItisInflammation (e.g., hepatitis = inflammation of the liver).
-PathyDisease or suffering (e.g., myopathy = a disease of the muscles).
Opposites worth pairing up: hypo- (low) vs. hyper- (high); -penia (too few) vs. -cytosis (too many). Same root, opposite direction — a common exam trap when the terms are swapped.

Self-Test Flashcards

What's the difference between physiology, pathology, and pathophysiology?
Physiology is normal body function. Pathology is the study of disease/injury. Pathophysiology combines both — the mechanisms of how and why disease disrupts normal function.
What does "homeostasis" mean, and what happens when it's disrupted for too long?
"Homeo" (same) + "stasis" (balance) — the body's steady state. Prolonged or excessive disruption, beyond what the body can compensate for, is when disease manifests.
What compensatory responses occur when body temperature rises vs. drops?
Rising temperature: vessels dilate and sweat glands secrete fluid to lose heat. Dropping temperature: vessels constrict, sweating stops, and shivering generates heat to conserve it.
Give an example of an intrinsic and an extrinsic cause of disease.
Intrinsic (from inside the body): genetics, immune status, hormone over/underproduction, autoimmune disease. Extrinsic (from outside): pathogens, toxins, lifestyle behaviors, stressors.
What three components make up the epidemiologic triangle?
Pathogen (agent), host, and environment — disease risk emerges when all three come together.
What's the difference between a sign and a symptom?
A sign is objective data (seen, heard, or touched by the clinician). A symptom is subjective data (what the patient reports).
What's the difference between etiology and prognosis?
Etiology is the cause of the disease. Prognosis is the expected course/outcome — the likelihood of returning to baseline.
What's the difference between an insidious onset and a latent onset?
Insidious: slow, gradual symptom progression that often goes unnoticed until advanced. Latent: the disease process is present but inactive, capable of becoming active later (e.g., chickenpox virus reactivating as shingles).
What does "prodromal" mean?
The early, nonspecific signs that a disease is beginning — e.g., chills or a mild headache before a cold or flu fully sets in.
What's the difference between an idiopathic and an iatrogenic cause of disease?
Idiopathic: the cause is unknown. Iatrogenic: caused by the healthcare field itself — a treatment, drug, action, or inaction.
What does an "exacerbation" mean in the context of a chronic disease?
A worsening of the disease — often an acute decline in a chronic condition, like an asthma attack triggered by allergen exposure.
What do the suffixes "-penia" and "-cytosis" mean, and how do they differ?
"-Penia" means a deficiency/too few (e.g., leukopenia = low WBC count). "-Cytosis" means an increase/too many (e.g., leukocytosis = excess WBCs, often from infection) — opposite directions of the same root concept.
