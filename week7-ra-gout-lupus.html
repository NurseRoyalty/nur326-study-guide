Rheumatoid Arthritis, Gout & Lupus

This page covers three conditions in the order they were taught. It starts with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), the autoimmune contrast to osteoarthritis: its etiology, pathophysiology, manifestations, systemic involvement, and drug management (NSAIDs, prednisone, methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, and biologics), with the RA-versus-OA comparison. It then covers gout, from purine and uric acid through hyperuricemia, risk factors, phases, manifestations, complications, and the gout drugs. It finishes with systemic lupus erythematosus (lupus): how it works, risk factors, manifestations, course, flares, pharmacotherapy, and the RA-versus-lupus comparison.

Cross-reference: RA is taught here as the contrast to OA; the OA side of every comparison is covered in Osteoporosis, Fractures & Osteoarthritis

What RA Is

The lecture abbreviates rheumatoid arthritis as RA, the same way osteoarthritis was abbreviated OA.

  • RA is a systemic disease versus a localized condition. This is the biggest difference from OA.
  • It is an autoimmune disease: the body begins to attack itself. In RA, the body attacks the synovial tissue in the joints.
  • It is classified as a type 3 hypersensitivity. The instructor says that if that term is not familiar, you should go back to the hypersensitivity lecture.
  • The attack on the synovium develops inflammatory processes and destroys the area of the joint. RA is an inflammatory disease of the synovium.

Cross-reference: type 3 hypersensitivity is reviewed in GI, GU, Skin & Hypersensitivity Review

Etiology & Risk Factors

Etiology

  • The cause is not well understood. Despite intensive research, the exact cause remains unknown.
  • Like other autoimmune conditions, it is thought to involve both an environmental factor and a genetic factor. The instructor's way of saying it: there is a genetic link and a triggering event.
  • You can be genetically predisposed to RA, but just because you have that predisposition does not mean you will develop it.
  • Environmental factors that play a role include an infection, an injury, or hormonal/reproductive factors.

Risk factors

Risk factorWhat the lecture says

Tobacco useLinked to RA development. It is thought it may possibly be a trigger, or involve some of the infectious agents from the trigger.

Why the 40 to 60 age range matters: it makes RA all the more destructive, because that is a very active time in people's lives. Patients are mid to late career, still working, and may still have children growing up, so the disease definitely interferes with lifestyle. The instructor also notes that some patients can be diagnosed even earlier than 40 to 60.

Pathophysiology

The lecture's sequence, from the immune attack to joint destruction:

  1. Immune complexes (autoimmune complexes) get deposited in the synovial tissue, and immune cells attack that synovial tissue. The instructor says we do not know why they target the synovial tissue.
  2. The activated immune cells are typically B cells (lymphocytes) and macrophages. They secrete cytokines that further attract other white blood cells.
  3. These immune cells produce RF, rheumatoid factor: a specific or special antibody against our own IgG antibodies.
  4. During phagocytosis by these white blood cells, large quantities of lysosomal enzymes are released. These enzymes further damage the tissue and affect the surrounding tissue.
  5. The process is chronic, persistent, and destructive to the joints. Because of the intense inflammatory response, cartilage is destroyed.
  6. As tissue destruction progresses, scar tissue develops. This scar tissue causes a lot of the problem, and in RA joints it is specifically called pannus.

Pannus

  • Scar tissue in the RA joint.
  • Very vascularized, because all scar tissue is: it is trying to get nutrients in to heal.
  • Made up of lots of inflammatory cells: T cells, macrophages, and other inflammatory cells.
  • Because pannus contains active immune and inflammatory cells, it is very destructive to the surrounding tissues.
  • End result: you lose the nice synovial tissue and synovial joint fluid, and basically get bone on bone with inflammation.

Manifestations

Internally the joint is being destroyed, but externally RA is an early disease with not a ton of obvious signs. The lecture separates the early vague picture from the later joint picture.

Early disease

  • Because RA is systemic, there are not a ton of obvious symptoms early on. Maybe some joint pain and discomfort.
  • Vague systemic symptoms: fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, generalized stiffness.

Later joint manifestations

  • Eventually joint manifestations appear, and they are symmetrical.
  • Heat, swelling, tenderness, stiffness.
  • Stiffness usually starts in the morning and lasts longer than an hour.
  • Swelling feels spongy, soft, and warm.

Symmetry: a key distinction from OA

  • In RA the joints are affected symmetrically.
  • OA is wear and tear on a unilateral joint.

Swelling: RA versus OA

  • RA swelling is spongy, soft, and warm.
  • OA swelling is hard. The instructor's example: think of a swollen ankle from spraining it; that is more like OA swelling.
  • RA swelling is softer and spongier than that.

Subluxation

  • Joint subluxations may be seen, along with muscle atrophy and tendon destruction.
  • In subluxation, the bone is displaced from its normal joint position and partially loses contact with the articulating surface.
  • This is different from a dislocation, where the articulating surface completely loses contact. Subluxation is "just not aligned perfectly."
  • It decreases range of motion and is very painful.
  • You cannot fix it, because the tissue is eroded away.
  • Very common in the hands of RA patients, but can also be seen in the knees.

Deformity: no Heberden's or Bouchard's nodes

  • RA does not produce the Heberden's or Bouchard's nodes seen in OA.
  • Instead you get a swan deformity. These are not bony growths but actual curving of the joint because of the inflammation.
  • This is in the more advanced disease stages, along with the subluxations.

Systemic Involvement

The instructor says multiple times that RA means more systemic involvement: other body systems besides just the joints, even though RA particularly affects the synovial joint areas. When doing a physical assessment of someone with RA, it is very important to assess all the systems, particularly the heart. RA can potentially affect any and all body systems, depending on how severe the disease is.

AreaWhat the lecture says
Fatigue and malaiseAn exceeding level of tiredness.
HeartCan develop pericarditis or myocarditis. The instructor says to assess the heart in particular.
LungsCan develop nodules on the lungs. They are typically monitored, maybe through x-rays or CT scans occasionally, to ensure they are not internal.
Sjogren's syndromeDestruction of moisture-producing glands: the lacrimal glands (eyes) and the salivary glands. Result: dry mouth, dry itchy eyes (dry tongue also shown in the lecture picture).
Rheumatoid nodulesImmune-mediated granulomas. Typically develop around the inflamed joints. Subcutaneous, firm, and sometimes painful. About 25% of people with RA develop them.

What to take away (instructor's emphasis)

  • You do not need to memorize all of the systemic manifestations. Remember that it is very important to understand the systemic involvement as a whole.
  • But you should be able to pick up on Sjogren's syndrome and rheumatoid nodules.

Management Overview

  • Like OA, the goals include relieving pain and swelling.
  • The difference: with OA, we cannot really do anything to stop or slow disease progression other than encourage weight-bearing activities and proper joint movement and motion. With RA, there are things we can do to actually slow the disease process, and we are getting much better at this than even 20 years ago.
  • Typically there is long-term drug therapy, and it requires a lot of patient adherence.

Glucocorticoids (Prednisone)

  • Prednisone is usually the steroid of choice in RA. Prednisone or hydrocortisone are the glucocorticoids named for short-term management of severe or acute flare-ups.
  • It causes a rapid suppression of inflammation, helping with the inflammatory processes in the joints.
  • It is used only when symptoms cannot be controlled, meaning the inflammation is not controlled with an NSAID.
  • There is lots of risk with long-term steroids, so the team tries very hard not to use them.
  • If a longer term is needed, it is usually in very small doses: less than 10 mg a day, usually as small as 2.5 mg.
  • Usually used in conjunction with DMARDs, in addition to NSAIDs.

Cross-reference: short- and long-term steroid concerns are covered in Stress & Steroids Review

DMARD 1: Methotrexate

Methotrexate is one of the first DMARDs. It is still used today as a first-line agent for RA in most patients. It does have a lot of side effects, but it does help. The instructor tells you to put it on your drug matrix, along with hydroxychloroquine.

Methotrexate is given ONCE PER WEEK, never daily. This is a huge safety issue: death can occur if it is administered daily instead of weekly. It is definitely something to teach the patient about.

Putting the risks and benefits together

  • The lecture admits this all sounds very bad and that it is an intense drug to take.
  • We are getting better at it: changing the dosing, monitoring closer, and understanding the side effects.
  • It does help these patients: it slows disease progression, they are able to function in daily life, and they typically can live pretty normally to an extent. They have a lot of things to deal with, but it helps.

DMARD 2: Hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil)

  • Not used as frequently for RA. It is actually an antimalarial drug (the instructor notes you probably heard about it during the COVID pandemic).
  • It can sometimes be used in anti-rheumatic diseases because of its anti-inflammatory effects: it dampens macrophage, T-helper cell, and inflammatory cytokine activity, turning down the inflammatory process.
  • It can slow the progression of RA, especially in combination with other DMARDs.
  • Much better tolerated than methotrexate, with fewer side effects.
  • About a 1% chance of retinopathy, so it is pretty rare. It can lead to blindness.
  • Teaching: tell patients that if they develop blurry vision or any vision problems, they should contact their health care provider.

Biologic Response Modifiers (Biologics)

  • Newer drugs used for really all autoimmune conditions (the lecture notes there are tons of autoimmune conditions, and RA is one of the big ones).
  • Sometimes just called biologics. They target the part of the immune system that triggers inflammation and causes joint tissue damage. Basically they target the body's own immune system and tell it to stop acting crazy.
  • Because they are newer, we are less familiar with how they affect patients longer term.
  • Known risks: increased risk of severe skin or lung infections, skin cancers, and some people can have serious allergic reactions.
  • There is a ton of them. You do not need to put any on your drug matrix, and you do not even need to know their names, but you should probably recognize many of them from commercials.
  • They are very expensive, but they work pretty well to keep these autoimmune conditions subdued.
  • Be aware of them: if you work in areas that care for these patients, you will begin to see them.

What Gout Is

The instructor opens by saying you have heard the word gout many times by now in your nursing career, including when kidney stones and some other conditions were discussed. The lecture separates two terms that are often used together.

Gout

  • The metabolic problem itself.
  • A disruption in uric acid metabolism.
  • Urate salts are deposited in certain tissues, generally in and around joints.

Gouty arthritis

  • How the gout actually affects the joint, and the inflammation of that joint.
  • An acute and very painful condition.
  • The condition is typically referred to as gouty arthritis.
  • Crystal deposits of uric acid are present in the joint (the lecture slide shows them).

"The disease of kings"

  • Gout is still talked about as "the disease of kings."
  • This is because it traditionally came from eating foods associated with wealthy people: smoked meats, cheeses, red wine, and beer.
  • Many old, rich kings had it back in the day, but now many different people get it.

Where Uric Acid Comes From: Purine

The crystals form because of the breakdown of purine.

The purine-to-uric-acid pathway

  • Purine is a nitrogen-containing compound; it is the parent compound of nitrogenous waste.
  • Purine breaks down into uric acid.
  • Uric acid is excreted in the urine by the kidneys.
  • Normally, uric acid dissolves in the blood and is excreted by the kidneys with no problem.
  • In people who develop gout, there is an inability to process it normally and they develop hyperuricemia.
High-purine foods and drinks named in the lecture
Alcohol, including beer and red wine
Organ meat: liver, kidney, brain
Red meat
Seafood, such as scallops

The instructor's point: there are many things that have a lot of purine in them. The "disease of kings" foods (smoked meats, cheeses, red wine, beer) are the traditional examples tied to gout.

Hyperuricemia: Why It Happens

Hyperuricemia (high uric acid in the blood) is usually related to two problems working together.

Overproduction of uric acid

  • Uric acid is overproduced.
  • This is the situation allopurinol is aimed at (see the drug section).

Underexcretion of uric acid

  • Not enough uric acid is excreted.

The changing stereotype

  • It used to be that only the rich could eat foods high in purine.
  • So the stereotypical image of a gout sufferer was a wealthy, overweight male.
  • Now obesity is seen in all different sociodemographics, so gout is no longer a disease of the wealthy but a disease of anyone who has this kind of diet.

Phases of Gout

The lecture numbers three phases and then describes progression to chronic gouty arthritis (shown here as phase 4). Hyperuricemia can persist even when the patient has no symptoms.

PhaseWhat is happening
1. AsymptomaticThe patient has no symptoms but has elevated serum uric acid levels. If the tissues are tested, crystals can be seen in the tissues. The crystals begin to accumulate, the tissue becomes damaged, and this causes acute inflammation.
2. Acute flare (attack)The acute inflammation becomes an acute flare or attack. There is a very high level of hyperuricemia.
3. Clinically inactive (between attacks)The patient continues to have hyperuricemia, but the inflammation is not there. They may have another flare-up in months or years.
4. Chronic gouty arthritisTypically, as patients age and the disease progresses, attacks recur closer and closer together until they develop chronic gouty arthritis, with joint pain and other symptoms present most of the time.

What the Patient Looks Like: Manifestations

Clinical picture of gout inflammation

  • Pain is the hallmark sign. It can be excruciating.
  • Burning pain, redness, swelling, and warmth of the joint.
  • Fever.
  • Symptoms are typically present for days to weeks.
  • It can be difficult to walk, and attacks can happen again.

Most common joint: the big toe

  • The joint most commonly attacked is the big toe joint, the large metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint.
  • About 50% of people with gout get it in that joint specifically.
  • It can occur in other joints, but the feet and the big toe are very common.

How severe the pain can be

  • The pain can get so intense that patients cannot even put on socks, and their feet cannot touch the bedsheets.
  • The instructor recalls seeing this in a real patient: staff could not touch his feet or lay even one thin hospital sheet over his toe. She says she once rolled her eyes learning about this in nursing school, until she saw it herself.

Nursing approach and patient teaching

  • We treat the symptoms.
  • We teach patients to stop drinking and stop eating this kind of diet; the hope is they will then be able to control it.

Complications

Tophi

  • Large, hard nodules made of uric acid crystal deposits in the soft tissue.
  • They may form below the skin, around the joints.
  • They can cause a local inflammatory response.
  • If drained, they may drain chalky material.
  • The lecture slides show pictures of tophi.

Kidney stones

  • Patients with gout are at high risk for kidney stones.
  • The instructor notes gout was already mentioned in the renal disorders lecture.

Cross-reference: kidney stones and the earlier gout mention are taught in Renal Disorders

Drug Therapy for Gout

First-line for an acute attack: NSAIDs

  • NSAIDs are typically the drug of choice for acute attacks.
  • They give anti-inflammatory effects and help reduce symptoms.

Steroids: only in limited situations

  • Similar to how steroids are used in other conditions, steroids are only given to patients who cannot take NSAIDs or whose gout is not responding to them.

The same NSAID and steroid theme (steroids reserved for when NSAIDs are not enough) is discussed for RA in the Glucocorticoids (Prednisone) section above.

Goals of gout-specific drugs

  • Decrease the symptoms of an acute attack.
  • Prevent a recurrence.
  • Besides NSAIDs, there are three drugs: allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid.
Drug matrix: the instructor says all three of these drugs (allopurinol, colchicine, probenecid) need to be on your drug matrix.

Allopurinol in detail

Prophylaxis only

  • Allopurinol is a prophylactic drug only; it will not help with acute attacks. Patients take it over a long time.
  • It is a xanthine oxidase inhibitor. The enzyme is needed to convert purine into uric acid, so blocking it prevents uric acid production.
  • The instructor says you do not necessarily need to remember the detailed mechanism, but you need to know it is a xanthine oxidase enzyme inhibitor.
  • It is for patients whose gout is related to excess uric acid production, as seen in hyperuricemia; again, it is a prevention medication.
  • Onset is 2 to 6 weeks, after which patients see their flare start dying down. They then continue taking it to prevent flares in the future.
  • Overall it is very well tolerated, but it is not a drug with no issues: it has some interactions and some rare effects of concern.
Allopurinol and skin reactions: the most common side effect is rash, but allopurinol is known to cause Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), which can turn into toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN). The instructor calls this a fatal skin condition that is often considered like an anaphylactic reaction.
Allopurinol and blood problems: there is a chance of agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia, so we are very worried about infection. Monitor white blood cells and the CBC.

Allopurinol interactions and what to teach

  • Hypoglycemic agents (anti-diabetes medicines): allopurinol can increase their effects. Examples given: metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists. It may cause hypoglycemia, so tell these patients to monitor their sugar closely.
  • Warfarin: allopurinol can increase its effect. Monitor the INR closely to make sure the blood is not getting too thin.
  • Effectiveness: monitored with serum uric acid levels.

Colchicine in detail

Oldest therapy, second-line

  • The oldest available therapy for gout, but second-line treatment.
  • It reduces the inflammatory response to the urate crystal deposits in the joints.
  • Mechanism: an inhibitor of cell mitosis; it can cause short-term leukopenia and bone marrow suppression.
  • The instructor says its action is not greatly understood, but it is thought to inhibit the metabolism, mobility, and chemotaxis of polymorphonuclear leukocytes. Basically it decreases inflammation, which is wanted in gouty arthritis, but it comes with side effects of concern.
  • Usually used for flares; it can be used more short-term for flare-ups. It can also be used for prophylaxis if the patient is not tolerating allopurinol.
  • It can be given PO only.
Colchicine toxicity: stop immediately. The drug can cause GI bleeding and disrupts cell mitosis in the GI system. If the patient has stomach-flu-type symptoms, this is an early sign of drug toxicity and the colchicine must be stopped immediately. Persistent vomiting is a major concern. If the drug is not stopped, the patient can go into multiple organ failure and die.

At this point the instructor also lists any person with severe renal, gastrointestinal, hepatic, or cardiac disorders, or bleeding disorders (the sentence is not finished in the lecture).

Probenecid in detail

Uricosuric agent

  • A uricosuric agent that effectively reduces serum uric acid levels.
  • It inhibits the reabsorption of uric acid, so it helps with hyperuricemia.
  • Can be used alone or in combination with allopurinol.
  • There are lots of drug interactions with this medication, so monitor for them if you are giving or prescribing it.

Patient teaching for probenecid

  • Take with food and drink and plenty of fluids.
  • Report signs of kidney problems: hematuria, change in the amount of urine, weight gain.
  • Report any signs of liver issues.

What Is Lupus?

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

  • The main type of lupus, and the one the lecture covers for the rest of the deck.
  • It is what people in general mean when they say "lupus" in the community.
  • "SLE" and "lupus" are used interchangeably; they all refer to the same disease process.
  • Systemic: it can involve many body systems.

Skin-only lupus

  • There is a type of lupus that affects only the skin.
  • The lecture only names it; it is not the version being taught.
  • The focus is the systemic version (SLE).

Definition

  • Lupus is an abnormal immune reaction of the body against its own cells, so it is an autoimmune disease.
  • The lecture calls it a particularly complex autoimmune disease.
  • It involves the production of autoantibodies.
TermDetail
Major autoantibodyANA = anti-nuclear antibody. This is the major autoantibody produced in lupus.
TestingAn ANA level can be drawn from plasma.
Target of the antibodiesThe body's own DNA. The antibodies are activated against the body's own DNA to form immune complexes.
Immune complexesThey circulate around the body and can target any major organ system: heart, skin, kidneys, brain.
ResultThe immune complexes trigger an inflammatory response that destroys whatever organ tissue they landed in.

How Lupus Damages the Body

The chain of events, in the instructor's own logic: the disease is "truly activated by the body's own DNA," and because DNA is everywhere in the body, the resulting immune complexes can land almost anywhere.

Step by step

  • 1. The immune system produces autoantibodies; the major one is ANA (anti-nuclear antibody).
  • 2. These antibodies are activated against the body's own DNA and form immune complexes.
  • 3. The immune complexes circulate around the body.
  • 4. They land in an organ (any major organ system can be targeted: heart, skin, kidneys, brain).
  • 5. They trigger an inflammatory response in that organ.
  • 6. That inflammatory response destroys the tissue where the complexes deposited.

Where RA complexes go

  • In RA, the immune complexes were deposited only in the synovial fluid.

Where lupus complexes go

  • In lupus they can deposit anywhere, because there is DNA everywhere and the complexes can just land somewhere.
  • The most common area is the kidney: the kidneys are very commonly affected when complexes deposit in the glomeruli.
  • The instructor stresses that the glomeruli are so important and can be pretty fragile.

Immune complexes and the hypersensitivity types are reviewed in the GI, GU, Skin & Hypersensitivity Review (see the cross-reference in the Rheumatoid Arthritis section above).

The instructor's example: Selena Gomez had to have a kidney transplant because of her battle with lupus and its effect on her kidneys.

Prognosis and Clinical Course

Poor prognosis

  • People with severe multi-system organ involvement (more than one organ involved) tend to have a very poor prognosis.

Good prognosis

  • The majority of people have mild disease.
  • They typically have a good prognosis and good survival outcomes with close monitoring and treatment.
  • The systems are usually not all affected at the same time; involvement can happen at different times.
  • Lupus has flare-ups, similar to how gout had flares.
  • The clinical course is exacerbations and remissions.

Flares: Warning Signs and Triggers

A flare is an acute exacerbation of the symptoms. The instructor's key point: try to recognize the warning signs and avoid the triggers.

Warning signs of a flare

  • More severe fatigue
  • Pain
  • Headache
  • The person just becomes more symptomatic in general, with systemic symptoms

Triggers to avoid

  • Sunlight exposure
  • Having an infection
  • Abruptly stopping a medicine
  • Stress

What is the biggest difference between RA and OA, what type of hypersensitivity is RA, and what does the body attack?
RA is a systemic, autoimmune disease versus OA, a localized condition. RA is classified as a type 3 hypersensitivity. The body attacks the synovial tissue in the joints, developing inflammatory processes and destroying the area of the joint; RA is an inflammatory disease of the synovium.
What does the lecture say about the cause of RA?
The exact cause is unknown. It is thought to involve a genetic factor and an environmental factor: a genetic link plus a triggering event. Being predisposed does not mean you will develop RA. Environmental factors include infection, injury, and hormonal/reproductive factors.
List the risk factors for RA given in lecture, and why is the typical age range especially destructive?
Genetic link/family history (pretty significant), age 40 to 60, female sex (2.5 times higher risk than men), and tobacco use (possibly a trigger or related to infectious agents from the trigger). The 40 to 60 range is a very active time in people's lives (mid to late career, possibly children still growing up), so the disease definitely interferes with lifestyle. OA, by contrast, increases with age.
Which immune cells are activated in RA, and what is rheumatoid factor (RF)?
Typically B cells (lymphocytes) and macrophages; they secrete cytokines that further attract other white blood cells. They produce RF, a special antibody against our own IgG antibodies. RF can be measured in the serum, which is how diagnosis is confirmed in addition to symptoms and physical assessment; CRP or sed rate can also be drawn as inflammatory labs.
What is pannus, and why is it so destructive?
The scar tissue in RA joints. It is very vascularized and made up of inflammatory cells (T cells, macrophages, others). Because it contains active immune and inflammatory cells, it is very destructive to surrounding tissue, leading to loss of synovial tissue and fluid and bone-on-bone with inflammation.
What vague symptoms appear early in RA, and what are the later joint findings?
Early: fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, generalized stiffness. Later: symmetrical joint heat, swelling, tenderness, and stiffness that starts in the morning and lasts longer than an hour.
Compare RA and OA in joint distribution, swelling, and joint deformity.
RA is symmetrical with spongy, soft, warm swelling; OA is wear and tear on a unilateral joint with hard swelling (like a sprained, swollen ankle). OA gives Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes (bony growths); RA does not, and instead produces swan deformity (curving of the joint from inflammation, not bony growths) in more advanced stages.
Distinguish subluxation from dislocation, and why can it not be fixed in RA?
Subluxation: the bone is displaced and partially loses contact with the articulating surface. Dislocation: the articulating surface completely loses contact. In RA it cannot be fixed because the tissue is eroded away; it decreases range of motion and is very painful, commonly seen in the hands and also the knees.
Which systemic manifestations of RA does the instructor say you should be able to pick up on, and what do they involve?
Sjogren's syndrome (destruction of moisture-producing glands: lacrimal and salivary, giving dry itchy eyes and dry mouth) and rheumatoid nodules (immune-mediated granulomas around inflamed joints; subcutaneous, firm, sometimes painful; about 25% of RA patients). Also assess the heart (pericarditis, myocarditis) and note lung nodules.
How does management of RA differ from OA, and what drug groups are used?
In OA nothing really stops or slows progression beyond weight-bearing activity and proper joint movement; in RA drugs can actually slow the disease. Therapy is long-term and needs patient adherence: NSAIDs for immediate relief, glucocorticoids for severe/acute flares, DMARDs, and biologics.
When and how is prednisone used in RA?
It is usually the steroid of choice, giving rapid suppression of inflammation, used when symptoms are not controlled with an NSAID. Because of long-term risks, it is avoided where possible; if needed longer term it is in very small doses (less than 10 mg a day, often as little as 2.5 mg), together with DMARDs and NSAIDs.
What is the major safety point about how methotrexate is dosed, and how does it work?
It is given once per week, never daily; death can occur if given daily instead of weekly, so teach this. It interferes with folate metabolism and is an immunosuppressive, so patients always need folic acid. It is a first-line RA agent in most patients.
List the methotrexate side effects, monitoring, and precautions from lecture.
GI effects (nausea, anorexia, vomiting); liver toxicity (monitor AST, ALT, alk phos; no alcohol); bone marrow suppression (monitor WBC/RBC, CBC); very high infection risk (see a doctor ASAP for infection signs); teratogenic (two forms of birth control); not for pre-existing liver or kidney conditions; 11 black box warnings; lecture notes an aplastic anemia risk using NSAIDs; shortened life expectancy from cardiovascular disease, cancer, and other conditions, so monitor risk versus benefit.
What is hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil), and what is its key risk?
An antimalarial used as a DMARD that dampens macrophage, T-helper cell, and inflammatory cytokine activity; it can slow RA progression, especially combined with other DMARDs. It is better tolerated than methotrexate, with about a 1% chance of retinopathy (can lead to blindness), so patients should report blurry vision or any vision problems.
What do you need to know about biologic response modifiers?
Newer drugs that target the part of the immune system that triggers inflammation and joint damage. Longer-term effects are less familiar. Risks: severe skin or lung infections, skin cancers, serious allergic reactions. They are very expensive but work well; you do not need to put any on your drug matrix or know their names.
Distinguish gout from gouty arthritis, and why is gout called "the disease of kings"?
Gout is the metabolic problem itself: a disruption in uric acid metabolism with urate salts deposited in tissues, generally in and around joints. Gouty arthritis is how gout affects the joint, meaning the inflammation of that joint; it is an acute and very painful condition. "Disease of kings" comes from traditionally eating foods associated with wealthy people (smoked meats, cheeses, red wine, beer); today many different people get it, because obesity now occurs across all sociodemographics.
Where does uric acid come from, and what two problems cause hyperuricemia?
Purine is a nitrogen-containing compound (the parent compound of nitrogenous waste) that breaks down into uric acid, normally dissolved in the blood and excreted in the urine by the kidneys. In gout the body cannot process it normally and hyperuricemia develops, from overproduction and underexcretion of uric acid.
Name the high-purine foods and drinks given in the lecture.
Alcohol (beer, red wine), organ meat (liver, kidney, brain), red meat, and seafood such as scallops.
List the risk factors for gout named in the lecture.
Male sex, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, sickle cell anemia, regular alcohol use (especially alcohol use disorders), a high-purine diet, diuretic use, and Black or African American race.
Describe the four phases of gout.
Phase 1 is asymptomatic: elevated serum uric acid, and crystals can be seen if tissues are tested; they accumulate and damage the tissue, causing acute inflammation. Phase 2 is the acute flare or attack, with a very high level of hyperuricemia. Phase 3 is clinically inactive: hyperuricemia continues but the inflammation is not there; another flare may come in months or years. Phase 4 is chronic gouty arthritis: attacks recur closer and closer together until joint pain and other symptoms are present most of the time.
What is the hallmark sign of gout, and which joint is most commonly affected?
Pain is the hallmark sign and can be excruciating, with burning pain, redness, swelling, and warmth of the joint, plus fever; symptoms are typically present for days to weeks. The most common joint is the big toe joint (the large metatarsophalangeal joint); about 50% of people with gout get it there. Patients may be unable to put on socks or have anything touch the foot, even a thin sheet.
What are tophi, and what other serious complication are gout patients at high risk for?
Tophi are large, hard nodules of uric acid crystal deposits in soft tissue. They may form below the skin, around the joints, can cause a local inflammatory response, and may drain chalky material if drained. Gout patients are also at high risk for kidney stones (already covered in the renal disorders lecture).
What is the drug of choice for an acute gout attack, when are steroids used, and which three gout drugs belong on your drug matrix?
NSAIDs are the drug of choice for acute attacks, for their anti-inflammatory effect and symptom relief. Steroids are only given if the patient cannot take NSAIDs or the gout is not responding. Goals of drug therapy: decrease symptoms of an acute attack and prevent recurrence. The three drugs for your matrix are allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid.
How does allopurinol work, who is it for, and how soon does it work?
It is a prophylactic drug only (not for acute attacks), taken long term. It is a xanthine oxidase inhibitor; the enzyme is needed to convert purine into uric acid, so it prevents uric acid production. It is for gout related to excess uric acid production. It takes effect after 2 to 6 weeks, and patients continue it to prevent future flares.
What are the side effects of allopurinol, and what is monitored?
Most common is rash. There is a chance of agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia (worry about infection), and it is known to cause Stevens-Johnson syndrome, which can turn into TEN, a fatal skin condition often considered like an anaphylactic reaction. Monitor serum uric acid (effectiveness) and WBC/CBC.
What are allopurinol's key drug interactions and the related nursing teaching?
It can increase the effects of hypoglycemic agents (e.g., metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists), possibly causing hypoglycemia, so patients should monitor their sugar closely. It can increase the effects of warfarin, so monitor the INR closely to make sure the blood is not getting too thin.
Summarize colchicine: place in therapy, mechanism, route, and the toxicity warning.
The oldest available gout therapy but second-line; reduces inflammation to urate crystals and is usually used for flares (prophylaxis if allopurinol is not tolerated). It inhibits cell mitosis (short-term leukopenia and bone marrow suppression) and is thought to inhibit metabolism, mobility, and chemotaxis of polymorphonuclear leukocytes. PO only. Stomach-flu-type symptoms or persistent vomiting are an early sign of toxicity: stop it immediately, or the patient can go into multiple organ failure and die.
Summarize probenecid: drug class, action, and patient teaching.
A uricosuric agent that reduces serum uric acid by inhibiting its reabsorption; used alone or with allopurinol. Take with food and drink and plenty of fluids. Report kidney problems (hematuria, change in urine amount, weight gain) and any liver issues. It has lots of drug interactions, so monitor for them.
Define lupus, name the two kinds mentioned, and give the major autoantibody and how it is measured.
Lupus is an abnormal immune reaction of the body against its own cells: a particularly complex autoimmune disease that involves production of autoantibodies. A type affects only the skin; the lecture focuses on systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which is what people mean by "lupus" in the community. The major autoantibody is ANA (anti-nuclear antibody), and an ANA level can be drawn from plasma.
Explain how lupus damages organs, why it can affect so many, and where it is most common.
Autoantibodies (ANA) are activated against the body's own DNA and form immune complexes, which circulate and can target any major organ system (heart, skin, kidneys, brain), triggering an inflammatory response that destroys the tissue they landed in. There is DNA everywhere, so the complexes can land anywhere. The most common area is the kidney, when complexes deposit in the glomeruli, which are important and can be fairly fragile.
Who typically gets lupus?
People with an inherited genetic component; females much more than males; typically ages 20 to 40 (a younger disease); most commonly Black and African American people.
Give the hormone-related risk statistics for lupus and the other risk factors listed.
Women taking estrogen-containing oral contraceptives have a 50% higher risk; women who started menstruating before age 10 have double the risk. Other risk factors: sun exposure (an environmental trigger and predisposing factor), smoking (releases multiple toxins), and a relation to having a lot of allergies to certain antibiotics.
What are the most common symptom and hallmark signs of lupus?
Fatigue is the most common symptom (extreme and very debilitating). The butterfly rash is one of the hallmark signs: a bilateral, red, irritating rash that spreads across the face like butterfly wings. Others: photosensitivity (sensitivity to light and sunshine), edema (usually in the legs or around the eyes), fever, and unusual hair loss.
Describe the Raynaud's-type change in lupus and its treatment.
The distal small capillaries constrict and reduce blood flow; it can be very painful and is usually triggered by cold. It is treated with calcium channel blockers.
List the lupus manifestations by organ, and how often is the kidney involved?
Brain: headache, dizziness, seizures, stroke. Lungs: pleuritis, pleural effusion. Heart: myocarditis, endocarditis. Blood vessels: vasculitis. Blood: anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia. Joints: arthritis. Kidneys: about half of patients develop kidney complications, which can lead to permanent kidney damage and the need for dialysis or a kidney transplant (the lecture's example: Selena Gomez).
What is the prognosis of lupus, and what is its clinical course?
Severe multi-system (more than one organ) involvement carries a very poor prognosis; most people have mild disease with a good prognosis and survival outcomes with close monitoring and treatment. The course is exacerbations (flares) and remissions, and the systems usually are not all affected at the same time.
What are the warning signs of a lupus flare, and what are the triggers to avoid?
Warning signs: more severe fatigue, pain, headache, and generally more symptomatic with systemic symptoms. Triggers: sunlight exposure, infection, abruptly stopping a medicine, and stress. The key is to recognize the warning signs and avoid the triggers.
How are NSAIDs and steroids used in lupus?
NSAIDs (anti-inflammatories) for headache, musculoskeletal symptoms, pleuritis, and pericarditis. Steroids are often needed: high dose for severe kidney disease or a lot of CNS involvement, low dose for something like arthritis.
What does hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil) do in lupus, and what else is used for multi-organ disease?
It helps with a lot of the skin and musculoskeletal problems and can prevent kidney and CNS organ damage, so many lupus patients are on it. Biologics and immunosuppressives are used if there is multi-organ involvement or concern about progression.
How are RA and lupus alike, how do their immune complexes differ, and what is the major difference?
Both are autoimmune and systemic (multiple body systems can be involved). In RA the immune complexes were deposited only in the synovial fluid; in lupus they can deposit anywhere. The major difference: RA is really only in the joints (with systemic fatigue and malaise), while in lupus (SLE) any kind of organ can be involved.
