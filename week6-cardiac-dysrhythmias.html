Cardiac Dysrhythmias

This page covers the heart's ELECTRICAL system — the conduction problems and abnormal rhythms that show up on an EKG. Structural and pump problems (coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure) are a separate topic and are only mentioned here where the lecture names them as a cause of a particular rhythm.

Cross-reference: coronary artery disease, MI, and heart failure are covered in full on the Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure page

Cardiac Conduction Basics

Coordinated heartbeats depend on four core properties of cardiac cells.

PropertyWhat It Means
AutomaticityThe ability to spontaneously generate an electrical impulse.
ExcitabilityThe ability to respond to a stimulus.
ConductivityThe ability to transmit an impulse to neighboring cells.
ContractilityThe ability to contract in response to the impulse.

Depolarization & repolarization

  • Depolarization is the electrical event that triggers systole (contraction).
  • Repolarization is the cell "recharging" back to its resting state.
EKG WaveformWhat It Represents
P waveAtrial depolarization.
QRS complexVentricular depolarization.
T waveVentricular repolarization.
Atrial repolarizationNot visible on the EKG — it happens at the same time as ventricular depolarization and is buried inside the QRS complex.

Normal conduction pathway: SA node → AV node → bundle of His → Purkinje fibers. The isoelectric line is the flat baseline of the EKG seen between waveforms.

Normal sinus rhythm — the baseline every rhythm below is compared against

  • Rate: 60–100 bpm
  • Rhythm: regular
  • PR interval: 0.12–0.20 seconds
  • QRS complex: under 0.12 seconds (narrow)

A 6-second strip is a standard way to estimate heart rate directly off an EKG strip. On the strip, each small box represents 0.04 seconds.

MNEMONIC: read every strip with the same four questions, in order — (1) What's the rate? (2) Is it regular or irregular? (3) Is there a P wave, and does it look normal? (4) Is the QRS narrow or wide? Almost every rhythm on this page falls out of the pattern of answers to those four questions.

Three Mechanisms Behind Dysrhythmias

  • Inappropriate automaticity — a cell that shouldn't fire an impulse does so anyway; often from electrolyte imbalances (especially potassium, but also calcium or magnesium) or from a heart attack.
  • Triggered activity — an extra impulse generated during or just after repolarization; from things like digoxin toxicity, sympathetic nervous system overstimulation, or a genetic cause.
  • Re-entry — from myocardial ischemia or electrolyte imbalance, where an impulse abnormally re-circulates instead of dying out.

Narrow-QRS (Supraventricular) Rhythms

These rhythms all originate at or above the AV node, and every one of them keeps a narrow QRS (under 0.12 seconds) — the ventricles are still being depolarized through the normal His-Purkinje pathway, even when the P wave and rate are abnormal.

Sinus bradycardia — nursing approach

  • Symptoms of concern (from decreased cardiac output): lightheadedness/dizziness (especially with exertion), fatigue, syncope or near-syncope, dyspnea, chest pain/discomfort, confusion.
  • Some people (especially very fit patients) live with a naturally slower rate and are fine — treat only if the patient is symptomatic.
  • If severe: atropine or a pacemaker.

Sinus tachycardia — nursing approach

  • Fluid volume deficit is often one of the FIRST signs seen as sinus tachycardia — treat the underlying cause.
  • For cardiac causes: beta blockers such as metoprolol, carvedilol, or propranolol (see Cardiac Pharmacology).

PSVT — causes & symptoms

  • Onset and offset are sudden (paroxysmal), and the mechanism is re-entry.
  • Causes: overexertion, stimulants, digoxin toxicity, rheumatic or coronary heart disease, Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (which can precipitate PSVT via other rhythm abnormalities and right-sided heart failure).
  • Symptoms: palpitations, chest pain, fatigue, dizziness, dyspnea, and low blood pressure.

PACs: generally a benign finding on their own, but frequent PACs can signal an increased risk for developing atrial fibrillation — if a patient has frequent PACs, it's worth checking their electrolytes and oxygenation status.

Atrial flutter

  • Originates from the AV node, effectively overriding the SA node as the pacemaker; mechanism is re-entry.
  • Regular, sawtooth ("shark-tooth") flutter waves in place of P waves.
  • Atrial rate can be as high as 250 bpm, but the ventricular rate is slower because not every atrial impulse conducts through — described as a conduction ratio (2:1, 3:1, 4:1 = atrial beats : ventricular beats).
  • QRS stays narrow.

Atrial fibrillation

  • Multiple chaotic electrical foci fire within the atria at once, producing an "irregularly irregular" rhythm.
  • Ventricular rate is typically ~100–175 bpm; no discernible P wave, only fine fibrillation waves.
  • The atria quiver instead of contracting in a coordinated way, so the patient loses the "atrial kick" (the atria's contribution to ventricular filling) — this costs about 20% of cardiac output.
  • A key clinical question in managing AFib is whether the ventricular rate is controlled.

Atrial fibrillation — symptoms

  • Palpitations ("my heart is racing"), fatigue, dizziness, chest discomfort, shortness of breath.
  • How symptomatic a patient is depends on how sick the heart is and how much cardiac output they're getting — a rate of 170 is far more likely to be symptomatic than a rate of 110.
  • A large portion of patients with AFib are entirely asymptomatic and don't know they have it — AFib is the most common type of treated dysrhythmia overall, but plenty of cases go undiagnosed.

Atrial fibrillation — complications & treatment

  • Complications: heart failure, and embolus/stroke risk, including pulmonary emboli.
  • Untreated AFib roughly DOUBLES the risk of cardiac death and increases stroke risk 4- to 5-fold; AFib is present in an estimated 15–20% of all strokes.
  • Treatment has two parts: rate control (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, digitalis, or amiodarone) and stroke prevention (anticoagulants or antiplatelets) — see Cardiac Pharmacology for the drugs themselves.
  • Non-pharmacologic options like ablation or cardioversion are typically handled in more specialized (med-surg/higher-acuity) settings.
  • Atrial flutter is treated with a similar approach, though it carries a somewhat lower stroke risk than AFib.

Wide-QRS (Ventricular) Rhythms

Ventricular dysrhythmias are a higher-acuity/critical-care-level topic. This is also where the QRS first becomes WIDE — a key signal that the impulse is no longer traveling through the normal, fast His-Purkinje pathway.

PVCs — repeating patterns

  • PVCs originate from an ectopic focus in the ventricle.
  • Bigeminy: a PVC every other beat.
  • Trigeminy: a PVC every third beat.
  • Quadrigeminy: a PVC every fourth beat.
  • There is no direct drug treatment specifically for PVCs — the underlying cause is treated instead.
V-tach is a deadly rhythm. Defined as three or more PVCs in a row — an ectopic ventricular focus firing rapidly, regular but very fast, with no P wave present. Requires an IMMEDIATE pulse check: if the patient has a pulse, treatment follows the ACLS/antiarrhythmic pathway; if pulseless, start CPR immediately. A patient can have a palpable pulse with V-tach — sometimes called "stable V-tach," depending on the rate — but even then, "sustained" V-tach (lasting more than about 10–15 seconds) is treated as a rapid-response emergency, since a pulse with V-tach can turn pulseless at any point. Causes: MI, coronary artery disease, severe electrolyte abnormalities, heart failure, drug toxicity.
V-fib is a deadly rhythm. The ventricles quiver chaotically without any effective, coordinated contraction — there is no measurable heart rate or cardiac output. The nurse must check for a pulse and begin CPR immediately if the patient is pulseless. Caution: telemetry monitors can glitch and produce a false V-fib-looking waveform — always look at the actual patient and check a pulse yourself before treating what the monitor shows.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What are the four properties of cardiac cells?
Automaticity (spontaneously generate an impulse), excitability (respond to a stimulus), conductivity (transmit an impulse to neighboring cells), and contractility (contract in response).
What does the P wave, QRS complex, and T wave each represent on an EKG?
P wave = atrial depolarization. QRS complex = ventricular depolarization. T wave = ventricular repolarization. (Atrial repolarization isn't separately visible — it's buried inside the QRS complex.)
What is the normal conduction pathway through the heart?
SA node → AV node → bundle of His → Purkinje fibers.
What are the four criteria for normal sinus rhythm?
Rate 60–100 bpm, regular rhythm, PR interval 0.12–0.20 seconds, QRS under 0.12 seconds (narrow).
What distinguishes sinus arrhythmia from a true pathologic dysrhythmia?
It's a normal variant — the rate fluctuates in sync with respiration (faster on inhalation, slower on exhalation), it's common in younger people, and every other parameter (P wave, PR interval, QRS) is normal.
Name the three general mechanisms behind dysrhythmias, with an example cause of each.
Inappropriate automaticity (e.g., electrolyte imbalance, especially potassium); triggered activity (e.g., digoxin toxicity, sympathetic overstimulation); re-entry (e.g., myocardial ischemia, electrolyte imbalance).
What are the rate/rhythm/QRS findings in sinus bradycardia, and how is it managed?
Rate under 60 bpm, regular, normal PR interval, narrow QRS. Treat only if the patient is symptomatic; if severe, atropine or a pacemaker.
Sinus tachycardia is often one of the first signs of what condition?
Fluid volume deficit (dehydration).
How does PSVT differ from sinus tachycardia in rate and P wave?
PSVT runs faster (150–250 bpm vs. 100–150 for sinus tach), originates somewhere above the ventricles but not necessarily the SA node, has sudden (paroxysmal) onset/offset via a re-entry mechanism, and P waves are not discernible — the QRS stays narrow in both.
What is the EKG finding in a PAC, and what should you check if a patient has frequent PACs?
An early beat with an abnormal-looking P wave, but a normal PR interval and normal QRS. Frequent PACs raise the risk of developing atrial fibrillation, so check the patient's electrolytes and oxygenation status.
What is the characteristic waveform of atrial flutter, and where does it originate?
Regular, sawtooth ("shark-tooth") flutter waves in place of P waves; it originates from the AV node, overriding the SA node, via a re-entry mechanism.
What does a conduction ratio like "3:1" mean in atrial flutter?
For every 3 atrial impulses (flutter waves), only 1 is conducted through to the ventricles — this is why the ventricular rate is slower than the atrial rate, which can run as high as 250 bpm.
What replaces the P wave in atrial fibrillation, and how is the rhythm itself described?
Fine fibrillation waves replace the P wave (no discernible P wave); the rhythm is described as "irregularly irregular."
What is the "atrial kick," and what happens to it in AFib?
The atrial kick is the atria's contribution to ventricular filling. In AFib the atria quiver instead of contracting in a coordinated way, so the atrial kick is lost — costing about 20% of cardiac output.
How much does untreated AFib increase the risk of cardiac death and stroke?
Roughly doubles the risk of cardiac death and increases stroke risk 4- to 5-fold; AFib is present in an estimated 15–20% of all strokes.
What are the two parts of the treatment approach for AFib?
Rate control (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, digitalis, or amiodarone) and stroke prevention (anticoagulants or antiplatelets).
What is the single biggest cause of PVCs, and what EKG change makes them the first rhythm in this sequence with a wide QRS?
Electrolyte imbalance is the single biggest cause. PVCs originate from an ectopic ventricular focus, so the QRS is wide (over 0.12 seconds) instead of narrow — the first rhythm in the sequence where that happens.
Define bigeminy, trigeminy, and quadrigeminy.
Bigeminy = a PVC every other beat. Trigeminy = a PVC every third beat. Quadrigeminy = a PVC every fourth beat.
How is V-tach defined, and what is the immediate nursing action?
Three or more PVCs occurring in a row (an ectopic ventricular focus firing rapidly, regular but very fast, no P wave). Immediate pulse check: if the patient has a pulse, follow the ACLS/antiarrhythmic pathway; if pulseless, start CPR immediately. Sustained V-tach (over ~10–15 seconds) is a rapid-response emergency even with a pulse.
What happens hemodynamically in V-fib, and what must the nurse do?
The ventricles quiver chaotically with no effective, coordinated contraction — there is no measurable heart rate or cardiac output. The nurse must check for a pulse and begin CPR immediately if the patient is pulseless. Caution: telemetry can glitch and produce a false V-fib-looking waveform, so always look at the patient and check a pulse yourself before treating the monitor.
What symptoms suggest a symptomatic (concerning) sinus bradycardia?
Lightheadedness/dizziness (especially with exertion), fatigue, syncope or near-syncope, dyspnea, chest pain/discomfort, and confusion — all from decreased cardiac output. Some very fit patients simply run slow and have none of these.
What symptoms does a patient with atrial fibrillation typically report?
Palpitations ("heart is racing"), fatigue, dizziness, chest discomfort, and shortness of breath — though a large portion of AFib patients are entirely asymptomatic and don't know they have it.
Besides stroke, what other clot-related complication can AFib cause?
Pulmonary emboli — clots forming in the quivering, poorly-emptying atria can travel anywhere, not just to the brain.
What is "stable V-tach"?
V-tach where the patient still has a palpable pulse, depending on the rate — but even a patient with a pulse in sustained V-tach (>10–15 seconds) needs an immediate rapid-response response, since a pulse with V-tach can turn pulseless at any point.
