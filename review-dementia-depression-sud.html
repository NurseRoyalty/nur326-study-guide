Dementia, Depression & SUD

NUR 325 review, covering: delirium and dementia (Alzheimer's, vascular, frontotemporal) and dementia pharmacology, depression and anxiety pathophysiology (panic, GAD, PTSD, social anxiety, OCD), depression/anxiety pharmacology (SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs, atypical antidepressants, benzodiazepines), and substance use disorder pathophysiology, treatment, and withdrawal.

Delirium — Hyperactive vs. Hypoactive

Delirium is a transient disorder of cognitive function, consciousness, and perception — onset can be sudden or gradual. Causes include drug/alcohol intoxication or withdrawal, hypoglycemia and other metabolic derangements (liver/kidney dysfunction), brain trauma or surgery, post-anesthesia states, and dehydration/electrolyte imbalance.

TypeCause / Risk FactorsSigns & Symptoms
Hyperactive DeliriumAutonomic nervous system overactivity; develops over 2–3 days. Seen in the ICU, post-surgery, during withdrawal, and in hospitalized elderly patients. Risk factors: benzodiazepines, narcotics, acute infection/sepsis, surgery, hypoxia, electrolyte/metabolic dysfunction, insomnia.Restless, irritable, poor concentration, insomnia, shaking, poor appetite, hallucinations, inattentive, dilated pupils, tachycardia, diaphoresis, elevated temperature. Can progress to excited delirium syndrome — combative, aggressive, rapid breathing, which can cause seizures or death. Typically resolves in 2–3 days but can persist weeks.
Hypoactive DeliriumRight-sided frontal/basal ganglion disruption; more common with metabolic disorders (liver or kidney failure).Decreased alertness, attention span, and perception; forgetful, apathetic, slow speech, frequent sleep. Easy to miss since the patient isn't combative.

Delirium vs. Dementia & Delirium Treatment

DeliriumDementia
OnsetAcute — hours to days.Chronic — slow, progressive over years.
CourseUsually reversible once the cause is treated.Irreversible once symptoms present; no cure.
Typical SettingHospitalized, often elderly, patients.Long-term, community or care-facility living.

Dementia patients are at higher risk for delirium, so consistent routines are crucial for a dementia patient to help prevent it. Delirium treatment centers on identifying and removing the cause and risk factors — using the lowest dose and shortest duration of opioids necessary, treating kidney/liver failure (e.g., dialysis), and removing causative medications. Prevention means avoiding CNS-altering medications, allowing normal sleep, and encouraging normal routines. Antipsychotics (Haldol, Seroquel — IV or PO) are reserved for when the patient is a danger to themselves or staff, and only short-term. If symptoms persist after the acute situation resolves (e.g., after discharge), the patient should be evaluated for another condition — at that point, it's no longer delirium.

Dementia — Pathophysiology & Types

Dementia is an acquired deterioration and progressive failure of cerebral functioning — impaired intellectual processes, memory, language, judgment, orientation, and decision-making. Patients are often not cognitively aware of their own decline; it's usually family or caregivers who report it. Dementia presents as agitation, wandering, frustration, and aggression.

Pathophysiology involves neuron degeneration, brain tissue compression, atherosclerosis of cerebral vessels, brain trauma, infections, and inflammation, and CNS infections (HIV, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease) causing atrophy — plus a genetic predisposition. Onset is generally slow, symptoms are irreversible once present, and there is no cure — treatment focuses on optimizing the patient's remaining functional capacity.

Types of Dementia

  • Dementia is an umbrella term — mixed dementia (more than one type at once) is common.
  • Alzheimer's disease — the leading cause of severe cognitive dysfunction in the elderly (detailed next).
  • Vascular dementia — the second most common type (detailed next).
  • Lewy body dementia — named as a type in this lecture but not detailed here.
  • Frontotemporal dementia — formerly Pick disease (detailed next).

Alzheimer Disease

Alzheimer disease affects about 6 million people in the U.S., about two-thirds of them women. The cause is unknown. Early-onset Alzheimer's has a more common genetic link; late-onset (sporadic) Alzheimer's is far more common but has no specific identified genetic link, despite showing the same cellular changes. Diagnosis is made by ruling out other conditions (brain tumor, vascular injury) plus mental status exams and brain imaging.

Risk Factors

  • Age over 65
  • Family history
  • Existing mild cognitive impairment
  • Down syndrome
  • Unhealthy lifestyle
  • Head trauma
  • Social isolation

Pathophysiology

  • Neuritic/amyloid plaques build up between neurons, and neurofibrillary tangles of tau protein accelerate degeneration.
  • Changes are centered in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus — the brain's memory areas.
  • Loss of synapses, acetylcholine, and other neurotransmitters drives memory/cognitive decline and brain atrophy.

These brain changes occur decades before symptoms appear. Initial symptoms are subtle — progressive forgetfulness. Progression is variable, and the disease ends in a non-verbal, vegetative state, then death.

Dementia Medications — Donepezil & Memantine

Alzheimer's medications target three problems: decreased acetylcholine, the buildup of plaques and tangles, and excess NMDA receptor stimulation.

DrugMechanism & UseSide Effects & Nursing Considerations
DonepezilA cholinesterase inhibitor — increases acetylcholine by inhibiting the enzyme (acetylcholinesterase) that breaks it down. Used for mild-moderate Alzheimer's.Mild side effects overall — GI (ulcer/GI bleed risk, avoid NSAIDs) and cardiovascular (bradycardia, reflex tachycardia, syncope — resolves with titration/tolerance). Give at bedtime. Nursing consideration: use adherence packaging (bubble packs/pill organizers), since these patients are forgetful.
MemantineThe only NMDA receptor antagonist on the market — blocks excess glutamate/NMDA stimulation, decreases calcium influx, and normalizes nerve transmission to prevent further neuronal damage. Used for moderate-severe Alzheimer's; can be combined with a cholinesterase inhibitor.Confusion, hypotension, headache, dizziness — most common side effect is constipation (advise fiber/stool softeners). Well tolerated; PO with or without food. The extended-release version is dosed once daily (vs. twice daily for the original).

Depression Pathophysiology

Depression affects more than 17 million people in the U.S. — about twice as common in women (men underreport it) — and is more common in white people and people who have never married. It's associated with cardiovascular disease (a 64% greater risk of coronary artery disease), is a leading cause of disability worldwide, and carries a high suicide risk. Etiology is complex — genetic, biological, environmental, and psychological factors combined.

Types

  • Major depressive disorder
  • Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)
  • Premenstrual dysphoric disorder
  • Substance/medication-induced
  • Due to another medical condition
  • Postpartum

Symptoms

  • Anhedonia
  • Fatigue or restlessness/irritability
  • Poor concentration
  • Low self-esteem, negative thinking
  • Sleep disturbances (either direction)
  • Appetite disturbances (either direction)

Diagnosis requires multiple symptoms, intense and persistent enough to impair psychosocial functioning. Treatment combines medications, psychotherapy/CBT, psychoeducation/support groups, and — for refractory cases — brain stimulation therapy; combination treatment works best. Medications take 4–8 weeks for full effect, a key patient-education point covered in more detail in the pharmacology sections below.

Panic Disorder & Generalized Anxiety Disorder

There are many distinct types of anxiety disorder, each treated differently. Panic disorder involves two psychological symptoms: anticipatory anxiety (fearful expectation that panic will start) and avoidance anxiety (personal strategies used to increase control and decrease panic risk). Etiology is bio/psycho/physiologic — genetics, family history, and childhood stress all play a role; stress hormones stimulate excess glutamate. A panic attack is an unexpected episode disproportionate to what's happening around the patient, and it can spiral into a panic cycle: anxiety → attack → fear of another attack → more anxiety → repeat.

Signs & Symptoms / FeaturesTreatment
Panic DisorderPalpitations, chest pain, smothering feeling, dizziness, nausea, fear of losing control, tingling, hot/cold flushes.1st line: CBT + antidepressants (SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs/MAOIs). 2nd line: benzodiazepines, for breakthrough only — not ongoing treatment.
Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)Chronic, lasting more than 6 months — excessive, uncontrolled, unrealistic worry, muscle tension, autonomic hyperactivity, exaggerated startle, poor concentration. Risk factors: substance use, genetics, childhood abuse/trauma.CBT, antidepressants (SNRIs/SSRIs), buspirone (unknown mechanism, no sedative effect, no abuse potential, doesn't intensify CNS depressants — but slow onset, not for breakthrough use), and benzodiazepines for short-term use only.

Vivid flashbacks, nightmares/night terrors, emotional blunting, irritability, exaggerated startle, memory/sleep/depression issues. SSRIs & SNRIs

Despite the name, antidepressants treat both depression and anxiety. SSRIs and SNRIs are used first-line because they're the safest class. All antidepressants carry a suicide risk that must be monitored before, during, and throughout treatment, and none work right away — full efficacy takes 4–8 weeks, a critical patient-teaching point. If a medication isn't working after that window, options are increasing the dose, switching to a different drug in the same class, switching drug class, or adding a second drug.

ClassMechanism & Exam DrugAdverse Effects
SSRIsBlock serotonin reuptake at the presynaptic nerve ending, leaving more serotonin available in the synapse. Exam drug: fluoxetine (Prozac). Over 20 SSRIs exist (all second-generation); no common suffix. Therapeutic benefit takes about 3–4 weeks. Don't combine with MAOIs.Weight gain; GI (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, dry mouth); CNS (headache, nervousness, insomnia); sexual dysfunction. Small risk of neonatal pulmonary hypertension/neonatal abstinence syndrome in pregnancy.
SNRIsBlock reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine. Exam drug: venlafaxine (Effexor). No common suffix; contraindicated with MAOIs.Similar to SSRIs, plus sweating, blurred vision, and increased LFTs — watch liver function tests especially with duloxetine (Cymbalta).
Serotonin syndrome: risk increases when a patient takes two serotonergic drugs together (especially an SSRI/SNRI with an MAOI). Symptoms — including altered mental status and confusion — appear 2–72 hours after the interacting dose. Always confirm a patient isn't on another serotonergic medication before starting or continuing an SSRI/SNRI.

Abruptly stopping an SSRI or SNRI can cause withdrawal syndrome: dizziness, headache, sensory disturbances (patients may describe their hands or feet as "on fire"), shaking, tremors, anxiety, and dysphoria. This is a major teaching point — patients who want to stop, or who can't afford/access refills, need a tapering plan with their provider instead of stopping cold.

TCAs & MAOIs

Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and MAOIs are older drug classes, prescribed less often than SSRIs/SNRIs because of more intense interactions and side effects.

TCA anticholinergic effects mnemonic: "hot as a hare" (overheated), "dry as a bone" (dry mouth/eyes/secretions), "blind as a bat" (blurry vision), "red as a beet" (flushed), and "mad as a hatter" (confusion).
ClassMechanism, Exam Drug & UseAdverse Effects
TCAsBlocks reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin. Exam drug: amitriptyline (Elavil). Used for 50+ years — good efficacy, tolerable side effects, inexpensive; also used for neuropathic pain, nocturnal enuresis, and TMJ.The anticholinergic effects above, plus sedation, orthostatic hypotension, sexual dysfunction, and cardiac toxicity. TCA + MAOI together can cause a hypertensive crisis.
MAOIsInhibits the monoamine oxidase enzyme that normally converts norepinephrine, serotonin, and dopamine to inactive products — making more of each available. Exam drug: phenelzine (Nardil). Used less often, generally for refractory or atypical depression. Also causes CNS stimulation and orthostatic hypotension, plus significant drug-drug interactions (antihypertensives, SSRIs, ephedrine, meperidine).See the hypertensive crisis callout below. Rapid blood pressure changes from these interactions can also cause stroke or coma.
TCA overdose can be fatal. This is a major concern precisely because TCAs are prescribed to patients with depression/anxiety who already carry a higher suicide risk — and the medication itself can sometimes increase suicidal thoughts. Screen for suicide risk before starting a TCA and monitor throughout treatment.
MAOI + tyramine-rich foods → hypertensive crisis — the most serious MAOI risk. Tyramine is found in aged cheese, smoked meats, yeast/bread, and red wine; patients on an MAOI must avoid these entirely. The same hypertensive crisis risk applies to MAOI + TCA and MAOI + meperidine combinations.

Substance Use Disorder — Pathophysiology, Risk & Protective Factors

Substance use disorder (SUD) is a disease — a complex interaction of environmental, biological, genetic, and brain-chemistry factors. It is explicitly not a personal failure or weakness, despite that being a common and outdated misconception.

Dopamine drives the reward pathway: substance use causes a large, rapid dopamine surge that greatly exceeds the normal pleasure response. Over time, the brain's normal dopamine pathways undergo adaptive downregulation, requiring more frequent and larger use to achieve the same effect. Addiction is also linked to prefrontal cortex changes. Brain imaging of dopamine transporters shows these changes are reversible with abstinence — for example, methamphetamine users show low dopamine transporter activity after 1 month abstinent, with activity returning toward normal after 14 months abstinent.

Risk Factors

  • Aggressive childhood behavior
  • Lack of parental supervision
  • Poor social skills
  • Drug experimentation
  • Drug availability at school
  • Community poverty

Protective Factors

  • Good self-control
  • Parental monitoring/support
  • Positive relationships
  • Good grades
  • School anti-drug policies
  • Neighborhood resources

These risk and protective factors are proxies for larger social determinants of health. Addiction risk also depends on the substance itself: type, route of administration, effect, age of first use, availability, and cost.

SUD Treatment, Screening & Medication-Assisted Treatment

Treatment is highly individualized and therapy-centered — CBT, contingency management, motivational enhancement, family therapy, and 12-step programs — alongside pharmacological interventions, addressing the whole person rather than the substance use alone. Medically assisted detoxification ("rehab") is a structured, medically supervised withdrawal process; not all rehabs are medically assisted, but it's encouraged since it manages potentially life-threatening withdrawal symptoms. Patients entering treatment should be screened for bloodborne pathogens (HIV/AIDS, hepatitis B/C) and tuberculosis due to needle-sharing risk, and for endocarditis, which is common with long-term IV drug use.

Withdrawal Syndromes — Opioids vs. Benzodiazepines/Alcohol

Withdrawal timelines are explicitly testable. Opioid withdrawal (including heroin) begins about 12–24 hours after the last dose, peaks around 72 hours, and lasts 5–10 days. It feels miserable — flu-like symptoms including nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, diarrhea, goosebumps, depression, drug cravings, anxiety, sweating, muscle aches, and fever — but it is not life-threatening. Treatment is supportive care (treating each symptom) plus medication-assisted treatment.

Benzodiazepine and alcohol withdrawal both act on GABA receptors and share similar signs, symptoms, and treatment. Non-dangerous symptoms: sleep disturbances, dry retching, irritability, nausea, increased tension, weight loss, anxiety, palpitations, panic attacks, headache, muscle aches, sweating, poor concentration, and perceptual changes.

Unlike opioid withdrawal, benzodiazepine and alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening. Dangerous symptoms include seizures, hallucinations, delirium tremens (DTs) — hallucination, disorientation, tachycardia, hypertension, fever, agitation, and diaphoresis — and Wernicke's encephalopathy, with its three hallmark traits: confusion/disorientation/attention issues, ocular motor dysfunction (nystagmus, lateral rectus palsy, conjugate gaze palsy), and gait ataxia. Treatment for both is a benzodiazepine taper (prevents seizures, decreases DT severity) plus 12-step/therapy support. Alcohol withdrawal specifically requires thiamine (vitamin B1) supplementation — chronic alcohol-related stomach inflammation impairs vitamin absorption, and thiamine deficiency is what causes Wernicke's encephalopathy.

How do hyperactive and hypoactive delirium differ?
Hyperactive delirium involves autonomic nervous system overactivity — restless, agitated, hallucinating, tachycardic, can progress to excited delirium syndrome (combative, can cause seizures/death). Hypoactive delirium involves right-sided frontal/basal ganglion disruption, more common with liver/kidney failure — decreased alertness, apathetic, slow speech, easy to miss since the patient isn't combative.
What's the key difference between delirium and dementia?
Delirium is acute (hours to days) and usually reversible once the cause is treated. Dementia is chronic, slowly progressive over years, and irreversible once symptoms present. Dementia patients are at higher risk for delirium, so consistent routines help prevent it.
What two brain changes define Alzheimer disease pathophysiology, and where are they centered?
Neuritic/amyloid plaques between neurons and neurofibrillary tangles of tau protein, centered in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus (the brain's memory areas) — driving loss of synapses, acetylcholine, and other neurotransmitters, then brain atrophy.
How do donepezil and memantine differ in mechanism and in which stage of Alzheimer's they treat?
Donepezil is a cholinesterase inhibitor (increases acetylcholine) used for mild-moderate Alzheimer's. Memantine is the only NMDA receptor antagonist on the market (blocks excess glutamate/calcium influx) used for moderate-severe Alzheimer's — the two can be combined.
Match each neurotransmitter to what a deficit or imbalance causes: dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin, GABA.
Dopamine deficit → Parkinson's-like symptoms, apathy, anhedonia. Norepinephrine — high causes anxiety/hyperactivity, low causes lack of focus/energy. Serotonin deficit → OCD-like symptoms, impulsivity, suicide risk, aggression. GABA (the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter) dysfunction is linked to anxiety/panic disorder and major depressive disorder.
What's the panic cycle, and what are anticipatory vs. avoidance anxiety?
Anticipatory anxiety is fearful expectation that panic will start; avoidance anxiety is using personal strategies to increase control and decrease panic risk. The panic cycle: anxiety → panic attack → fear of another attack → more anxiety → repeat.
Recite the TCA anticholinergic side-effect mnemonic and what each phrase means.
"Hot as a hare" (overheated), "dry as a bone" (dry mouth/eyes/secretions), "blind as a bat" (blurry vision), "red as a beet" (flushed), "mad as a hatter" (confusion).
What is the most serious risk with MAOIs, and what specifically triggers it?
A hypertensive crisis, triggered by eating tyramine-rich foods (aged cheese, smoked meats, yeast/bread, red wine) while on an MAOI. The same crisis risk applies to MAOI + TCA and MAOI + meperidine combinations. Rapid BP swings can also cause stroke or coma.
A patient abruptly stops their SSRI. What happens, and what should have been done instead?
Withdrawal syndrome: dizziness, headache, sensory disturbances (hands/feet feeling "on fire"), shaking, tremors, anxiety, dysphoria. The patient should have tapered off with their provider instead of stopping cold — a key teaching point for any patient who wants to stop or can't access refills.
What's the antidote for benzodiazepine overdose, and what must never be combined with a benzodiazepine?
Flumazenil is the antidote. Never combine a benzodiazepine with another CNS depressant — Benadryl, alcohol, opioids, or barbiturates — due to the risk of fatal respiratory depression.
How do methadone and buprenorphine/naloxone (Suboxone) differ in mechanism?
Methadone is a full synthetic opioid analgesic and mu agonist — it occupies opioid receptors to reduce cravings/withdrawal. Suboxone combines buprenorphine (a partial opioid agonist, alone called Subutex) with naloxone (an opioid antagonist) — the agonist+antagonist combination has lower abuse potential than methadone or buprenorphine alone.
Why is benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal more dangerous than opioid withdrawal, and what prevents the worst outcomes?
Opioid withdrawal is miserable (flu-like symptoms) but not life-threatening. Benzodiazepine and alcohol withdrawal CAN be life-threatening — seizures, hallucinations, delirium tremens, and Wernicke's encephalopathy. Both are treated with a benzodiazepine taper (prevents seizures, reduces DT severity); alcohol withdrawal additionally requires thiamine supplementation to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy.
