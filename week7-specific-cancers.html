Lung, Breast, Cervical & Colorectal Cancer

This page covers the lecture's four "specific cancers," in the order taught. It begins with lung cancer: why it matters in Kentucky, late diagnosis and survival, disparities, etiology, pathogenesis, the two types, and signs and symptoms. It then covers breast cancer (statistics, disparities, risk factors, DES, BRCA genes, and genetic counseling), cervical cancer (prevention, risk factors, and the HPV vaccine), and colorectal cancer (risk factors, polyps, symptoms, and screening).

Big Picture: Why Lung Cancer Matters

The instructor opens the specific-cancer section with some context before turning to lung cancer.

Context for the "specific cancers" section

  • Historically these were the four cancers with the highest prevalence (lung, breast, cervical, and colorectal), but through advancement they are not seen quite as much now, especially cervical cancer. Cervical cancer is still mentioned, along with a little about the HPV vaccine.
  • The cancer with the highest prevalence is non-melanoma skin cancer. Skin cancer was covered in NUR 325, and the instructor clarifies that the skin cancer she means is non-melanoma skin cancer, "just to clarify that."
  • This part of the lecture is "really focused on specific types of cancers that we see here in Kentucky."
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States, and that is also true in Kentucky.

Late Diagnosis & Survival

The central problem in lung cancer (and in cancer in general) is that most patients do not present until the cancer is at an advanced or metastatic stage.

Why lung cancer is caught late

  • Most patients do not present with cancer, especially lung cancer, until they are at an advanced or metastatic stage.
  • The lecture says exact percentages vary slightly depending on study and region, but it is "pretty well documented" that most lung cancer cases are diagnosed at a late stage, often when the disease has already spread to distant sites.
  • Two reasons are given: a lack of symptoms in the early stages, and limited screening uptake in the communities.
  • Early diagnosis and detection significantly improve survival rates.

Five-year survival by stage at diagnosis

Stage when caughtFive-year survival rate (as stated in lecture)
Stage 1Over 60%
Stage 4Drops below 10%

Screening

  • Screening is done with low-dose CT scans.
  • The lecture calls this "very effective for early detection."

Age

  • The average age of diagnosis in the United States is around 70.
  • So lung cancer is often seen in patients over 65.

Racial, Ethnic & Socioeconomic Disparities

Exam note from the instructor: "You don't need to memorize these for the exams," but it is important for healthcare providers to recognize the disparities. The instructor notes that many chronic conditions show racial and ethnic disparities, and that these are very well studied and prevalent in cancer. Significant disparities are present in lung cancer diagnoses and survival.
GroupDisparity stated in lecture

Black womenLower incidence than white women.
Black Americans overallMore likely to be diagnosed at later stages, receive less treatment, and have lower survival rates.
Other ethnic groupsThis type of inequality in lung cancer diagnosis and treatment is seen in almost every ethnic group, including Hispanic and Asian groups.

Contributing factors the lecture names

  • Healthcare access: studies have linked the disparities to healthcare access. People of color are more likely to be uninsured, which affects diagnosis, screening, and treatment.
  • Socioeconomic factors: poverty, education level, and rural residence contribute to worse outcomes. The instructor says this is seen significantly in Kentucky data.
  • Rural Kentucky: a large portion of the Kentucky population is rural, which limits access to screening. If a patient needs a CT scan, the question is whether there is a CT scanner, a radiologist, and an oncologist available to do this at the rates needed.
  • Systemic issues: biases in healthcare delivery and underrepresentation in clinical trials.

Etiology (Causes)

The instructor reminds you that etiology means cause.

Cigarette smoking: the leading cause

Smoking and lung cancer

  • The leading cause of lung cancer is cigarette smoking, "to no one's surprise."
  • 80 to 90% of patients with lung cancer are current or former smokers.
  • Risk increases with the total number of cigarettes smoked. This is expressed as a cigarette pack-year.
  • Cigarette smoke is both a promoter and a known carcinogen: it causes cancer, and it promotes proliferation of mutated genes that continue to promote the development of cancer.

Pack-year example from the lecture

  • A 60-year-old who smokes two packs per day and started at age 20 has an 80-pack-year smoking history.
  • You may hear and see pack-years in practice, and there are calculators that do this.

Cross-reference: carcinogens versus promoters in general are taught in Cancer Fundamentals, Metastasis & Etiology

E-cigarettes and vaping

  • Smoking rates in the country are going down, "which is great," but "we are seeing" e-cigarette and vape use.
  • The instructor reiterates that these "are not good for you."
  • There are no good studies to say that vaping increases the risk of lung cancer. There is some data to suggest it is a little less carcinogenic than cigarette smoking, but "that data is not great right now."
  • The instructor gets a lot of questions about vaping and e-cigarettes and says she does not have the answers. It is being actively studied.

Radon prevention

  • This combination is a really big reason for pushes to have radon testing of new homes and buildings, so that people know how much radon exposure they are getting.
  • There are radon mitigation systems that can be used.

Nursing point: take a thorough health history

  • If you work in the oncology or preventative space, it is important to obtain really good health histories: where the patient works and what their exposures are.
  • The instructor sees this quite a bit in Kentucky when thinking about carcinogenic chemical exposures, because of the high rate of blue-collar workers in the state.
  • Early diagnosis is "definitely key to effective treatment," although most patients are not diagnosed until the later stage.

Pathogenesis

There are many known causes of lung cancer (smoking and other environmental toxins), and the instructor says we have a pretty decent understanding of what happens, especially with smoking. She calls this one of the best-understood cancers (along with cervical cancer): it is not a nice process, but knowing it explains why we want to avoid these carcinogens and lets us educate our populations.

Step-by-step: how lung cancer develops

StepWhat happens
1. Cilia are paralyzedSmoke and toxins paralyze the cilia along the respiratory tract. Normally the cilia sweep away carcinogens and bacteria.
2. Carcinogens accumulateThe cilia become less effective, causing an accumulation of carcinogens along the airway tract.
3. Overload plus geneticsThe pathogenesis of lung cancer involves an overload of carcinogens along the airway from smoking and environmental toxins, and then usually some kind of genetic predisposition acquired from your parents.
4. Dysplasia and mutationsLung cancer commonly begins in an area of dysplasia. Wherever the damage from carcinogens occurs along the airway tract, that is usually where the initial mutations are seen. It becomes a thickened area of the bronchial mucosa.
5. Proliferation and erosionThe lesion proliferates and can erode the lining of the epithelium.
6. GrowthIt grows into the bronchial lumen or becomes a mass within the lung tissue. The cancer destroys and invades surrounding tissues.
Key point stressed twice: smoke and other environmental toxins paralyze the cilia of the respiratory tract. The instructor repeats this and says it is important to recognize.

Cross-reference: dysplasia as a cellular change is covered in Cellular Adaptation, Infection & Immunity

Types of Lung Cancer

Exam scope from the instructor: "You don't really need to know specifics about types of cancer." This lecture stays in broad generalities. The only thing to recognize is that there are two types, non-small cell and small cell, and that non-small cell is more common. "I'm not going to ask you any questions specific" beyond these two types. (If you go into oncology, recognizing the different types is very important.)

Non-small cell lung cancer

  • Makes up about 85 to 90% of all lung cancers.
  • The more common type.
  • Tends to grow slower than small cell lung cancer.
  • Growth rates can vary depending on the subtype, such as adenocarcinoma versus squamous cell carcinoma.

Small cell lung cancer

  • Tends to grow a little more rapidly.
  • Metastasizes quicker.
  • Generally responds well to initial treatment, but tends to relapse quicker.

Breast Cancer

Overview & Statistics

  • Breast cancer is very prevalent and common in the United States.
  • It does affect men and women, but it is much more likely to affect women, due to the increased amount of breast tissue that women tend to have.
  • One in eight women in the US will be diagnosed with breast cancer at some point in their lifetime.
  • It is the most common cancer in the United States. The instructor's comparison: lung cancer tends to kill you sooner, but breast cancer is the most common as of now, outside of the skin cancer already mentioned in lecture.

DES (Diethylstilbestrol)

  • DES was a drug given to some pregnant women in the United States between 1940 and 1971 to prevent miscarriage.
  • Later it was found that women who took DES have a higher risk of getting breast cancer.
  • Why it still matters: we are in the era where women who took DES would be in their late 60s, 70s, and 80s, or the people who were pregnant might be in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, so we need to keep an eye on that.
  • DES use is also linked to cervical cancer (see the cervical cancer section).

BRCA1 and BRCA2 Genes

What they are

  • BRCA1 and BRCA2 are genes; mutations in the genetic code are what carry the risk.
  • People with BRCA mutations have a much higher risk of breast cancer — a known genetic link.
  • Other genes are also connected to breast cancer, but BRCA1 and BRCA2 are the most common and well studied.
  • We all have these genes, but we can test for their mutations.

Cancers linked to BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations

  • Breast cancer
  • Ovarian cancer
  • Colon cancer
  • Pancreatic cancer
  • In males with the mutations: an increased risk of prostate cancer

Genetic Counseling, Subtypes & Prevention

  • A lot of cancers now get genetic counseling, and breast cancer is one of them.
  • There are many different tags and markers of breast cancer. Triple negative was mentioned in the disparities discussion. The instructor does not go into the different types of breast cancer; just know that they exist.
  • If you go into oncology you need to spend time in the literature to learn which cancers are common and which treatments are expected with certain types, including whether radiation versus chemo is given. This is covered a little more in med-surg, and you will probably learn more once you are in the field.
  • If there is a family history, providers often test for the BRCA mutations early.
  • Women who find they have these mutations often get a preventative mastectomy or an oophorectomy (removal of the ovaries, so you do not get ovarian cancer). The instructor says this is pretty common and that more and more genetic counseling is being done for these kinds of things.

Cervical Cancer

Overview & Prevention

  • Cervical cancer used to be much more prevalent, in the 80s, 90s, and early 2000s.
  • Its prevalence has really decreased because of screening with Pap smears and the HPV vaccine.
  • Essentially, cervical cancer can be prevented with the HPV vaccine and frequent screenings.
  • Like skin cancer, Pap smears can detect early cancer mutations so they can be found sooner.
  • The HPV vaccine prevents human papillomavirus (HPV), which is a huge risk for cervical cancer development. By getting the vaccine you prevent HPV and significantly decrease cervical cancer rates.
  • Almost 100% of cervical cancer patients test positive for HPV, so cervical cancer can be prevented by preventing HPV through the HPV vaccine.

Colorectal Cancer

Overview & Statistics

  • Colorectal cancer begins anywhere along the colon or the rectum.
  • It is the fourth most common cancer in men and women.
  • It is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths.
  • Males have much higher rates of both incidence and dying from colorectal cancer than females. The instructor says this could be related to some of the risk factors.

How genetic syndromes get picked up (instructor's example)

  • The instructor describes a friend from nursing school whose dad had colorectal cancer very early. He had this kind of genetic syndrome, so all of his children were tested, and they do have it.
  • Because of that, those children have to have colonoscopies every two years even though they are very young.
  • The instructor's point: this is generally how these syndromes are picked up — "how would I even know that?" — through family history and testing of relatives.

Polyps

Screening for colorectal cancer is significant, and what the screening is often looking for is polyps.

  • Polyps are non-cancerous growths that can develop into colorectal cancer.
  • They are usually found during routine screening colonoscopies or flexible sigmoidoscopies — either one.
  • They are usually asymptomatic: most polyps do not cause any symptoms.
  • Some may present with rectal bleeding, changes in bowel habits, or abdominal pain, but generally polyps do not cause symptoms, so a lot of the time they are only picked up through screening.
  • You may see the polyp type on a report from a colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy.

Adenomatous polyps

  • The most common type of polyp.
  • Have a higher potential to develop into cancer.

Hyperplastic polyps

  • Less common.
  • Typically do not turn into cancer.

Symptoms of Colorectal Cancer

As with many cancers covered in this unit, the symptoms are nonspecific.

  • Changes in bowel habits.
  • Blood in the stool.
  • Diarrhea.
  • Constipation.
  • Feeling that the bowel does not empty all the way — usually more of a rectal cancer symptom. Diarrhea and constipation can be from colon or rectal cancer.
  • Abdominal pain, aches, or cramping that does not go away.
  • Weight loss when you do not know why.

The instructor's practical message: as long as you are peeing, pooping, eating, and drinking you are probably okay, but if you notice significant changes in these along with some of the other symptoms, you definitely need to be checked out. The first thing they will probably do is give you a colonoscopy or a sigmoidoscopy.

Screening

The instructor says you will learn more about screening in med-surg and includes it here because it is important enough to reiterate. Screening depends on your risk.

Instructor's example: why family history and genetics matter

  • The instructor's husband had a bunch of GI issues and had a colonoscopy in his early 30s, and they found a lot of polyps. He does not have colorectal cancer, but he is at higher risk, so he has to have more frequent colonoscopies.
  • He is one of four siblings, so all of his siblings were encouraged to get screened earlier than 45, so that higher risk could be determined in the family.
  • The instructor's point: genetics are obviously going to be a component, because the instructor's husband is very healthy — not sedentary, has a good BMI, and eats pretty healthy. Those kinds of things can change the risk picture a little bit.

What is the leading cause of lung cancer, how does smoke act, and what is a pack-year?
Cigarette smoking; 80 to 90% of lung cancer patients are current or former smokers. Risk increases with the total number of cigarettes smoked, expressed as a pack-year. Example: a 60-year-old who smokes two packs per day and started at age 20 has an 80-pack-year smoking history.
What does the lecture say about e-cigarettes and vaping and lung cancer risk?
They are not good for you, but there are no good studies showing they increase lung cancer risk. Some data suggest they are a little less carcinogenic than cigarette smoking, but that data is not great. The instructor does not have the answers; it is being actively studied.
How much does passive smoke raise lung cancer risk, and why does COPD raise it?
Consistent exposure to passive smoke increases risk by 20 to 30%, which is the big reason for smoke-free laws. With COPD (listed as asthma, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema) the lungs are in a chronic inflamed state, which acts as a promoter and can cause lung cancer.
Why did the instructor emphasize radon, and what is done about it?
Radon is a chemical that comes from the ground, high levels are very common in Kentucky, and it is a known promoter and carcinogen. Radon and smoking together have a compounding effect, a very dangerous combination. This drives pushes for radon testing of new homes and buildings, and radon mitigation systems can be used.
Besides smoking, passive smoke, COPD, and radon, what other causes of lung cancer were listed, and what should a nurse obtain from patients?
Asbestos (seen in construction workers and in buildings that still have it), arsenic exposure, genetic predisposition, and a history of radiation therapy to the chest (also seen with breast cancer). A nurse in oncology or prevention should obtain really good health histories, including where the patient works and what their exposures are, since Kentucky has a high rate of blue-collar workers.
What is the role of the cilia in lung cancer pathogenesis?
Normally the cilia of the respiratory tract sweep away carcinogens and bacteria. Smoke and environmental toxins paralyze the cilia (a point the instructor stressed twice), so they become less effective and carcinogens accumulate along the airway tract.
Describe how a lung cancer lesion forms and grows after carcinogens overload the airway.
With carcinogen overload plus usually some genetic predisposition from your parents, lung cancer commonly begins in an area of dysplasia, where the initial mutations occur and the bronchial mucosa thickens. The lesion proliferates and can erode the epithelial lining, then grows into the bronchial lumen or becomes a mass within the lung tissue, destroying and invading surrounding tissue.
What are the two types of lung cancer to recognize, and how do they compare?
Non-small cell (the more common type, about 85 to 90% of lung cancers) and small cell. Non-small cell tends to grow slower, with rates varying by subtype (adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma). Small cell grows a little more rapidly and metastasizes quicker; it generally responds well to initial treatment but relapses quicker.
List the signs and symptoms of lung cancer from lecture, and explain why they are hard to interpret.
A cough that does not go away or gets worse; coughing up blood or rust-colored sputum; chest pain worse with deep breathing, coughing, or laughing; hoarseness (if the tumor compresses the laryngeal nerve); loss of appetite; unexplained weight loss; shortness of breath; feeling tired or weak; infections like bronchitis or pneumonia that keep coming back; new onset of wheezing. They coincide with symptoms of chronic smokers, so they are hard to differentiate, which is why screening and knowing risk factors matter.
What is the instructor's rule of thumb for reassuring patients worried about weight loss, weakness, tiredness, and loss of appetite?
As long as you are not losing weight (not on purpose), can eat, drink, urinate, and have bowel movements, and are not actively having a fever or infection, you are probably doing okay and not actively dying.
Which cancer does the lecture say has the highest prevalence, and which four specific cancers were historically the most prevalent?
Non-melanoma skin cancer has the highest prevalence (covered in NUR 325). Historically the four with the highest prevalence were lung, breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer, but through advancement they are not seen quite as much now, especially cervical cancer.
How common is breast cancer in the US, and why is it more likely in women?
One in eight women in the US will be diagnosed at some point in their lifetime, and it is the most common cancer in the US (outside of skin cancer). It affects men and women but is much more likely in women because of the increased amount of breast tissue women tend to have.
Give the breast cancer disparity statistics for Black women compared with white women.
Average age at diagnosis is 60 for Black women versus 62 for white women; Black women are 40% more likely to die from breast cancer; they have the lowest five-year relative survival of any racial or ethnic group; and they are diagnosed with the harder-to-treat triple negative breast cancer more than any other group. The instructor says not to memorize the statistics, but to be aware of the disparities.
Which reproductive history factors raise breast cancer risk, and why?
Starting menstrual periods before age 12 and starting menopause after 55. The longer span between starting and stopping the menstrual period means longer exposure to hormones, which raises the risk.
List the non-modifiable breast cancer risk factors, and what does the instructor say about obesity appearing on both lists?
Age over 50; reproductive history; obesity (estrogen and fat deposits); dense breast tissue; family history of breast or ovarian cancer; radiation to the chest; DES; and the BRCA1/BRCA2 genes. The instructor apologizes for listing obesity on both lists: you cannot really control estrogen and fat storage, but the instructor considers it more modifiable than non-modifiable. On the modifiable list the factor is being overweight or having obesity after menopause.
What is DES, and why does it still matter?
Diethylstilbestrol, given to some pregnant women in the US between 1940 and 1971 to prevent miscarriage. Women who took it have a higher risk of breast cancer (and DES use is also linked to cervical cancer). Those women would now be in their late 60s, 70s, and 80s, and the people who were pregnant might be in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, so we need to keep an eye on that.
Which hormone-related therapies raise breast cancer risk, and what is the instructor's caution?
Hormone replacement therapy with estrogen and progesterone, usually taken during menopause, raises risk if taken for more than five years; certain hormonal oral contraceptives also raise it (not all birth control is hormonal). The instructor says these should not be demonized because they do a lot of good for the people who need them; it is just something to think about.
Which pregnancy, breastfeeding, and lifestyle factors raise breast cancer risk?
Having a first pregnancy after 20, not breastfeeding, or never having a full-term pregnancy (all about hormone exposure); alcohol (even just one drink increases risk); night shift work (the instructor assumes hormones get out of whack after long-term night shift work); and smoking and other carcinogen exposures.
What are BRCA1 and BRCA2, which cancers are linked to their mutations, and what do women with the mutations often do?
Genes that we all have, but mutations can be tested for; they are the most common and well studied of the genes connected to breast cancer. Mutations are linked to breast, ovarian, colon, and pancreatic cancer, and to prostate cancer in males. With a family history providers often test early, and women with the mutations often get a preventative mastectomy or an oophorectomy (removal of the ovaries so you do not get ovarian cancer).
Why has cervical cancer become much less prevalent, and how strong is the HPV link?
Because of screening with Pap smears (which detect early cancer mutations) and the HPV vaccine; cervical cancer can essentially be prevented with the vaccine and frequent screenings. Almost 100% of cervical cancer patients test positive for HPV, so preventing HPV through the vaccine significantly decreases cervical cancer rates.
List the cervical cancer risk factors besides HPV infection itself.
Smoking; sexual history and STIs (earlier, more frequent, more partners means more likely to get HPV); other STIs such as chlamydia and gonorrhea compounding HPV; immunosuppression such as HIV or AIDS (probably more susceptible to HPV); genetics and family history; and DES usage.
What are the key facts about the HPV vaccine (effectiveness, safety, ages)?
The current vaccine is 97% effective in preventing cervical cancer and cervical changes, and 100% effective in treating and preventing genital warts. It is very safe, with decades-long studies showing no significant risk or side effects. It is best received as an adolescent around age 11 or 12, but can be received up to age 45.
What are the colorectal cancer statistics given in lecture?
It begins anywhere along the colon or rectum; it is the fourth most common cancer in men and women and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths; males have much higher rates of incidence and of dying from it than females.
List the colorectal cancer risk factors, including the genetic conditions.
Increasing age; inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's or ulcerative colitis); family history of colorectal cancer or polyps; genetic conditions such as familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer (called Lynch syndrome); sedentary lifestyle; diets low in fruits and vegetables, low in fiber, high in fat, or with processed meats such as deli meat; overweight and obesity; alcohol; and tobacco.
What are polyps, and how do adenomatous and hyperplastic polyps differ?
Polyps are non-cancerous growths that can develop into colorectal cancer, usually found on screening colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy and usually asymptomatic (some cause rectal bleeding, bowel habit changes, or abdominal pain). Adenomatous polyps are the most common type and have a higher potential to develop into cancer; hyperplastic polyps are less common and typically do not turn into cancer.
What symptoms of colorectal cancer does the lecture list, and which is more typical of rectal cancer?
Changes in bowel habits, blood in the stool, diarrhea, constipation, a feeling that the bowel does not empty all the way, abdominal pain, aches, or cramping that does not go away, and unexplained weight loss. The bowel not emptying all the way is usually more of a rectal cancer symptom; diarrhea and constipation can be colon or rectal cancer. The first step is probably a colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy.
When does colorectal screening begin, and how do flexible sigmoidoscopy and colonoscopy differ?
Screening begins at 45 at regular intervals; higher-risk people (IBD, family history, genetic conditions) may begin before 45. A flexible sigmoidoscopy is less intensive because it examines only one third of the colon; a full colonoscopy is usually done only every 10 years, depending on whether polyps and such are found.
