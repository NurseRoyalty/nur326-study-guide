Endocrine Disorders

This page covers the adrenal glands end to end: the adrenal cortex (Cushing's syndrome and Addison's disease — a cortisol excess/deficiency mirror pair) and the adrenal medulla plus ADH disorders (pheochromocytoma, SIADH, and diabetes insipidus — each a single-hormone excess-or-deficiency problem). Thyroid and parathyroid disorders get their own page next, since they involve a completely different gland and hormone system.

Adrenal Cortex Background

Quick Pituitary Review

  • The pituitary gland has an anterior and a posterior portion.
  • The anterior pituitary secretes several hormones, including thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) — ACTH is the focus of this section.
  • The posterior pituitary secretes antidiuretic hormone (ADH/vasopressin) and oxytocin — ADH is covered later on this same page.
  • ACTH acts on the adrenal cortex to stimulate release of adrenal cortical hormones.

The adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys. Each gland has an inner medulla (secretes epinephrine/norepinephrine — covered later in the Pheochromocytoma section) and an outer cortex. When stimulated by ACTH, the adrenal cortex releases three types of steroid hormones.

MNEMONIC: The "3 S's" of adrenal cortex hormones — Sugar = cortisol (the main glucocorticoid, and the most clinically important of the three), Salt = aldosterone (mineralocorticoid), Sex = androgens (sex steroids).

These hormones are made on demand and are NOT stored in cells, and they regulate the body's response to stress.

Cortisol's Normal Functions (the key to understanding both Cushing's and Addison's below)

  • Increases glucose availability — raises blood sugar for extra energy.
  • Maintains vascular tone and blood pressure.
  • Breaks down fat and protein for energy.
  • Suppresses the immune and inflammatory response.
  • Activates the CNS/alertness ("fight or flight" readiness).

Cushing's Syndrome (Cortisol Excess)

Cushing's syndrome is a collection of signs and symptoms most commonly associated with hypercortisolism (too much cortisol).

CauseDetail
PrimaryThe adrenal cortex itself is dysfunctional and secretes excessive cortisol directly (e.g., an adrenal tumor).
SecondaryThe problem is in the anterior pituitary — something causes the pituitary to produce excess ACTH, which then drives excessive cortisol production from an otherwise normal adrenal gland.
ExogenousCaused by chronic steroid medication use (e.g., prednisone, dexamethasone). This is the MOST COMMON cause of Cushing syndrome in the United States — steroids are prescribed often, and being on them (especially at higher doses for an extended period) can result in Cushing's.

Even though the adrenal cortex makes three hormone types, cortisol overproduction causes the primary symptoms seen in Cushing's — aldosterone and androgen overproduction also occur but don't drive the main clinical picture the way cortisol excess does.

Why Cushing's Patients Can't Handle a New Stressor (e.g., Surgery)

  • Because cortisol is chronically elevated in Cushing's, the feedback loop that would normally let cortisol rise further in response to a new stressor doesn't work — cortisol is already maxed out and can't respond to acute triggers the way it should.
  • This means a Cushing's patient undergoing surgery may need supplemental ACTH or cortisol administered pre-operatively.
  • Because ACTH is chronically suppressed by the elevated cortisol (negative feedback), the adrenal gland can eventually atrophy from disuse, especially with long-standing disease.

Cushing's Syndrome — Clinical Manifestations

Every finding below traces back to one of cortisol's normal functions being pushed into excess.

Cortisol Function InvolvedManifestations
Glucose effectsPatients become essentially diabetic — glucose intolerant and hyperglycemic (excess cortisol keeps telling the body to raise blood sugar).
Vascular effectsHypertensive state (cortisol's vasoconstriction/BP-maintaining function chronically "on"); also capillary fragility leading to easy skin bruising, because tiny capillaries can't tolerate being chronically "squeezed" and start to fray.
Protein/fat breakdown effectsMuscle wasting and muscle weakness (protein constantly broken down); thinning of skin, osteoporosis, and bone pain; a characteristic fat redistribution to the abdomen, shoulders, and face while the limbs become thin (an "apple-shaped" body appearance).
Immune suppression effectsImpaired wound healing, impaired immune response, and higher risk of infection.
CNS effectsMood swings and inability to sleep (insomnia), from chronic CNS activation.

Additional Classic Physical Findings

  • Thinning hair in normal hair-bearing areas (pubic region, axilla, scalp), but abnormal hair growth in women where it typically doesn't grow — chin, chest, areolas (hirsutism, also seen with some medications).
  • Central/truncal obese abdomen with thin extremities.
  • Gynecomastia (breast development).
  • "Buffalo hump" — a fat pad on the back of the neck.
  • "Moon face."
  • Purple stretch marks (striae).
  • Easy bruising/ecchymosis from fragile capillaries.
  • Thin, fragile skin and poor bone density.
  • Elevated blood pressure from sodium/fluid retention (related to the aldosterone excess that accompanies Cushing's).

Cushing's Syndrome — Treatment & Pharmacology

Treatment is directed at the cause: surgery (and sometimes radiation) for a pituitary tumor (secondary cause) or adrenal tumor (primary cause). For exogenous steroid-induced Cushing's, the treatment is to SLOWLY taper the steroid dose (never stop it abruptly) to get the patient down to the lowest dose that avoids these manifestations.

Drug therapy is available to help suppress steroid synthesis when surgery/radiation isn't available or as a bridge/adjunct — these are NOT typically the primary/preferred treatment, but they need to be known.

DrugDetail
AminoglutethimideBlocks the first step in the synthesis of ALL adrenal steroids (glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, and androgens all decline). Used only for TEMPORARY therapy to decrease cortisol — for example, while a patient is waiting for radiation or surgery. Typically not used longer than about 3 months. Reduces cortisol levels by about 50%, but doesn't treat the underlying cause — it's a bridge/"band-aid." Adverse effects: drowsiness, nausea, anorexia, and rash.
KetoconazoleActually an antifungal drug (recognizable by the "-azole" suffix, a classic antifungal drug-class ending), but it also inhibits glucocorticoid synthesis/blocks adrenal hormone formation, giving it the same use/indication as aminoglutethimide. As with antifungals generally, it can be very toxic to the liver — patients should avoid alcohol while taking it, and it shouldn't be used in patients with existing liver disease. Should NOT be given during pregnancy, because it can cause fetal thyroid damage.

Addison's Disease (Adrenal Cortex Hyposecretion)

The opposite of Cushing's: NOT ENOUGH adrenal cortical hormone. By definition, Addison's is a disease of the adrenal cortex that causes hyposecretion of all three adrenal cortical hormones — not enough cortisol, not enough aldosterone (fluid/electrolyte balance), and not enough androgens (male sex hormones). As with Cushing's, the most severe effects come from the CORTISOL deficiency specifically.

EtiologyDetail
IdiopathicCause unknown.
AutoimmuneThe MOST common cause.
Other causesAny process that damages/destroys the adrenal gland.

Pathophysiology

  • This is a PRIMARY adrenal cortical insufficiency — the adrenal gland itself is damaged/destroyed and stops secreting cortisol.
  • Because of the lost negative feedback, the anterior pituitary hypersecretes both ACTH and melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH), trying to stimulate cortisol production — but because the adrenal gland itself is the problem, it can't respond no matter how much ACTH is sent.
  • Manifestations typically don't become evident until about 90% of the adrenal cortex has been destroyed — the disease is often quite advanced by diagnosis, and onset is typically very slow.

General symptoms: poor appetite, weakness, malaise, along with electrolyte imbalances. A classic and distinctive finding is hyperpigmentation/a tanned appearance, caused by the excess MSH being co-secreted along with ACTH, which drives excess melanin production. President Kennedy had Addison's disease, and this hyperpigmentation is why he always appeared tan — though it was kept fairly private at the time, since it was considered a chronic disease state.

Aldosterone Loss Effects (Hypoaldosteronism)

  • Not retaining sodium and water — losing both → hyponatremia and dehydration.
  • Because sodium and potassium work inversely, these patients also develop hyperkalemia.
  • Salt cravings, because sodium is so low.
  • Low blood pressure, low vascular tone, low cardiac output, and low circulating blood volume.

Cortisol Loss Effects

  • Low energy/glucose availability → hypoglycemia, weakness, fatigue.
  • High ACTH levels (the body's compensatory attempt).
  • Hyperpigmentation from MSH co-secretion, as above.

Addison's Disease — Treatment & Pharmacology

Adrenal insufficiency requires LIFELONG corticosteroid replacement therapy, regardless of the underlying cause.

DrugDetail
HydrocortisoneDrug of choice — it has BOTH glucocorticoid AND mineralocorticoid activity. Can also use prednisone or dexamethasone. Can be given IM, IV, or PO.
FludrocortisoneThe ONLY mineralocorticoid replacement drug available. Works like aldosterone to help with salt/water balance. Given PO, once daily.

Dosing Strategy

  • Replacement therapy should try to mimic the body's NORMAL cortisol secretion pattern (cortisol naturally rises overnight and peaks in the early waking hours).
  • Some clinicians give the entire daily dose at bedtime; others divide it, giving two-thirds in the morning and one-third in the afternoon.
  • Consistency (taking it every day, the same way) is what matters most.
  • Dosing is kept as small as possible while still managing symptoms.
Corticosteroid therapy in Addison's disease can NEVER be abruptly stopped — doing so can precipitate an Addisonian crisis, a life-threatening medical emergency.

Patient Teaching: "Sick Day" Dosing

  • Patients must increase their glucocorticoid dose during ANY kind of stress — physiologic, mental, or emotional (infection, surgery, trauma, or significant emotional stress) — because a healthy adrenal gland would normally increase glucocorticoid output in response to stress, but a patient with adrenal insufficiency can't do this on their own; the extra dose has to be supplied externally.
  • Failing to increase the dose during a stress event can be fatal.
  • A common teaching guideline is the "3-by-3 rule" — take three times the usual dose for three days during a stress event (always in coordination with their care provider).
  • Patients should always carry an adequate supply of their medication, including BOTH an injectable preparation and an oral preparation.
  • Patients should wear some form of medical identification (like a medical alert bracelet) to inform emergency personnel of their condition.

Addisonian Crisis

An acute insufficiency of serum corticosteroids — a true medical emergency where the body essentially "crashes" because it doesn't have enough stress hormone available to meet an increased demand. Also referred to as acute adrenal insufficiency, though when it happens to a known Addison's patient it's typically called an Addisonian crisis specifically.

Triggers

  • Sudden loss/destruction of adrenal gland function.
  • Sudden increase in stress on top of a chronic adrenal insufficiency condition.
  • Sudden cessation of corticosteroid drug therapy.
Any additional stress on the body in a patient with adrenal insufficiency requires more stress hormone than is available — this can be life-threatening if the glucocorticoid dose isn't urgently increased.

There is also a separate, opposite-direction acute emergency called "severe Cushing syndrome," presenting as a massively elevated serum cortisol with severe hypokalemia, often associated with sepsis/opportunistic infection, uncontrolled hypertension, and heart failure — this is the acute crisis version of cortisol EXCESS, the mirror image of an Addisonian crisis, which is cortisol DEFICIENCY.

Pheochromocytoma

A rare tumor of the adrenal MEDULLA — the center part of the adrenal gland, which normally secretes catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine) in response to sympathetic nervous system stimulation. A pheochromocytoma causes excessive production and release of catecholamines. About 90% of the time this tumor is benign — the "-oma" suffix on a tumor name typically indicates a benign tumor, a useful naming pattern to remember. Pheochromocytomas are extremely rare and more often seen in young and middle-aged adults.

TermDetail
PheochromocytomaTumor of the adrenal medulla causing excessive catecholamine (epinephrine/norepinephrine) production and release; about 90% benign.
"-oma" suffixA naming pattern where a tumor name ending in "-oma" typically indicates the tumor is benign.
EpinephrineStimulates BOTH alpha and beta receptors, located in the heart, lungs, and vessels.
NorepinephrineStimulates almost entirely alpha receptors.

Hallmark presentation

  • Hypertension is the hallmark — it can be intermittent or persistent.
  • Accompanied by bouts of the classic triad: headache, diaphoresis, and tachycardia (also common symptoms of very high blood pressure in general).
  • Sporadic hypertensive episodes can be triggered by anything that stimulates the sympathetic nervous system: stress, excitement, exercise, or smoking.
Benign does not mean safe: even though the tumor itself is typically benign, the resulting severe hypertension puts the patient at high risk for stroke or cardiac events — these patients need to be treated urgently, with a calm, low-stimulus environment.

Pheochromocytoma — Treatment & Pharmacology

Surgical removal of the tumor is the preferred/definitive treatment. Drug therapy can be used to control blood pressure, especially before surgery or for patients whose tumor is inoperable, in which case they may take alpha blockers chronically. The main mechanism causing the hypertension is activation of alpha receptors, so alpha blockers are given — typically about 10–14 days before surgery and briefly afterward, since tumor manipulation during surgery can release a massive burst of catecholamines, keeping the patient at risk even during the procedure.

Phenoxybenzamine

  • An older alpha-blocking drug whose only real modern use is for pheochromocytoma.
  • Unlike more typical alpha blockers used for blood pressure (like doxazosin/Cardura), phenoxybenzamine is a NONCOMPETITIVE receptor antagonist — meaning its receptor blockade is not reversible.
  • Normally an irreversible drug effect would be undesirable, but in this specific condition it's exactly what's wanted: long-lasting, irreversible blockage of alpha receptors to keep blood pressure controlled.
  • Alpha receptors work on smooth muscle in general — blocking them causes smooth muscle relaxation and vessel widening, lowering blood pressure.

Side effects (typical of blood-pressure-lowering drugs generally)

  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • Reflex tachycardia
  • Nasal congestion
  • Sexual side effects in men

Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH) Background

ADH (also called vasopressin) is released from the posterior pituitary every day, in response to high serum osmolality (concentrated blood) or hypotension (low blood pressure). Its job is water retention in the kidneys — when the body senses low blood pressure or overly concentrated blood, it wants to hold onto fluid rather than release it as urine, which dilutes the blood back toward normal and helps raise blood pressure.

MNEMONIC: "ADH stops the pee" — a simple way to remember that ADH causes water retention (the opposite of what a diuretic does).

SIADH (Too Much ADH)

SIADH stands for Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone — an abnormal production or sustained secretion of ADH, meaning the body inappropriately holds onto ALL of its water. Characterized by fluid retention, low serum osmolality (dilute blood — low concentration of solutes), and low serum sodium — because the body is holding onto water without holding onto sodium, the urine ends up concentrated instead.

Causes

  • Most common: a malignant tumor — specifically small cell carcinoma / adenocarcinoma of the lung, which can inappropriately secrete ADH on its own.
  • Central nervous system disorders: head trauma, stroke, brain tumor.
  • Certain drugs: morphine, SSRIs, some chemotherapy agents.
  • Miscellaneous conditions: hypothyroidism, infection.
  • The malignant tumor cause is by far the most common.

When ADH is released (whether from a tumor, infection, or trauma), it tells the kidneys to hold onto all the water, increasing intravascular fluid volume and diluting the vascular system — this causes hyponatremia, decreased serum osmolality, and concentrated urine (sometimes called dilutional hyponatremia).

Manifestations

  • Signs of hyponatremia and fluid volume excess: fatigue, confusion, lethargy, altered/dulled sensorium, muscle twitching, convulsions.
  • GI effects: impaired taste, anorexia, vomiting.
  • Severity tracks with how low the sodium gets — normal sodium range is 135–145 mEq/L.
  • In severe cases, patients can develop "water intoxication" — when serum sodium drops lower than the sodium concentration inside cells, cells (including brain cells) start pulling in water and swelling, causing further neurologic problems.
Sodium below ~115 mEq/L means severe, irreversible neurologic damage: severe hyponatremia from SIADH can cause water intoxication from cellular swelling, especially dangerous in brain cells.

SIADH — Treatment

General measures

  • Directed at the underlying cause: remove the tumor if that's the cause, stop the causative medication if a drug is responsible, or treat/wait out the underlying condition if it's a head trauma or stroke.
  • Fluid restriction is a core non-drug measure.
  • Loop diuretics can be given to promote diuresis, but ONLY if the sodium is above 125 mEq/L — giving a diuretic when sodium is already very low risks losing even more sodium.
  • Salt tablets may also be given.
  • Pharmacotherapy is generally not first-line.

Demeclocycline (chronic/refractory SIADH)

  • Used for chronic/refractory SIADH, such as from an unremovable lung tumor.
  • Actually a tetracycline broad-spectrum antibiotic (originally used as an antifungal, interestingly) that has a side effect of interfering with the kidney's response to ADH, making the kidneys less sensitive to it.
  • Because it's a tetracycline, it carries typical tetracycline side effects: photosensitivity (patients need to avoid sun exposure), teeth staining, and nephrotoxicity.

Diabetes Insipidus (Too Little ADH)

The opposite problem from SIADH: not enough ADH, or the kidneys don't respond properly to the ADH that is present — the patient loses ALL their water (versus SIADH, where the body retains all its water). There are two types, distinguished by cause.

Neurogenic (central) DI

  • Damage to the hypothalamus or pituitary gland interferes with the RELEASE of ADH from the brain.
  • Typically associated with traumatic brain injury, stroke, or a cerebral infection, and often occurs following an injury or surgical intervention.
  • Onset is SUDDEN.
  • Usually PERMANENT, because the pituitary has been damaged/destroyed in some way.

Nephrogenic DI

  • A kidney problem — the kidneys are unable to properly respond to ADH.
  • Usually related to loss of kidney function (e.g., chronic kidney disease), or caused by certain drugs, notably lithium (used for bipolar disorder).
  • Onset is SLOW and progressive (versus the sudden onset of neurogenic DI).

Regardless of type, the patient is losing large amounts of essentially pure water.

Presentation

  • Massive polyuria — patients can lose as much as 15 liters of urine a day.
  • Accompanied by extreme thirst (polydipsia) and dehydration, since the body is trying to compensate; patients may drink up to 15 liters of fluid a day trying to keep up.
  • Causes significant electrolyte imbalances.
  • Because patients become so intravascularly dry, they can progress to hypovolemic shock, which can be fatal.
Uncontrolled diabetes insipidus can be fatal: severe dehydration from massive, ongoing water loss can progress to fatal hypovolemic shock.

Diabetes Insipidus — Treatment

Treatment differs by type, which is an important testable distinction.

Neurogenic DI — Desmopressin (DDAVP)

  • Since the problem is that the brain isn't releasing ADH at all, the treatment is synthetic ADH replacement — desmopressin, also known as DDAVP (not a trade name — a commonly used shortened name for the drug itself).
  • Essentially straight synthetic ADH replacement.
  • Can be given via nasal spray (common), PO, IV, or subcutaneously, usually in small doses.
  • Nasal spray can cause some nasal irritation; overly large doses can cause an overdose/opposite effect (excessive water retention).

Nephrogenic DI — Thiazide diuretic (HCTZ)

  • Giving more ADH/DDAVP does NOT work for this type, since the kidneys themselves can't respond to ADH whether or not it is present.
  • Instead, treated, somewhat counterintuitively, with a thiazide diuretic (HCTZ).
  • Research has shown this paradoxically reduces polyuria and increases urine osmolality in these patients, even though thiazides are normally used to promote diuresis for other conditions.
  • The exact mechanism for why this works in nephrogenic DI isn't fully understood, but it's an established, testable treatment fact.

Why doesn't DDAVP work for nephrogenic DI? Because the kidneys are the problem — they can't respond to ADH even when it's present, so adding more ADH doesn't help.
