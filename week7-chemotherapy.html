Chemotherapy, Antineoplastic Drug Classes & Antiemetics

This page covers chemotherapy from the general principles to the specific drugs. It starts with what anti-neoplastic drugs and cytotoxic chemotherapy are, how chemo fits with surgery and radiation, the two groups of chemo drugs, what affects whether chemo works, the general principles, and the toxicities of hitting rapidly dividing cells (stomatitis and magic mouthwash, myelosuppression and the nadir, extravasation, monitoring, contraindications, fertility, and pregnancy). It then moves to the classes of anti-cancer medication you are expected to know (alkylating agents, antimetabolites, antitumor antibiotics, mitotic inhibitors, hormonal agents, and biologics and targeted therapies) with their cell-cycle specificity and toxicities, and closes with the two antiemetics, ondansetron and promethazine.

Anti-Neoplastic Drugs & Cytotoxic Chemotherapy

Anti-neoplastic drugs

  • The general term for drugs used to treat cancer.
  • These are various types of chemotherapy agents that inhibit the growth and the spread of malignant cancer cells.
  • All the drug types other than the cytotoxic agents can be considered anti-cancer or anti-neoplastic drugs.

Chemotherapy (cytotoxic chemotherapy)

  • A common form of cancer treatment that uses cytotoxic drugs to kill or inhibit cancer cells.
  • Cytotoxic refers to the ability of these drugs to kill cells.
  • They particularly target rapidly dividing cells (this idea drives almost everything else on this page).
  • Cancer chemotherapy, strictly speaking, refers only to cytotoxic drugs; cytotoxic agents are typically the only ones called chemotherapy.
  • The instructor will not get into the weeds on this wording; it is just good to know.

How much you are expected to know

  • The instructor stresses that chemotherapy is an ongoing area of research and changes very consistently; it is an ever-evolving field that changes very quickly.
  • For anyone who ends up in oncology (for example at Markey or another cancer center), the advice is to really know the drugs you are giving and the types of cancers they treat, to take notes, and to stay aware of the changes.
  • This course only introduces the basics. The instructor explains the drug matrix and what you are expected to know; this page covers the general overview.

Surgery, Radiation & Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is one tenet (one pillar) of treating cancer. The other two named in the lecture are surgery and radiation, and these treatments are often used in combination.

TreatmentWhat it does
SurgeryGenerally removes tumors or affected tissues.
Radiation therapyUses high-energy radiation to kill or damage cancer cells.
ChemotherapyWhat it does depends on the kind of drug, but it also targets the cancer cells.

Choosing the treatment course

  • A lot of the time these treatments are used in combination, and the type of cancer being treated influences what the normal course of treatment is.
  • Lots of discussions need to take place before treatment begins: deciding whether to do surgery, radiation, chemo, just one of them, and so on.
  • The instructor says oncology is really ahead of most of the fields studied so far in shared decision making: oncologists work together with their patients to figure out the best treatment course for what is going on with that patient.
  • You will learn more about this in your med-surg content.

Two Main Groups of Chemotherapy Drugs

Cell cycle nonspecific

  • Kill cancer cells regardless of what phase of the cell cycle the cell is in.
  • It does not matter what phase the cells are in.

Cell cycle specific

  • Only work during certain phases of the cell cycle, like mitosis or DNA synthesis.
  • Those are the anti-metabolites and the mitotic inhibitors.
  • The instructor says you do not need to write this down quickly because it will be discussed as the material moves forward.

Cross-reference: the lecture points back to the cancer slides, which discuss what phase the cells are in; see Cancer Fundamentals, Metastasis & Etiology

Growth fraction

  • Regardless of the group, these medications work faster on rapidly growing tumors.
  • Chemotherapy tends to be more effective against tumors that have a high growth fraction.
  • Reason (instructor's): a high-growth-fraction tumor has more cells that are actively dividing, which makes them more vulnerable to cytotoxic chemotherapy agents.

Factors That Affect Whether Chemo Works

The lecture lists these as the factors that determine whether chemo will cure the cancer or work effectively.

FactorWhat the lecture says
Doubling timeTumors that grow quickly respond better to chemo (as already noted above).
Type of cancerSome cancers are inherently more sensitive to chemotherapy. Some cancers are less likely to spread, so they are more effectively cured by surgery, and so on.
Efficacy of the treatment planIs the correct drug selected? Is it dosed appropriately? Is it scheduled appropriately? There are whole specific oncologists and pharmacists who are dedicated to this area.
Drug resistanceAs with other types of medications, cancers can become drug resistant, which can significantly reduce treatment effectiveness over a longer period of time. Example: if a patient got one type of chemotherapy at the beginning and the cancer then recurred (relapsed), that could be because the cancer cells became resistant to that type of chemotherapy.
General health of the patientA patient's overall condition affects their ability to tolerate and respond to treatment. Chemo drugs are very harsh medications and are not easy or gentle on the system.

General Principles of All Chemotherapy Drugs

Principles that apply across chemo

  • No one drug is effective against all types of cancer. (The instructor's point: if there were one, we would simply teach that one drug and move forward.)
  • Low therapeutic index. There is a fine line between chemotherapy being therapeutic and having toxic effects. Clinicians are monitoring the cancer spread (for example with PET scans) against the adverse or toxic effects of the drugs, and it is a very fine line.
  • Toxicity comes from affecting rapidly dividing cells. Chemo drugs can kill the harmful cancer cells but also affect normal cells.
  • Combination therapy usually works better than a single drug. The different mechanisms of action of different cytotoxic medications are maximized when multiples are used. There is some efficacy even as a single drug, depending on the type of cancer.
  • Avoid overlapping toxicity. When combining drugs, the team is careful that the drugs do not have overlapping toxicity. Example: you would not use two chemotherapy types that both cause cardiotoxicity.

Toxicities from Rapidly Dividing Cells

Chemo drugs affect rapidly dividing cells, so the normal tissues that divide quickly take the damage along with the cancer. Hair follicles, the GI tract, and bone marrow are very commonly affected because they are rapidly dividing. These are common side effects seen with every single chemotherapy.

Hair folliclesHair loss
GI tractA ton of nausea and vomiting (also stomatitis in the mouth, see below)
Bone marrowNeutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia

Stomatitis & Magic Mouthwash

Stomatitis is inflammation of the oral mucosa. It is commonly affected by almost all chemo medications, again because the cells of the mouth are rapidly dividing.

The instructor's way of thinking about it

  • A blister in your mouth (a canker sore) heals pretty quickly. A blister on your hands takes a little longer to heal.
  • Skin cells are rapidly dividing too, but they are not as fast as the cells of your mouth or your GI tract.

Magic mouthwash

  • The instructor says you can put it on your drug matrix if you want; it is a drug you need to be familiar with.
  • It is a prescription cocktail for stomatitis (a prescription medication).
  • It is commonly prescribed for stomatitis, and the exact contents vary.
  • Patients swish, gargle, and spit it out. It can be swallowed if there is thought to be esophageal involvement.

DiphenhydramineHelps with the inflammation.

Simpler versions and what it can and cannot do

  • Simple versions may only have lidocaine, an antifungal, and diphenhydramine.
  • Overall, it numbs the mouth, prevents antifungal infection (thrush), and helps with inflammation.
  • It does not cure anything and will not fix the stomatitis, but it does treat the symptoms.

Myelosuppression

What it is

  • Bone marrow suppression or depression.
  • The bone marrow is a rapidly dividing area, so it is commonly targeted and affected as an adverse effect of most chemotherapies.
  • It typically is caused by chemotherapy, but it can also be caused by radiation.

What it causes

  • Reduced production of white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets.
  • This means neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia.
  • A very high risk of infection due to the leukopenia (usually neutropenia).

Why neutropenia is dangerous

  • Neutrophils are critical in fighting bacterial infections, or infections in general.
  • Their depletion makes patients very vulnerable to infection.
  • Patients are also vulnerable because they become anemic and thrombocytopenic.

Prophylactic antibiotics: levofloxacin

  • Because of the neutropenia or leukopenia, it is often recommended that patients get prophylactic antibiotics (the instructor says this will be discussed more in med-surg).
  • Levofloxacin is very common.
  • It is recommended in high-risk patients who have prolonged neutropenia, defined in the lecture as an ANC less than 500 for greater than 7 days.
  • It helps prevent bacterial infections.

Cross-reference: levofloxacin as an antibiotic is covered in Antimicrobials & Antibiotics

The nadir

The nadir is the lowest point of the white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets, and it is about 7 to 14 days after chemotherapy. This is when patients are at highest risk. The instructor highlights it as important.

The nadir is the time of highest risk for infection, anemia, and bleeding. The instructor calls this a very important teaching point and a point of awareness for nursing interventions: the nadir is the lowest point of white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets, and the time of highest risk for infection, anemia, and bleeding.

Extravasation & Central Access

Extravasation is a very serious complication of IV chemotherapy. Chemotherapy drugs may leak into the surrounding tissues during IV administration, which can cause severe tissue damage.
  • Most chemotherapy drugs are given IV, or a lot of them are.
  • Treatment protocols for preventing and treating extravasation are well established.
  • Because of this risk, chemo is generally given through a portacath or some other deep tunneled line.

Drug Monitoring: No Routine TDM

A common assumption that is wrong

  • You might expect that, because these drugs have a lot of side effects and a lot of risk, there would be lots of narrow therapeutic monitoring (drug-level monitoring).
  • The instructor says that is actually not true: most chemotherapy drugs do not require routine therapeutic drug monitoring.
  • Some do, but it is not as common as you would think.

Contraindications

Usually NOT contraindications

  • Toxicities, adverse effects, and allergies are not usually contraindications.
  • There are no absolute contraindications.
  • Many side effects are expected and managed proactively.
  • Even allergies can require premedication or a desensitization protocol.

Reasons to delay or adjust chemo

  • A white blood cell or neutrophil count that is very, very low.
  • Known active infection.
  • Significantly decreased nutrition or hydration status.
  • Organ dysfunction, usually liver or kidney (monitored with kidney and liver labs).
  • In these situations chemotherapy may be delayed or adjusted, so these can act as contraindications.

Why these are weighed rather than treated as absolute

  • Low white cell counts and poor nutrition status are really common in patients with cancer.
  • The healthcare provider weighs the risks and benefits.
  • The reasoning given: the cancer is not going to stop growing just because the patient is sick.

Fertility & Pregnancy

Fertility

  • A major concern in the instructor's words "generally" for female patients, but it can affect males as well.
  • Any chemotherapy can impair fertility, especially in post-puberty patients.
  • Fertility preservation options: sperm banking or egg freezing.
  • This generally should be discussed before treatment; it is something we need to be aware of.

Pregnancy

  • Most chemotherapy drugs are classified as class D or X.
  • This means they pose significant risk to fetal development or are generally contraindicated during pregnancy.
  • Unfortunately, some cancers are more likely to be found during the pregnancy period, which generates scary discussions and conversations that have to be had.

Cell-Cycle Specific vs Nonspecific Drugs

The lecture reminds you that there are cell cycle phase specific drugs and cell cycle phase nonspecific drugs (the instructor calls it a tongue twister). The big differentiating point is whether the drug can affect cells resting in G0.

Cell cycle phase specific

  • Only work on a specific phase of the cell cycle.
  • Examples from the lecture: vinca alkaloids (vincristine) only work during the mitosis phase; methotrexate only works in the S phase.
  • They cannot interrupt cells that are in G0, the resting phase where a lot of cells tend to sit.
  • Implication: this kind of medication needs to remain in the blood for extended periods — either by prolonged infusions or by small doses at short intervals over extended times — to optimize dosing.
  • They do not kill cells in the resting phase.

Cell cycle phase nonspecific

  • It does not matter where the cells are in the cycle — the drugs are just toxic.
  • They can even affect cells in the G0 phase; they do kill cells in the resting phase.
  • Disadvantage: they are not as effective at injuring just the proliferating cells.
  • Examples from the lecture: cyclophosphamide and doxorubicin.
Combination therapy: because of this difference, drugs are generally used in combination — a cell cycle specific agent plus a cell cycle nonspecific agent. Make sure you understand that one includes cells in G0 and one does not.

The instructor says you do not need to memorize the cell-cycle slide. You should know that vinca alkaloids fall under cell cycle phase specific; that class is discussed specifically below, so if it matters you will see it on that slide.

Cytotoxic Agents in General

This is a brief overview of the cytotoxic agents as a group; the specific types of drugs can go on your drug matrix.

Common features of all cytotoxic agents

  • All of them work by disrupting DNA synthesis.
  • Cytotoxic agents are the largest class of chemotherapy and act directly on cancer cells to cause their death.
  • They target high growth fraction cells, which are cells that reproduce very rapidly.
  • All of these drugs commonly affect hair follicles and cause nausea, vomiting, and stomatitis. Myelosuppression is also listed among the usual toxicities (named with doxorubicin).
Handling cytotoxic agents is dangerous. Any cytotoxic agent is very dangerous to handle and there are strict protocols. You need to be certified to deliver cancer drugs. A lot of these are vesicants. They may require double gloving, disposal in black bins, and many steps before administering (for example, flushing something like three times). If you work in an area that delivers cancer drugs you will be trained on this; the instructor does not teach the specifics, only that this is very common with all cytotoxic agents.

Cardiotoxicity is the big concern with antitumor antibiotics. There are maximum doses over time (a maximum lifetime dose). The worry is development of congestive heart failure, which can be fatal. These patients usually require closer monitoring of EF and baseline echocardiograms. The cardiotoxicity can occur acutely, while they are receiving the drug, or post-infusion, years down the road.

The whole field of cardio-oncology came about because of this drug. Because of its cardiotoxicity there are a lot of issues faced with it, even though it is very effective.

Biologics & Targeted Drug Therapies

This is the newest class of anti-cancer medication and it is growing rapidly. The instructor thinks this is the future of chemotherapy, although the instructor is not a cancer specialist and notes that many of the cytotoxic drugs work and work well; biologics are where the research world is "spinning." Biologics use the body's own immune system to recognize and destroy the inappropriate or wrong cancer cells. Targeted drugs are designed to interfere with specific molecular targets involved in cancer growth (for example, tyrosine kinase inhibitors) and generally fall under biologics.

The instructor will not ask you for a specific type of monoclonal antibody or CAR-T therapy; you just need to understand biologics overall.

  • Some are available subcutaneously (sub-Q) or IM.
  • Very few are available orally, or by intrathecal, intravascular, or ventricular routes (as transcribed).

Antiemetics for Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea & Vomiting

All of these drugs cause nausea and vomiting, and that is a really big issue for nutrition and hydration in patients receiving chemotherapy. The instructor wants you to know these two antiemetic drugs.

Cross-reference: the general profile of ondansetron and promethazine is covered in GI Pharmacology

Ondansetron (Zofran)

  • Created specifically for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting; also used in other areas (many of us have probably taken Zofran).
  • It blocks the type 3 serotonin receptors located in the chemo trigger zone in the brain.
  • Very effective by itself, but also very effective combined with other drugs, especially steroids, to help decrease nausea.
  • Given orally or IV.
  • Most common side effects: headache, diarrhea, and dizziness.

Promethazine (Phenergan)

  • Used for chemo, post-op, and general nausea and vomiting.
  • A first-generation antihistamine, which should indicate to you that it will make you very, very tired.
  • Major adverse effect: drowsiness or sedation.
  • Can also cause significant respiratory depression, like an opioid.
  • Lots of drug-drug interactions to be aware of.
  • Best given by IV piggyback because of the risk of extravasation. Make sure the IV works before IV pushing it, and dilute it down if you can.
Promethazine black box warnings: (1) use in children under 2 years old, and (2) gangrenous extravasation. Be aware of this when pushing the drug: even though it is for nausea and vomiting and is not the chemo itself, it can still be very dangerous.

What are anti-neoplastic drugs, what does "cytotoxic chemotherapy" mean, and which drugs does "chemotherapy" strictly refer to?
Anti-neoplastic drugs are the drugs used to treat cancer; various chemotherapy agents that inhibit the growth and spread of malignant cancer cells. Cytotoxic chemotherapy uses drugs that kill or inhibit cancer cells (cytotoxic = able to kill cells), particularly targeting rapidly dividing cells. Strictly, chemotherapy refers only to cytotoxic drugs; the other drug types are anti-cancer or anti-neoplastic.
Name the three tenets of cancer treatment, what each does, and what the instructor says about shared decision making.
Surgery generally removes tumors or affected tissues; radiation uses high-energy radiation to kill or damage cancer cells; chemotherapy (depending on the drug) also targets the cancer cells. They are often used in combination. Oncology is really ahead of most fields in shared decision making: oncologists work with patients to find the best treatment course, and lots of discussions take place before treatment begins.
Why is chemo more effective against tumors with a high growth fraction?
Those tumors have more cells that are actively dividing, which makes them more vulnerable to cytotoxic chemotherapy agents. Regardless of group, chemo works faster on rapidly growing tumors.
List the factors the lecture gives for whether chemo will work, and explain how drug resistance can explain a relapse.
Doubling time (fast-growing tumors respond better); type of cancer (some are inherently more sensitive, others are less likely to spread and are better cured by surgery); efficacy of the plan (right drug, right dose, right schedule); drug resistance; and the patient's general health. If a patient got one type of chemo at the beginning and the cancer relapsed, the cells may have become resistant to it.
State the general principles that apply to all chemotherapy drugs.
No one drug is effective against all cancers. Low therapeutic index: a fine line between therapeutic and toxic effects (monitoring cancer spread, for example with PET scans, against adverse effects). Toxicity comes from affecting rapidly dividing cells. Combination therapy usually works better because the different mechanisms of action are maximized, but the team avoids overlapping toxicity (for example, not using two chemo types that both cause cardiotoxicity).
Which tissues are most affected by chemo's effect on rapidly dividing cells, and what are the timing and other common effects?
Hair follicles (hair loss), the GI tract (nausea and vomiting), and bone marrow (neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia). Nausea and vomiting occurs several days after chemo. Hair loss (reversible) begins 7 to 14 days after chemo and starts to regenerate 1 to 2 months after therapy ends. Fatigue is extremely common, and dose-limiting adverse effects can occur (too nauseous, anemic, or neutropenic to tolerate needed dose increases).
What is stomatitis, and why does chemo cause it?
Inflammation of the oral mucosa, seen with almost all chemo meds because the cells of the mouth are rapidly dividing. The instructor's comparison: a mouth canker sore heals faster than a blister on the hands, because skin cells are not as fast as the mouth or GI tract.
What are the components of magic mouthwash, and what does it do and not do?
A prescription cocktail whose exact contents vary: viscous lidocaine (numbs the mouth), Maalox/Mylanta, diphenhydramine (helps with inflammation), nystatin (antifungal; prevents thrush), prednisolone, and distilled water. Simple versions may have only lidocaine, an antifungal, and diphenhydramine. Patients swish, gargle, and spit (it can be swallowed if esophageal involvement is suspected). It treats the symptoms but does not cure or fix the stomatitis.
Define myelosuppression and state what it causes.
Bone marrow suppression or depression, typically caused by chemotherapy but also possible with radiation. It reduces production of white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets, causing neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, and a very high infection risk (usually from neutropenia).
What prophylactic antibiotic is common in chemo patients, and for whom is it recommended?
Levofloxacin, recommended in high-risk patients with prolonged neutropenia: an ANC less than 500 for greater than 7 days. It helps prevent bacterial infections.
What is the nadir, when does it occur, and why does it matter for nursing?
The nadir is the lowest point of the white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets, about 7 to 14 days after chemotherapy. It is when the patient is at highest risk for infection, anemia, and bleeding. The instructor calls it a very important teaching point and a point of awareness for nursing interventions.
What is extravasation, and how is the risk addressed?
Chemo drugs leaking into the surrounding tissues during IV administration, which can cause severe tissue damage. Well-established protocols exist to prevent and treat it, and chemo is generally given through a portacath or other deep tunneled line because of this risk.
Do most chemo drugs need routine therapeutic drug monitoring, and are toxicities or allergies contraindications?
Most do not require routine TDM (some do). Toxicities, adverse effects, and allergies are usually not contraindications; there are no absolute ones. Side effects are managed proactively, and allergies may need premedication or a desensitization protocol. Chemo may be delayed or adjusted for very low WBC or neutrophils, active infection, poor nutrition or hydration, or liver or kidney dysfunction, with risk and benefit weighed because the cancer will not stop growing just because the patient is sick.
What are the fertility and pregnancy considerations with chemotherapy?
Any chemo can impair fertility, especially in post-puberty patients (a major concern generally in females, but males too); sperm banking or egg freezing should be discussed before treatment. Most chemo drugs are class D or X: significant risk to fetal development or generally contraindicated in pregnancy.
What do you need to know for the exam about the drug classes?
You must know the classes (alkylating agents, antimetabolites, antitumor antibiotics, mitotic inhibitors, hormonal agents, and some biologics; targeted drugs generally fall under biologics) but not specific drug names. The instructor will always give the class, because the class tells you the expected adverse effects and toxicities. You will not be asked to pick a drug for a specific cancer.
Distinguish cell cycle phase specific from nonspecific drugs, including the effect on G0 cells.
Specific drugs (methotrexate in S phase; vinca alkaloids in mitosis) work only in a given phase and cannot interrupt cells in G0, the resting phase. Nonspecific drugs (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin) are toxic regardless of cell-cycle position and can affect G0 cells, but are less effective at injuring only proliferating cells.
What is the dosing implication of cell cycle specific drugs, and why are drugs generally combined?
They must remain in the blood for extended periods: prolonged infusions or small doses at short intervals over extended times. Combination therapy pairs a cell cycle specific agent with a nonspecific one, because one includes cells in G0 and the other does not.
What do all cytotoxic agents have in common, and what must you know about handling them?
They disrupt DNA synthesis, act directly on cancer cells, and target high growth fraction (rapidly reproducing) cells. They are very dangerous to handle: strict protocols, certification to deliver, many are vesicants, may need double gloving, black-bin disposal, and multiple flushes. Common toxicities include effects on hair follicles, nausea, vomiting, and stomatitis.
Describe cyclophosphamide: its class, cell-cycle specificity, and uses.
Alkylating agent; cell cycle phase nonspecific. Used for a variety of cancers including breast cancer, lymphoma, leukemia, and ovarian cancer.
What toxicities are specific to alkylating agents like cyclophosphamide?
Extreme vesicant (extensive tissue damage if it extravasates); hemorrhagic cystitis due to a metabolite (look for blood in the urine or bladder issues); sterility, especially with prolonged use (fertility concerns); skin and nail discoloration (less common); resistance over time, so it may be used with another drug and not used if the cancer relapses.
What class is methotrexate, which phase does it work in, and how does it work?
Antimetabolite; cell cycle phase specific, acting primarily in the S phase by inhibiting DNA synthesis. It mimics natural metabolites like folic acid and interferes with folate-dependent enzymes such as dihydrofolate reductase. Resistance can develop via increased drug efflux, altered target enzymes, or enhanced DNA repair.
What toxicities and nursing points go with antimetabolites (methotrexate)?
Nephrotoxicity, especially at high doses (keep patients hydrated); hepatotoxicity with long-term use, more with non-oncology use (monitor liver enzymes); FDA category X, causing fetal death and abnormalities (teratogenic, contraindicated in pregnancy, so discuss birth control/fertility). Uses include leukemias, lymphomas, breast, head and neck cancers, osteosarcoma, and non-oncology uses like rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis.
What is the antitumor antibiotic example, its specificity and origin, and what is the major concern with it?
Doxorubicin; cell cycle phase nonspecific; derived from Streptomyces bacteria; very effective for many cancers, especially breast cancers. The major concern is cardiotoxicity, especially with cumulative dosing (maximum lifetime dose): congestive heart failure that can be fatal. Monitor EF and obtain baseline echocardiograms; it can occur acutely or years after infusion, and the field of cardio-oncology arose because of this drug.
What are the two names for this class, and what are vincristine's mechanism, specificity, and source?
Mitotic inhibitors, also known as vinca alkaloids (know both terms). Vincristine is cell cycle specific (mitosis phase), inhibits microtubule formation and prevents cell division, and comes from the periwinkle plant.
What is the hallmark side effect of vinca alkaloids, and what safety points does the lecture stress?
Peripheral neuropathy (hallmark; many neurotoxic effects). It is a significant vesicant. It does not typically cause significant bone marrow suppression, which makes it useful in combination with myelosuppressive drugs. It is given IV only; intrathecal administration will kill the patient.
What are hormonal agents used for, and what example is named?
Hormone-sensitive cancers such as breast and prostate cancers; the example is tamoxifen.
What are biologics and targeted therapies, and what types does the lecture list?
The newest, rapidly growing class; they harness the body's own immune system to recognize and destroy cancer cells (targeted drugs interfere with specific molecular targets, e.g., tyrosine kinase inhibitors). Types: immune checkpoint inhibitors, T cell transfers (CAR-T), monoclonal antibodies, cancer vaccines (prostate, HPV-related cancers), and immune system modulators (melanoma, renal cell carcinoma). They are used alone or with other chemotherapy.
Name the side effects of biologics that differ from cytotoxic toxicities, including immune-related events and CAR T-cell effects.
Flu-like symptoms; infusion/injection site reactions; GI upset (not necessarily at the same rate). Immune-related adverse events, especially with checkpoint inhibitors: skin rashes, colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, and endocrinopathies, many ending in "-itis," from overreaction of the immune system. Neurologic effects especially with CAR T-cell therapy, and cytokine release syndrome (CRS), which can be very life-threatening and needs very close monitoring. Serious infections from immunosuppression differ from true myelosuppression. Most are IV; some sub-Q or IM.
Why are antiemetics emphasized with chemotherapy, and how does ondansetron (Zofran) work, how is it given, and what are its side effects?
All of these drugs cause nausea and vomiting, a big issue for nutrition and hydration. Ondansetron was created specifically for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and blocks the type 3 serotonin receptors in the chemo trigger zone in the brain. Given orally or IV; effective alone or combined with other drugs, especially steroids. Most common side effects: headache, diarrhea, and dizziness.
What class is promethazine (Phenergan), what are its adverse effects, how is it given, and what are its black box warnings?
A first-generation antihistamine used for chemo, post-op, and general nausea and vomiting. Major adverse effect is drowsiness/sedation; it can also cause significant respiratory depression like an opioid, and has many drug-drug interactions. Best given by IV piggyback because of extravasation risk; make sure the IV works before pushing, and dilute it if you can. Black box warnings: children under 2 years old, and gangrenous extravasation.
