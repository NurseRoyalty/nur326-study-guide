Osteoporosis

This is the first of several lectures on diseases and problems of the musculoskeletal system. Osteoporosis is low bone density with structural deterioration of the bone, and it matters because of the risk of fractures. This topic covers gender and bone mass, how bone density is measured and categorized (T-scores and DEXA), who gets osteoporosis, the pathogenesis (osteoclasts vs. osteoblasts), what patients look like, hip fractures (the biggest complication), and the pharmacotherapy: calcium and vitamin D, bisphosphonates (alendronate/Fosamax), SERMs (raloxifene/Evista), calcitonin, and why hormone replacement therapy is not recommended.

Gender & Bone Mass

The lecture starts with why women are affected so much more than men. Several factors affect bone mass: age, gender, race, genetics, reproductive status, calcium levels, and exercise. The lecture looks particularly at age and gender.

Women

  • Experience greater bone loss in the early post-menopausal years; after that period of bone loss ends, the loss continues at a more gradual rate.
  • Also have a lower peak bone density than men.
  • These factors combine so that women reach a fracture threshold (the point where the risk of fracture significantly increases) much earlier than men.
  • Over a lifetime, women lose about 35% of cortical bone and 50% of trabecular bone.
  • Beginning at age 30, the amount of bone resorbed by osteoclasts exceeds the amount formed by osteoblasts, resulting in a steady decrease in bone mass with age.

Men

  • Lose only about two-thirds of the amount women lose over a lifetime, so men lose about a third less than women.
  • The same pattern is seen in men: after age 30 the bone-mass curve starts to go down.
  • Bone mass decreases as a function of age in both males and females, but women lose much more in the immediate post-menopausal period.

The instructor points to a slide graph of bone mass versus age: both curves start to go down after age 30, and women's curve drops steeply in the early post-menopausal years and then continues down at a more gradual rate.

Why We Care: Bone Density, T-Scores & DEXA

We care because low bone density means a greater likelihood of breaking bones, and low bone density is considered osteoporosis. Bone density is measured with a T-score. Osteoporosis is defined as a bone mineral density 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass. The measurement is done with a DEXA scan and reported as T-scores (in standard deviations).

CategoryT-score (DEXA)What it means

Severe osteoporosisOsteoporosis (T-score below −2.5) plus a history of a fragility fractureThe diagnosis of "severe" is given when there is a history of a fragility fracture.

What is a fragility fracture?

  • The person fell from standing and broke a bone.
  • It is not a traumatic accident; the bone broke because it is so porous.

Osteopenia vs. Osteoporosis

  • Osteopenia is the stage before osteoporosis, with thinning of the trabecular matrix. Trabecular bone is the porous bone. It is seen with a T-score between −1 and −2.5 on DEXA.
  • Osteoporosis is "holey" (porous) bone, reached at a T-score of −2.5. It is obviously a weaker bone.
  • Osteoporosis is a common yet very serious disease. It is serious because of the risk of fractures, and it is characterized by low bone density and structural deterioration of the bone.
  • It is most common in the hip, vertebrae, and wrist bones, which the lecture describes as trabecular bones.

Characteristics of the Disease

  • Low bone mass.
  • Microarchitectural deterioration of the bone (the lecture shows X-rays/a figure of the changes in bone architecture at a microscopic level).
  • In the slide image, the osteoporotic bone (on the left) shows clear loss of bone, with large gaps and breaks within the architecture of the bone.
  • This increases bone fragility and ultimately leads to weak bones and fractures.

Pathogenesis

Bone is a living tissue. Bone remodeling is the process of resorption and formation. It occurs throughout your lifetime, and about 10% of bone tissue is replaced each year.

Osteoclasts

  • Cause bone resorption (breakdown).
  • Stimulate the production of enzymes that dissolve bone.
  • The instructor's shorthand: osteoclasts "destroy" bone.

Osteoblasts

  • Build up the protein matrix of bone.
  • Osteoblasts build bone.
MEMORY AID (from the instructor): osteoBlasts Build; osteoClasts destroy. The instructor remembers it with the word "build."
In osteoporosis: increased osteoclast activity and decreased osteoblast activity. We cannot make new bone because we do not have the osteoblast function, and we have too much bone resorption from osteoclasts.

Manifestations

Early

  • There are really no manifestations early on.
  • So the approach is risk-factor identification: if someone is high risk, DEXA scans are started earlier.

Later

  • Fractures
  • Pain
  • Loss of height
  • Stooped posture, like kyphosis

Hip Fractures

One of the biggest, most concerning, and most common complications of osteoporosis is the hip fracture.

Epidemiology, Mortality & Outcomes

  • Osteoporotic hip fractures lead to increased risk of mortality: once you have an osteoporotic hip fracture, the risk of death increases by about 2.5 to 4 times.
  • Deaths do not occur from the fracture itself, but from complications after it that are related to immobility: sepsis, skin breakdown, immobility, pneumonia, and blood clots.
  • Much more common in women older than 65, because osteoporosis is much more common in women.
  • The most common location is the proximal third of the femur.
  • In addition to the increased risk of death, independent living is usually lost, and these patients often end up in some kind of care facility such as a nursing home.

Insufficiency (Fragility) Fractures

  • There may be no obvious history of trauma.
  • The patient may complain of vague knee, groin, or thigh pain.
  • In these cases the bone is so damaged and has lost so much density that it can no longer bear weight, and that is when the fragility fracture develops.
  • Once the fracture occurs there is typically no difference in the presentation; just know that a fragility fracture may be present in this situation.

Pharmacotherapy: Goals & Overview

The goal of pharmacotherapy is to reduce fractures. Pharmaceutical companies test drugs that increase bone mineral density, but an increase in bone density does no good if it does not reduce fractures. That is why, when looking at the effects of these drugs, you want to see how well they reduce fractures.
  • Prevention mainly consists of calcium and vitamin D supplementation.
  • Treatment consists of either promoting bone formation or decreasing bone resorption.
  • The most common drugs (the ones covered) decrease bone resorption.
  • At this time there is only one drug that promotes bone formation. It is a new class of drugs that focuses on the parathyroid. The instructor will not cover it because it is still not commonly used.
  • Drug groups covered: bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), and hormone therapy (which the lecturer says includes calcitonin).

Bisphosphonates: Alendronate (Fosamax)

  • Alendronate is "your first drug" and the first-line drug class for osteoporosis.
  • Reduces fractures by about 50%.
  • Used for both prevention and treatment.
  • Binds permanently to the surface of the bones and inhibits osteoclast activity, so bone breakdown decreases. The true mechanism of how it works is not really well known.
  • Adverse effects: GI effects are common; esophageal ulcerations.
  • It is typically now given only once a week, so patients do not have to think about it every day.
  • The instructor stresses that the low bioavailability is what causes many of the teaching points.
Safety / administration teaching for alendronate: tell patients they do not want to lie down right after taking it; they want to stay sitting up (standing) for a while, because it can cause esophageal ulcerations. Do not take it with calcium because it has low bioavailability (only about 1%), and even a glass of juice or coffee can cut that 1% by about 60%. So tell people to take it with one sip of water in the morning and make sure they stay up standing so it does not cause esophageal ulcerations.

SERMs: Raloxifene (Evista)

SERM = selective estrogen receptor modulator. The one the instructor says you need to know is raloxifene (Evista).

  • Works by mimicking estrogen: it produces estrogen-like effects on bone by binding to estrogen receptors.
  • Reduces bone turnover and increases bone density, but not as well as bisphosphonates.
  • This drug is safer than using estrogen (hormone replacement).
  • Used to prevent and treat postmenopausal osteoporosis only; not everybody will get it. Bisphosphonates are definitely the first-line drugs.
  • In one study it reduced the risk of spinal fractures by 50% but not any other types of fractures, including hip.
  • Side effects: hot flashes and leg cramping, and there is a stroke risk.
  • You must take adequate calcium and vitamin D replacement for SERMs to work.
Raloxifene clot / stroke risk: there is a stroke risk. Discontinue at least 72 hours before a planned procedure, because the drug mimics estrogen and there is an increased risk of clotting. Any prolonged immobilization period or high risk of blood clotting is problematic. "That's why we also say not to smoke or drink." It cannot be used if the patient is pregnant.

Calcitonin

The instructor covers calcitonin "quickly." Note that in this lecture calcitonin is described as part of hormone therapy.

  • Not used for long-term therapy; it is not as effective as SERMs or bisphosphonates for long-term therapy, but you may see it used short term.
  • Inhibits bone removal by osteoclasts.
  • Treatment only, not prevention.
  • May provide an analgesic effect in patients with acute vertebral fractures.
  • Has been shown to reduce spinal fractures only, and the benefits come only after taking it for 5 years.
  • It can also reduce pain in someone who has a hip fracture, which is a unique feature compared to the other drugs: you will not see that with a SERM or a bisphosphonate.
  • Increases spinal bone density, but it is not a first-line drug: it is expensive, and bisphosphonates are much more effective.
  • It is typically given intranasal, so it can cause nasal irritation.

Hormone Replacement Therapy

  • HRT is no longer commonly used to treat osteoporosis because of its risks: blood clots, breast cancer, and other cancers.
  • Remember that post-menopause is related to the drop in estrogen, so estrogen replacement is sometimes used in the post-menopausal period because of other issues.
  • However, estrogen/hormone replacement therapy is not recommended in osteoporosis because those risks are just too high.

Why do women reach the fracture threshold earlier than men, and how much more bone do they lose over a lifetime?
Women lose more bone in the early post-menopausal years (then continue to lose at a gradual rate) and have a lower peak bone density than men. Over a lifetime, women lose about 35% of cortical bone and 50% of trabecular bone; men lose only about two-thirds of this amount (about a third less).
At what age does bone resorption by osteoclasts begin to exceed bone formation by osteoblasts, and what is the result?
Beginning at age 30. The result is a steady decrease in bone mass with age (seen in men as well as women).
How is osteoporosis defined, and what test is used to measure it?
A bone mineral density 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass, measured with a DEXA scan and reported as a T-score.
Give the T-score cut-offs for normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis.
Normal: −1 or greater. Osteopenia: between −1 and −2.5 (low bone mass). Osteoporosis: less than −2.5.
When is osteoporosis called "severe," and what is a fragility fracture?
Severe is diagnosed when there is a history of a fragility fracture. A fragility fracture is when the person fell from standing and broke a bone (no traumatic accident); the bone broke because it is so porous.
What is osteopenia, and in which bones is osteoporosis most common?
Osteopenia is the stage before osteoporosis, with thinning of the trabecular matrix (T-score between −1 and −2.5). Osteoporosis is most common in the hip, vertebrae, and wrist bones, described as trabecular bones.
List the risk factors for osteoporosis given in lecture.
Aging; female sex (especially post-menopausal); Caucasian or Asian race (Caucasians about 20% likelihood over age 50); history of an adult fracture; genetics/family history (a first-degree relative with a fragility fracture is a very high risk); low body weight; thin, small frame; smoking; alcohol; long-term corticosteroids; no weight-bearing exercise; lack of calcium or vitamin D; eating disorders and gastric bypass (malnutrition); lack of estrogen or testosterone; excessive caffeine.
Why is long-term corticosteroid therapy a major risk factor for osteoporosis?
Because it inhibits osteoblast function (the build-up of bone when there is breakdown).
Calcitonin: place in therapy, benefits, route, and side effect?
Placed by the lecturer under hormone therapy. Not first line and not for long-term therapy (may be seen short term); treatment only, not prevention. Inhibits bone removal by osteoclasts. Reduces spinal fractures only, with benefit only after 5 years; may be analgesic in acute vertebral fractures and can reduce pain in a hip fracture (unique). Expensive; bisphosphonates are much more effective. Given intranasal, so it can cause nasal irritation.
Describe the pathogenesis of osteoporosis in terms of osteoclasts and osteoblasts.
Bone remodeling (resorption and formation) goes on for life, with about 10% of bone tissue replaced each year. Osteoclasts break bone down (via enzymes that dissolve bone); osteoblasts build the protein matrix. In osteoporosis there is increased osteoclast activity and decreased osteoblast activity, so new bone cannot be made and there is too much resorption.
What do patients with osteoporosis look like early versus later?
Early there are really no manifestations, so risk-factor identification is used and high-risk patients get DEXA scans earlier. Later: fractures, pain, loss of height, and stooped posture like kyphosis.
What do hip fractures do to mortality and independence, and what actually causes the deaths?
Risk of death increases about 2.5 to 4 times, and independent living is usually lost (often ending in a nursing home or care facility). Deaths come not from the fracture itself but from immobility-related complications: sepsis, skin breakdown, pneumonia, and blood clots.
Who most commonly gets osteoporotic hip fractures, and where do they occur?
Women older than 65 (because osteoporosis is much more common in women). The most common location is the proximal third of the femur.
How does a hip fracture present, and why is there little bruising?
Sudden onset of hip pain before or after a fall, loss of ability to walk (though a minimally impacted fracture may still bear weight for a while), severe groin pain, and an externally rotated and shortened leg. There is little bruising because the fracture is usually encapsulated. A fragility fracture may have no obvious trauma and only vague knee, groin, or thigh pain.
What is the goal of osteoporosis pharmacotherapy, and what do prevention and treatment consist of?
The goal is to reduce fractures (an increase in bone density does no good if it does not reduce fractures). Prevention is mainly calcium and vitamin D supplementation. Treatment either promotes bone formation or decreases bone resorption; decreasing resorption is the more common approach.
Compare the fracture reduction stated for alendronate, raloxifene, and calcitonin.
Alendronate: about 50% reduction in fractures. Raloxifene: 50% reduction in spinal fractures in one study, but not other types including hip. Calcitonin: spinal fractures only, with benefit only after 5 years. (Only one bone-forming drug exists, a new parathyroid-focused class, and it is not covered because it is not commonly used.)
What elemental calcium range does the lecture give, and what does vitamin D do?
For a true calcium replacement (not calcium carbonate/Tums), elemental calcium is around 1200 to 2000 (the instructor says you do not need to memorize doses). Vitamin D increases the absorption of calcium, and calcium helps with bone sturdiness.
Alendronate (Fosamax): class, benefit, mechanism, administration teaching, and safety concern?
First-line bisphosphonate for prevention and treatment; reduces fractures by about 50%. It binds permanently to bone surfaces and inhibits osteoclast activity. Bioavailability is only about 1% (juice or coffee cuts it by about 60%), so no calcium with it, take it with one sip of water in the morning, typically once a week. Do not lie down right after taking it; stay sitting up/standing, because it can cause esophageal ulcerations (GI effects are common).
Why is hormone replacement therapy not recommended for osteoporosis?
It is no longer commonly used because of its risks (blood clots, breast cancer, and other cancers); those risks are just too high. Estrogen replacement is sometimes used post-menopause for other issues, but it is not recommended in osteoporosis.
Raloxifene (Evista): how does it work, what fractures does it reduce, and what are its side effects and precautions?
A SERM that mimics estrogen (binds estrogen receptors), reduces bone turnover and increases bone density, but not as well as bisphosphonates. In one study it reduced spinal fractures by 50% but not other types, including hip. Side effects: hot flashes, leg cramping, and stroke risk. Needs adequate calcium and vitamin D; stop at least 72 hours before a planned procedure (increased clotting risk because it mimics estrogen); no smoking or drinking; not for pregnancy.
