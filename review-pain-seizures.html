Pain & Seizures

NUR 325 review, covering: pain pathophysiology (nociception, pain classification), non-opioid and adjuvant analgesic pharmacology (NSAIDs, acetaminophen, tramadol, gabapentin/pregabalin), opioid pharmacology (morphine, hydromorphone, fentanyl, and more; naloxone), and seizure pathophysiology/pharmacology (classification, status epilepticus, antiepileptic drugs).

Pain Basics — Definition, Neuroanatomy & Nociceptors

As a nurse, pain is whatever the patient says it is — a complex experience shaped by physical, cognitive, spiritual, emotional, and environmental factors, plus previous experience and genetics. Acute pain is protective: it promotes withdrawal from a painful stimulus (pulling a hand off a hot stove) and teaches avoidance. Chronic pain lasts months to years, is not protective, and can develop from acute pain.

Three parts of the nervous system are involved in pain: afferent pathways (begin in the peripheral nervous system, travel to the spinal cord and up into the cortex), interpretive centers (cortical/subcortical brain areas that interpret the sensation as pain), and efferent pathways (descending messages that produce the physical/mental response — e.g., pulling a hand away). This whole process is called nociception.

Nociceptors

  • Pain receptors — free nerve endings in the afferent peripheral nervous system. Stimulation causes nociceptive pain.
  • Respond to mechanical (pressure/distortion), thermal (heat/cold), and chemical (acids, inflammatory chemicals) stimuli — but only once a stimulus reaches a certain intensity/threshold.
  • Unevenly distributed — dense in skin, dental pulp, meninges, and some internal organs (more acute pain felt there); sparse in the brain, alveoli, and deep tissue (less acute pain felt there).

The Physiology of Pain — Four Phases & Nerve Fiber Types

Neurotransmitters modulate pain transmission — excitatory ones (norepinephrine, acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin) enhance/transmit pain signals and sensitize nociceptors; inhibitory ones (GABA — gamma-aminobutyric acid) suppress it. Endorphins are endogenous opioids produced in the brain that dull/inhibit pain — the name is short for "endogenous morphine," since morphine mimics their effect.

PhaseWhat Happens
TransductionA painful stimulus is converted to an action potential at the sensory receptor. Chemical mediators (e.g., prostaglandins) released with injury/inflammation lower the pain threshold.
TransmissionThe pain impulse conducts along A-delta and C fibers into the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, then up through the brain to the thalamus.
PerceptionThe message reaches the somatosensory cortex — conscious awareness of pain's location/intensity. Influenced by attention, distraction, anxiety, fear, fatigue, prior experience, genetics, age, and culture.
ModulationSynaptic transmission of the pain signal is amplified or dampened — e.g., by endorphins closing spinal cord "gates," similar to how opioid analgesics work.

A-delta Fibers

  • Small diameter (not the smallest); transmit sharp, well-localized, "fast" pain (stinging, cutting, pinching).
  • Can trigger a spinal reflex withdrawal before pain is even perceived (e.g., a needle stick).

C Fibers

  • Even smaller diameter, far more numerous; found in muscles, tendons, organs, and skin.
  • Transmit dull, aching, burning, poorly-localized pain — often constant.

Pain Threshold, Tolerance & the Gate Control Theory

TermDefinition
Pain ThresholdThe lowest intensity of pain a person can recognize. Has "perceptual dominance" — intense pain at one site can raise the threshold (mask pain) elsewhere. Raised by stress, exertion, acupuncture, and sexual activity.
Pain ToleranceThe greatest intensity of pain a person can handle — varies widely between and within people. Decreased by repeated pain exposure, fatigue, anger, boredom, apprehension, sleep deprivation. Increased by alcohol, persistent opioid use, hypnosis, distraction, or strong faith beliefs.
Opioid ToleranceAn adaptation from opioid exposure that changes drug receptors over time — does not by itself indicate addiction.

The gate control theory: stimulating large-diameter nerve fibers (A-alpha, A-beta) via touch, massage, distraction, or activity can "close the gate" on pain signals traveling through the smaller A-delta/C fibers, attenuating the pain signal before it reaches the thalamus/cortex. It doesn't always work, but it explains why instinctively rubbing a stubbed toe or bumped elbow can ease the pain.

Pain & Inflammation — Chemical Mediators

Tissue injury triggers chemical mediators (from plasma) that produce inflammation's signs — pain, swelling, redness, warmth, immobility. Key mediators: histamine and arachidonic acid metabolites (prostaglandins, leukotrienes). Prostaglandins promote inflammation, pain, and fever, but also protect the stomach lining, promote clotting (via platelet activation), and affect kidney function/blood vessel dilation. COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes break down arachidonic acid into prostanoids, which become prostaglandins — this is the pathway NSAIDs target.

Acute Pain

  • Sudden onset; resolves once the injury heals or mediators clear. Lasts seconds to under 3 months.
  • Stimulates the autonomic nervous system: increased heart rate/blood pressure, sweating, anxiety, dilated pupils.

Chronic Pain

  • Lasts > 3–6 months, beyond expected healing time; often out of proportion to observable tissue damage. Can be ongoing or intermittent (e.g., migraines).
  • Does not produce the same ANS response as acute pain, but causes significant psychosocial/emotional burden. Linked to CNS/peripheral nervous system sensitization changes.

Classifying Pain — Nociceptive vs. Neuropathic, Referred & Phantom Pain

TypeOriginCharacter
Nociceptive — Cutaneous/SomaticPain receptors in/around skin, joints, tendons, musculoskeletal system.Aching, constant; can be sharp/well-localized (A-delta) or dull/less-localized (C fibers, e.g., a broken bone).
Nociceptive — VisceralOrgans, often with inflammation; transmitted via C fibers.Poorly localized, deep, diffuse; cramping, splitting, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis. Often produces referred pain.
NeuropathicOriginates within the central nervous system — a lesion/injury to the nerves themselves; more associated with chronic pain.Shooting, burning, electric-shock, sharp, numbness, motor weakness; poorly localized. Often does not respond to usual pain medications. Examples: diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, phantom limb pain, trigeminal neuralgia.

Referred pain is felt at a distance from its actual source, since the brain can't always distinguish the origin — classically, chest/jaw/neck/shoulder pain during a myocardial infarction. Phantom pain originates at the site of an amputated limb — constant, most intense right after amputation, and generally resolves over time; once thought psychological, it's now understood to reflect inappropriate signals in the spinal cord and brain.

Atypical Analgesics — Tramadol, Gabapentin & Pregabalin

DrugDetail
Tramadol (Ultram)A "miscellaneous analgesic" — binds weakly to mu opioid receptors and inhibits norepinephrine/serotonin reuptake. PO only (plain, combined with acetaminophen, or extended-release). Used for moderate-severe pain. Mild side effects: drowsiness, dizziness, headache, nausea, constipation, respiratory depression. Use cautiously with other CNS depressants (SSRIs, MAOIs, TCAs) — lowers the seizure threshold, raising seizure risk in combination.
Gabapentin (Neurontin) & Pregabalin (Lyrica)Technically anticonvulsants, used for pain management — specifically neuropathic pain. Mechanism not fully known; thought to suppress neuronal firing. Complement/enhance opioid analgesia and can help manage some opioid side effects. Adverse effects: drowsiness, dizziness, visual problems; an overdose is only partially reversed by naloxone. Gabapentin has a ceiling effect (no added benefit above ~1,800 mg/day); pregabalin does not.

NSAIDs — COX-1 vs. COX-2 Mechanism

NSAIDs are anti-prostaglandins — they block the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzyme needed to produce prostaglandins. Used for mild-moderate pain (headache, myalgia, arthritis, post-op pain), inflammation, and fever.

EnzymeRoleEffect of Blocking It
COX-1Protects gastric mucosa; needed for thromboxane synthesis (platelet aggregation) — largely beneficial, "housekeeping" prostaglandins.Loses GI protection and platelet function — the source of NSAID GI/bleeding side effects.
COX-2Responsible for inflammation and fever.Stops inflammation/fever — the therapeutic effect, without necessarily disrupting COX-1's protective roles.

Non-selective NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, ketorolac) block both COX-1 and COX-2 — effective, but with the full GI/bleeding side-effect profile. Selective COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib/Celebrex) block only COX-2, preserving COX-1's GI/platelet protection — about 87% lower risk of GI ulcer with long-term use, but a higher risk of cardiovascular thrombotic events (heart attack, stroke) as a trade-off. Aspirin is unique: it's an irreversible COX-1 inhibitor in platelets, reducing thromboxane formation — its antiplatelet effect. The other NSAIDs lack this antiplatelet property.

NSAIDs — Individual Drugs, Black Box Warnings & Reye Syndrome

DrugDetail
AspirinNon-selective, irreversible. Combined with caffeine = Excedrin; enteric-coated forms exist (never crush). Antithrombotic (more so than ibuprofen/naproxen). Not part of the NSAID cardiovascular black box warning (unlike the others).
IbuprofenNon-selective — easiest on the stomach of the non-selective NSAIDs; the most commonly used.
NaproxenNon-selective — hardest on the kidneys, but longer-acting than ibuprofen (once-daily dosing).
Ketorolac (Toradol)Non-selective — the most potent NSAID, given IV/IM (rarely PO). Analgesic effect comparable to morphine without the addiction/respiratory-depression risk — useful for opioid-avoidant or opioid-addicted patients. Very hard on kidneys; limit to 5 days or less.
Celecoxib (Celebrex)Selective COX-2 inhibitor — less GI toxicity than non-selective NSAIDs (not zero), higher cardiovascular thrombotic risk.

Since 2006, the FDA requires NSAID black box warnings for cardiovascular risk (all except aspirin) and GI risk (bleeding/ulceration, can be fatal — watch elderly patients and long-term users especially). Aspirin also carries its own risk of salicylate poisoning/toxicity: acute toxicity presents as nausea, vomiting, seizures, cerebral edema; chronic toxicity as nausea, vomiting, tinnitus, and hearing loss.

Reye syndrome: never give aspirin to children — especially children under 15 with a viral/flu-like illness. It's rare but causes severe swelling of the liver and brain, with a high mortality rate. Acetaminophen and ibuprofen are the appropriate choices for fever/pain in children instead.

Acetaminophen — Pharmacology, Dosing Limits & Toxicity

Acetaminophen (Tylenol/APAP) is not an NSAID — no anti-inflammatory activity, but does have analgesic and antipyretic effects (acts on the hypothalamus; heat dissipates via vasodilation). Mechanism is largely unknown, thought to decrease CNS prostaglandin synthesis. Used for mild-moderate pain and fever. Has a ceiling effect around 1,000 mg. Preferred over NSAIDs for patients on anticoagulants, those prone to GI upset, and children with viral illness (no Reye syndrome risk). IV acetaminophen reduces opioid requirements post-operatively and still counts toward the daily total.

Acetaminophen overdose causes hepatotoxicity — a toxic metabolite is normally detoxified by liver glutathione stores; an overdose depletes glutathione, letting the toxic metabolite accumulate and cause hepatic necrosis. Adult limit: 4 grams/24 hours (count combination products like Percocet/Norco/Lortab toward this total); patients with liver disease or chronic alcohol use should not exceed 2,000 mg/24 hours. Watch for jaundice, elevated LFTs, and creatinine. Antidote: acetylcysteine (Mucomist) — PO or IV (PO has a terrible odor, often better tolerated IV) — effective for acute overdose, not for chronic misuse/chronic liver failure.

Opioid Basics — Classes, Assessment & Respiratory Depression

All opioids are high-alert drugs, derived originally from the opium poppy; today mostly synthetic. Three chemical classes: morphine-like, meperidine-like, and methadone-like — a patient's allergy to one class doesn't necessarily apply to another. The pain-treatment approach escalates: non-opioid ± adjuvant → add an opioid to a non-opioid ± adjuvant → opioid ± non-opioid ± adjuvant for moderate-severe pain. Assess level of consciousness, blood pressure, pulse, and respirations before and periodically after every dose.

Respiratory depression is the biggest concern with any opioid — it's what kills in an overdose. If respiratory rate is < 10 breaths/minute, assess sedation level and use physical stimulation to try to arouse the patient; if significant stimulation is needed to arouse them, reduce the next opioid dose by about 25–50%. Initial drowsiness from a first dose diminishes with continued use. Opioid-naive patients (little/no prior opioid exposure — often elderly or young patients) have an exaggerated response and higher risk, so start at the lowest effective dose and favor PO over IV unless severe pain needs rapid control.

Individual Opioids — Morphine, Hydromorphone & Fentanyl

DrugDetail
MorphineThe prototype opioid — a mu-receptor agonist mimicking endogenous endorphins. Schedule II, high abuse potential. Given PO, IV, epidurally, or rectally; used for moderate-severe pain. MS Contin = extended-release (12–24 hr), good for chronic pain. Adverse effects: respiratory/CNS depression, constipation, drowsiness, fatigue, confusion, dry mouth, itching (a histamine-release pharmacologic effect, not a true allergy). Significant interaction with alcohol/CNS depressants. Typical starting doses: ~30 mg PO q3–4h, or 1–4 mg IV.
Hydromorphone (Dilaudid)Semi-synthetic, similar to morphine but 7× more potent (1 mg IV hydromorphone ≈ 7 mg IV morphine — typical doses 0.2–1 mg). Schedule II; PO or IV; used for severe pain. Same adverse effect profile as other opioids.
FentanylFully synthetic, extremely potent (0.1 mg IV fentanyl ≈ 10 mg IV morphine — dosed in micrograms). Used for moderate-severe (especially severe) pain, surgical induction, and chronic pain (transdermal patch, worn ~72 hr — not for opioid-naive patients, effective for cancer pain). PO, IV, IM, and transdermal routes. Its potency and cheap synthesis are why illicit fentanyl increasingly contaminates street drugs like heroin.

Individual Opioids — Meperidine, Codeine, Oxycodone, Hydrocodone & Methadone

DrugDetail
Meperidine (Demerol)Synthetic, Schedule II, weaker/shorter-acting than morphine with less respiratory depression — but rarely used today. A toxic metabolite accumulates with repeated dosing and can cause seizures, so it's not recommended for long-term/multiple-dose use. Seen occasionally in the ED for acute migraine or immediately post-op to stop shivering. Use cautiously in older adults/renal dysfunction.
CodeineSchedule II alone, Schedule III combined with acetaminophen. Used for mild-moderate pain, but more often as an antitussive (cough suppressant). PO only. Not given to children under 18 (life-threatening breathing problems). Strong nausea/GI upset is an expected effect, often mistaken by patients for an "allergy."
OxycodoneSchedule II, semi-synthetic derivative of codeine, ~10× more potent — PO only, for moderate-severe pain. High abuse potential (OxyContin = time-release form). Combined with acetaminophen = Percocet.
HydrocodoneFor mild-moderate pain and cough relief — about 6× more potent than codeine (less potent than oxycodone), usually combined with acetaminophen (Lortab, Norco, Vicodin). PO only.
MethadoneFully synthetic, Schedule II — the opioid of choice for opioid-addiction detoxification/treatment. Long half-life (dosed q12–24h vs. q4–6h for most opioids); still addictive, but with less euphoric "high." PO.

Naloxone, the Opioid-Naive Patient & Common Opioid Side Effects

Naloxone (Narcan) is an opioid antagonist — it binds mu receptors without activating them, blocking other opioids from doing so. No analgesic effect, and doesn't itself cause respiratory depression, which is why it's the drug of choice to reverse opioid-induced respiratory depression/suspected overdose (including heroin). Rapid onset (< 2 minutes); given IV, IM, or intranasally. Has a short half-life — repeat dosing (or a continuous drip) is often needed. Adverse effects from abrupt reversal: return of withdrawal symptoms, blood pressure changes, dysrhythmias, pulmonary edema, and the original pain returning abruptly ("with a vengeance").

Common Opioid Side EffectManagement
ConstipationVery common — proactively give stool softeners, encourage hydration/nutrition. Untreated, it can progress to paralytic ileus (especially post-op).
Nausea/VomitingCommon enough that some patients refuse opioids over it — may need a concurrent antiemetic.

Care should be collaborative — some patients decline opioids due to personal or family history of substance use disorder, fear, or simply disliking the effect; respect those preferences and consider adjuvants/alternatives. These side effects are much more common in opioid-naive patients than in those who take opioids regularly.

Seizure Terminology, Pathogenesis & Classification

TermDefinition
SeizureA brief episode of abnormal electrical activity in brain neurons — can have motor, sensory, and/or cognitive manifestations.
ConvulsionA more severe seizure with involuntary spasmodic muscle contractions.
EpilepsyA disease involving recurrent, paroxysmal seizure activity — requires more than one seizure with no evidence of a reversible metabolic cause.
Myoclonic SeizureBrief, shock-like jerks of a muscle/muscle group, often occurring in a row — easily mistaken for a tic, tremor, or clumsiness.

A seizure starts at the seizure focus — a group of neurons (often near scar tissue/gliosis) firing abnormally and autonomously, which can "recruit" nearby neurons and spread the seizure locally or globally. About 50% of seizures are primary/idiopathic (epilepsy, with a genetic predisposition but no identifiable cause). Secondary seizures have an identifiable cause — blood sugar imbalance, drugs, fever (febrile seizures, common in children), traumatic brain injury, stroke, meningitis, or tumors. EEG (electroencephalograph) monitors brain electrical activity to help diagnose seizures. The seizure threshold is a person's likelihood of having a seizure — raised by adequate sleep and taking AEDs as prescribed; lowered by many medications (bupropion, some antibiotics, tramadol, anesthetic gases), alcohol, menstruation, missed medication doses, stress, and illness.

Old TermCurrent Term
Partial SeizureFocal Seizure (one area of the brain)
Simple SeizureAware (patient's awareness intact during the seizure)
Complex SeizureImpaired Awareness
Grand Mal SeizureGeneralized Onset, Tonic-Clonic

Classification uses three features: where it originates (focal, generalized, or unknown), the patient's level of awareness, and other features like motor involvement. Generalized onset seizures involve both brain hemispheres at once. Subtypes: tonic-clonic (tonic phase = prolonged muscle contraction/cry; clonic phase = alternating contraction/relaxation, limb jerking) and absence seizures (brief, ~30-second loss of awareness with repetitive eye blinking — almost exclusively in childhood, rare after age 14). Focal onset seizures originate in one localized brain region and are further classified by awareness level.

Status epilepticus — a series of seizures without a postictal recovery period, or a single seizure lasting 30 minutes or more. A true emergency, especially with tonic-clonic seizures: it can progress to respiratory arrest, hypoxia, permanent brain damage, and death. Focal seizures can also progress into generalized ones if neuronal activity isn't down-regulated. Monitor and time every seizure.

Antiepileptic Drug (AED) Therapy — Principles

AEDs (also called anticonvulsants) aim to control/prevent seizures while maintaining quality of life — a constant risk/benefit balance, since these drugs carry serious side effects. About 70% of patients become seizure-free on a single AED; the other ~30% need multiple medications. Patients are typically on AEDs for life, though a provider may taper the dose after 1–2 seizure-free years under medical supervision. A single seizure usually doesn't start AED therapy (about 40% chance it won't recur) — treatment starts after recurrence.

Never abruptly stop an antiepileptic drug — doing so will cause seizures. AEDs also carry a black box warning for increased risk of suicidal ideation, worsening depression, and unusual mood changes — educate every patient starting one, and give them clear follow-up steps if these symptoms appear.

Therapy typically starts as monotherapy, slowly titrated with serum drug level monitoring to find the therapeutic range that balances seizure control against toxicity — providers switch to a different single drug rather than stacking multiple drugs if the first doesn't work well. AEDs work by (1) raising the seizure threshold, (2) limiting the spread of a seizure discharge, and (3) slowing nerve impulse conduction — many do this by raising GABA levels, since low GABA is linked to seizures. AEDs are generally teratogenic (higher birth defect risk) — pregnant patients with epilepsy need close OB/neurology co-management. Common side effects across AEDs: dizziness, drowsiness (CNS depression, worse with more than one AED), and GI upset.

Individual AEDs & Rapid Seizure Management

DrugDetail
Phenytoin (Dilantin)First-line for tonic-clonic and focal seizures. PO usually, IV for emergent use. Highly protein-bound (use cautiously with malnutrition, CKD, low albumin) and a CYP450 inducer, so many drug interactions. Therapeutic range ~10–20 mcg/mL; above that, toxicity (nystagmus, ataxia, dysarthria, encephalopathy). Distinctive adverse effects: gingival hyperplasia (prevent with good dental care — a classic exam topic), hirsutism, osteoporosis, and "Dilantin facies" (subcutaneous facial tissue hypertrophy).
Valproic Acid (Depakote)Works for essentially every seizure type (generalized, absence, myoclonic, tonic-clonic, and focal) — unusual versatility. Also used for bipolar disorder. PO or IV, highly protein-bound. Contraindicated in liver disease and urea cycle disorders. Adverse effects: hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis.
Topiramate (Topamax)Adjunct for focal and secondary generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Mechanism poorly understood (structurally similar to fructose). PO only. Watch for visual changes (can cause acute angle-closure glaucoma); can interact with contraceptives; increases cleft lip/palate risk in pregnancy.
Levetiracetam (Keppra)Adjunct for focal seizures (with or without generalization) — widely used inpatient, less so outpatient. Mechanism not well understood, generally well tolerated. PO or IV.

Rapid seizure management: most seizures stop spontaneously within about 2 minutes without intervention. For an actively epileptic or seizing inpatient, IV access is recommended for rapid medication administration; the inpatient gold standard is IV benzodiazepines (or rectal/other forms if IV access isn't available). Airway protection is the top priority — keep the head away from side rails. Also check blood glucose, since hypoglycemia is a common seizure cause. A single seizure doesn't automatically mean starting AEDs.

How do A-delta fibers and C fibers differ in the type of pain they transmit?
A-delta fibers transmit sharp, well-localized, "fast" pain (stinging, cutting, pinching) and can trigger a reflex withdrawal before pain is even perceived. C fibers are smaller, far more numerous, and transmit dull, aching, burning, poorly-localized pain that's often constant.
Name the four phases of nociception in order.
Transduction (painful stimulus becomes an action potential), transmission (impulse travels via A-delta/C fibers to the spinal cord and up to the thalamus), perception (conscious awareness in the somatosensory cortex), and modulation (the signal is amplified or dampened, e.g., by endorphins).
What's the difference between pain threshold and pain tolerance?
Pain threshold is the LOWEST intensity of pain a person can recognize (has "perceptual dominance" — pain at one site can mask pain elsewhere). Pain tolerance is the GREATEST intensity of pain a person can handle, and it varies widely between people and even within the same person over time.
How do you distinguish nociceptive pain from neuropathic pain?
Nociceptive pain arises from peripheral pain receptors responding to actual/impending tissue injury (cutaneous/somatic or visceral) and usually responds to standard pain medications. Neuropathic pain originates from a lesion/injury within the central nervous system itself, is more associated with chronic pain, and often does NOT respond to usual pain medications (shooting, burning, electric-shock qualities).
What's the key functional difference between COX-1 and COX-2, and why does that matter for NSAID side effects?
COX-1 produces largely protective prostaglandins (gastric mucosa protection, platelet aggregation via thromboxane). COX-2 produces the prostaglandins responsible for inflammation and fever. Non-selective NSAIDs block both (full therapeutic effect but GI/bleeding side effects); selective COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib) spare COX-1's protective effects but raise cardiovascular thrombotic risk instead.
Why is aspirin never given to a child with a viral illness?
Reye syndrome — a rare but serious, high-mortality condition causing swelling of the liver and brain, seen in children (especially under 15) taking aspirin during a viral/flu-like illness. Acetaminophen and ibuprofen are the appropriate alternatives.
What's the maximum daily acetaminophen dose for a healthy adult, and what's the antidote for overdose?
4 grams (4,000 mg) per 24 hours for a healthy adult — counting combination products like Percocet/Norco/Lortab; 2,000 mg/24 hr max for patients with liver disease or chronic alcohol use. The antidote for acute overdose is acetylcysteine (Mucomist), PO or IV — it restores the glutathione needed to detoxify the toxic metabolite before hepatic necrosis sets in.
A patient on IV morphine has a respiratory rate of 8. What's the nursing priority, and what happens to their next dose?
Respiratory depression is the biggest danger with any opioid. Assess sedation level and attempt physical stimulation to arouse the patient. If significant stimulation is needed to arouse them, the next opioid dose should be reduced by about 25-50%.
Rank morphine, hydromorphone, and fentanyl by potency, with the numeric relationship.
Fentanyl is the most potent (0.1 mg IV fentanyl ≈ 10 mg IV morphine — 100x), then hydromorphone (1 mg IV hydromorphone ≈ 7 mg IV morphine — 7x), then morphine as the baseline/prototype. This is why fentanyl doses are given in micrograms.
What does naloxone (Narcan) actually do, and what are three key facts about it?
Naloxone is an opioid antagonist — it binds mu receptors without activating them, blocking other opioids. Key facts: (1) it has NO analgesic effect of its own, (2) it does NOT cause respiratory depression, and (3) it has a SHORT half-life, so repeat dosing or a continuous drip is often needed to sustain reversal.
What two criteria must be met for someone to be diagnosed with epilepsy?
They must have had MORE THAN ONE seizure, and there must be NO evidence of a reversible metabolic cause (like a blood sugar imbalance, drug effect, or fever) — if a reversible cause explains the seizures, it isn't classified as epilepsy.
What is status epilepticus, and why is it dangerous? What's the cardinal rule about stopping an antiepileptic drug?
Status epilepticus is a series of seizures without a postictal recovery period, or a single seizure lasting 30 minutes or more — it can progress to respiratory arrest, hypoxia, permanent brain damage, and death. Separately: antiepileptic drugs must never be stopped abruptly — doing so will cause seizures, regardless of how well-controlled the patient has been.
