Blood Cells & CBC Interpretation

NUR 325 review, covering: red and white blood cell basics, CBC/CBC-with-differential interpretation, leukocytosis/leukopenia/neutropenia, hematopoietic growth factors, and white blood cell disorders (mononucleosis, myelodysplastic syndrome, leukemias, lymphomas, multiple myeloma). WBC-focused — doesn't overlap the RBC-focused Anemia/Polycythemia pages.

Red Blood Cells, Hemoglobin & Hematocrit

Erythrocytes (RBCs) are the most abundant blood cell, shaped like a biconcave disc (thick outer rim, thin center). They carry hemoglobin, which binds oxygen and delivers it to tissues — RBCs are the primary cell responsible for tissue oxygenation.

LabWhat It MeasuresNormal Range
RBC CountTotal erythrocytes in the blood.~4.2–6.2 million/mm³
Hemoglobin (Hgb)Amount of hemoglobin in the blood — reflects oxygen-carrying capacity. Low with bleeding, folate/B12 deficiency, certain cancers, kidney/liver disease. High with polycythemia, COPD, high altitude, heavy smoking.Males 14–18, females 12–16
Hematocrit (Hct)The percentage of blood volume made up of packed RBCs. Low with anemia, bleeding, or fluid overload (dilution). High with polycythemia, COPD, dehydration, shock, congenital heart disease — since hematocrit is a percentage, changes in plasma volume alone can shift it even if RBC mass hasn't changed.Males 40–52%, females 37–47%

Other RBC labs exist (mean corpuscular volume/MCV — RBC size; mean corpuscular hemoglobin/MCH — hemoglobin amount per RBC by weight) but are used less at the bedside — when an abnormal RBC lab comes back flagged, look it up and connect it to the clinical picture rather than memorizing every value.

White Blood Cells — Types & Function

Leukocytes (WBCs) exist to protect the body — immune function and inflammation. There are five types, and their percentages always add up to 100%.

"Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas" — Neutrophils, Lymphocytes, Monocytes, Eosinophils, Basophils: the five types of white blood cells.

Granulocytes

  • Have a nucleus with several lobes and granules that release inflammatory/immune mediators.
  • Neutrophils — most prevalent at a fresh injury/infection site; the first responders.
  • Eosinophils — role in phagocytosis tied to antigen-antibody reactions; rise with allergic reactions and parasitic infections.
  • Basophils — role in phagocytosis; rise with allergic/hypersensitivity and inflammatory reactions.

Agranulocytes

  • Fewer granules, don't release the same inflammatory mediators — but still participate in immune/inflammatory processes.
  • Lymphocytes (T-cells, B-cells) — primary immunity cells, usually reside in lymphoid tissue rather than circulating blood.
  • Monocytes/macrophages — potent phagocytosis, clean up cellular waste/debris; monocytes still secrete some cytokines despite being agranulocytes.
Eosinophils increase with "worms, wheezes, and weird diseases" — parasitic infections, allergic/asthma-type reactions, and other hypersensitivity conditions.

Neutrophils & Lymphocytes — Shift to the Left

Neutrophils come in two forms: segs (mature, "segmented" neutrophils) and bands (immature neutrophils). Neutrophils rise with acute bacterial infection and trauma — in the acute stage, the bone marrow releases more immature bands than usual, called a shift to the left (an increased proportion of bands relative to segs).

Lymphocytes rise with chronic bacterial infection and acute viral infection — in chronic bacterial infection, T- and B-cells act as memory cells; in acute viral infection, they act as active fighters. Think: neutrophils for something acute and fast-resolving (like strep throat), lymphocytes for something chronic and long-standing (like a long-term MRSA wound) or a viral illness.

CBC with Differential — Interpreting Percentages vs. Absolute Counts

A standard CBC reports WBC count, RBC count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelets. A CBC with differential ("diff") goes further, breaking the total WBC count into the proportion of each of the five WBC types — those five percentages always add up to 100%.

TermMeaning
LeukocytosisTotal WBC count above normal (normal ≈ 5,000–10,000/mm³).
LeukopeniaTotal WBC count below normal.
NeutropeniaLow neutrophil count specifically, measured by the absolute neutrophil count (ANC).

Because the "diff" percentages are relative to one another, a rising percentage in one WBC type mathematically pushes another type's percentage down — even if that second type's absolute count hasn't changed at all. Example: WBC count 10,000, neutrophils 60% (6,000) and lymphocytes 30% (3,000). If neutrophils rise and the WBC count climbs to 20,000 with neutrophils now 75% (15,000), lymphocytes might still be 3,000 in absolute terms — but their share is now only 15% of the larger total. Always interpret the diff alongside the total WBC count, not the percentages alone.

Leukocytosis, Leukopenia & Neutropenia

The total WBC count reflects how intense an immune/inflammatory process is — a large elevation suggests a bigger process than a modest one. Fever often accompanies infection and is a protective mechanism (not a disease itself) — it can slow microorganism growth, improve immune response, and decrease bacterial virulence.

Neutropenia (ANC < 1,000, most often from cancer treatment — chemo or radiation) leaves a patient with little to fight infection, making even a routine infection potentially life-threatening. Neutropenic precautions: meticulous hand hygiene, avoid contact with anyone sick, avoid raw fruits/vegetables/grains, no fresh flowers, and keep the patient's door shut in the hospital. The first several precautions apply at home too.

Hematopoietic growth factors (granulocyte colony-stimulating factors, G-CSF) treat significant leukopenia/neutropenia: filgrastim (Neupogen) is a leukopoietic growth factor made via recombinant DNA technology that promotes granulocyte proliferation, differentiation, and activation — used for chemo-induced leukopenia, malignancy, bone marrow transplant/stem cell harvesting, and chronic neutropenia; given IV or (more often) subcutaneously; common adverse effect is bone pain (makes sense — bone marrow is where these cells are made), and it can also cause leukocytosis. Peg-filgrastim is a long-acting derivative (half-life ~17 hours, delayed renal excretion) allowing less frequent dosing, with the same bone-pain effect.

Infectious Mononucleosis

A self-limiting, lymphoproliferative infection of B lymphocytes, most often caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). Most common in adolescents/young adults (college students) — about 1 in 4 teens/young adults who get EBV develop mono. Spread mainly through saliva ("the kissing disease"), but also through other bodily fluids (blood, semen) via sexual contact, blood transfusion, or organ transplant. Long, insidious incubation (4–8 weeks) with nonspecific early symptoms, so it's often not recognized right away.

TopicDetail
Clinical ManifestationsLymphadenopathy (swollen neck/axillary nodes), hepatitis, splenomegaly, extreme fatigue, fever, sore throat, body aches.
DiagnosisElevated WBCs, ~95% lymphocytes.
CourseAcute phase ~2–3 weeks; full resolution takes 2–3 months. Avoid contact sports during this time (splenic rupture risk from splenomegaly).
TreatmentNo specific treatment — supportive care (antipyretics, rest, hydration). No vaccine; risk reduction means not sharing food/drinks, kissing, or personal items like toothbrushes with an infected person.

Myelodysplastic Syndrome

A group of related hematologic disorders — also called bone marrow failure disorder — where blood cells made in the bone marrow fail to mature properly, accumulate abnormally in the marrow, or have a shortened lifespan. Most common in older adults (65+). Etiology is largely unknown, though some people may have a genetic predisposition triggered by environmental exposures (radiation, chemotherapy, long-term benzene exposure).

Hallmark finding: cytopenias (low counts across blood cell types), which can produce anemia, infection, and spontaneous bleeding/bruising. Diagnosed via labs and bone marrow biopsy. Treatment depends on severity — mild/chronic low-grade anemia may need no treatment; more severe disease may use a hematopoietic growth factor like filgrastim; severe cases may need chemotherapy or bone marrow transplant.

Leukemias — Acute vs. Chronic, Lymphocytic vs. Myelocytic

Leukemias are malignant neoplasms of immature, undifferentiated cells derived from a single hematopoietic cell line — these leukemic cells proliferate in the bone marrow, circulate in the blood, and infiltrate the spleen/lymph nodes. Classified two ways: by cell line (lymphocytic — B cells, T cells, plasma cells — vs. myelocytic — myeloid stem cells) and by pace (acute vs. chronic).

TypeDetail
Acute Lymphocytic Leukemia (ALL)Most common childhood leukemia (~80% of childhood acute leukemias); peak age 3–7. 5-year survival: ~71.7% overall, ~91.9% in children.
Acute Myelocytic Leukemia (AML)Primarily an adult disease (median age 64; ~80% of adult acute leukemia cases). 5-year survival: ~29.4% adults, ~68.7% children.
Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL)Most common leukemia in older adults; ~30% of all U.S. leukemia cases. Signs: fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, recurrent infections (leukemic B cells don't make normal antibodies). 5-year survival: ~88.2%.
Chronic Myelocytic Leukemia (CML)~15% of U.S. leukemia cases; typical onset age 40–50 (can occur in children). High granulocyte count and splenomegaly; signs include fatigue, weight loss, diaphoresis, bleeding, abdominal discomfort. 5-year survival: ~69.7%.

Acute leukemias have a sudden, "stormy" onset with signs tied to decreased mature WBCs/RBCs/platelets — bone pain, bruising, fever, fatigue, infection, unexplained pain (in children, sometimes a sudden refusal to walk). Diagnosed when immature WBCs make up 60–100% of marrow cells. Chronic leukemias have an insidious onset, often discovered incidentally on routine labs.

Treatment aims for remission: cytotoxic chemotherapy first (to destroy rapidly dividing cells), followed in some cases by bone marrow/stem cell transplant (donor types: volunteer donor, identical twin, or the patient's own cells; typically harvested from the anterior/posterior iliac crest). Transplant risks: graft-versus-host reaction (the graft attacks host tissue as foreign), infection, and relapse.

Malignant Lymphomas — Hodgkin vs. Non-Hodgkin

Lymphomas are neoplasms of cells derived from lymphoid tissue.

Hodgkin Disease (Hodgkin Lymphoma)

  • ~30% of malignant lymphomas; occurs across all ages.
  • Painless, progressive, rubbery enlargement of a single lymph node or group — most often cervical (neck).
  • Diagnostic hallmark: Reed-Sternberg cells (originate from B cells) on biopsy.
  • Spreads/grows in a predictable pattern — this predictability supports a relatively good prognosis.
  • Other findings: fever, night sweats, weight loss, malaise. Treated with chemotherapy, radiation, or stem cell transplant.

Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma

  • No Reed-Sternberg cells. Can arise from lymph nodes or any lymphoid tissue; most common in older adults.
  • Spread is unpredictable and often early to liver, spleen, bone marrow — less certain prognosis, often diagnosed at a later, more widespread stage.
  • Painless lymph node enlargement plus extranodal involvement (skin, GI tract, bone/bone marrow), fever, night sweats, weight loss, malaise, itchiness.
  • Treated with chemotherapy, radiation, stem cell transplant (refractory cases), or immune-modulating drugs (e.g., rituximab).

Multiple Myeloma

Also called plasma cell myeloma — a malignant disorder of mature, antibody-secreting B lymphocytes (plasma cells). Produces abnormal immunoglobulins/fragments, including a monoclonal antibody (M protein), and increases osteoclast activity, causing bone destruction/reabsorption. Impairs humoral immunity (antibody production is disrupted) and forms multiple tumor sites within bone.

TopicDetail
EpidemiologyTypically diagnosed at age 65+; more common in African American/Black patients than white/Caucasian patients. Unknown etiology.
PresentationSlow, insidious onset. Bone pain is usually the first symptom (sometimes discovered via a fracture or back pain workup); also anemia, recurrent infections, and bleeding (bone marrow depression); hypercalcemia (from bone breakdown via increased osteoclasts, releasing stored calcium).
DiagnosisLabs, imaging, bone marrow exam. Findings: monoclonal antibody protein in serum/urine, pancytopenia (all blood counts low), hypercalcemia, Bence Jones protein (urine), elevated serum creatinine (kidney involvement), and osteolytic lesions on X-ray (spine, ribs, skull, long bones).
TreatmentRanges from watchful waiting to steroids, chemotherapy, biologic therapy, stem cell transplant, and bisphosphonates; ensure adequate hydration.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the difference between hemoglobin and hematocrit, and what does each measure?
Hemoglobin measures the amount of the oxygen-carrying protein in the blood (reflects oxygenation capacity). Hematocrit measures the PERCENTAGE of blood volume made up of packed red blood cells — since it's a percentage, changes in plasma volume alone (like fluid overload or dehydration) can shift hematocrit even without a true change in red cell mass.
Name the five types of white blood cells and which two categories they fall into.
Granulocytes: neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils. Agranulocytes: lymphocytes, monocytes. Mnemonic: "Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas" (Neutrophils, Lymphocytes, Monocytes, Eosinophils, Basophils).
A CBC with diff shows an increase in bands. What does this indicate, and what is this pattern called?
An increase in bands (immature neutrophils) relative to segs (mature neutrophils) is called a "shift to the left" — it indicates an acute bacterial infection, since the bone marrow is releasing immature neutrophils faster than usual to respond.
A patient's total WBC count rises from 10,000 to 20,000, and their lymphocyte percentage drops from 30% to 15% — but the absolute lymphocyte count is still 3,000 in both cases. How is this possible?
Diff percentages are relative to the total WBC count and to each other. If neutrophils rise sharply (as in acute bacterial infection) and drive the total WBC count up, lymphocytes can hold the same ABSOLUTE count while their PERCENTAGE share drops — because the total pool got bigger, not because lymphocytes decreased. Always interpret the diff alongside the total count.
A patient's ANC is 800. What is this called, why is it dangerous, and what precautions are needed?
This is neutropenia (ANC < 1,000) — most often from chemo/radiation. It's dangerous because the patient has little ability to fight infection, making even routine infections potentially life-threatening. Neutropenic precautions: meticulous hand hygiene, avoid contact with sick people, avoid raw fruits/vegetables/grains, no fresh flowers, and keep the hospital door shut.
What does filgrastim (Neupogen) do, and what is its most common adverse effect?
Filgrastim is a granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) that promotes proliferation, differentiation, and activation of granulocyte-producing cells — used for chemo-induced leukopenia and related conditions. Most common adverse effect: bone pain (makes sense, since bone marrow is where these cells are produced).
A college student presents with extreme fatigue, sore throat, swollen cervical lymph nodes, and splenomegaly, several weeks after a mild illness. What should be suspected, and what activity should be restricted?
Infectious mononucleosis (usually from Epstein-Barr virus) — the long incubation period (4-8 weeks) explains the delay. Contact sports should be avoided due to splenomegaly and the risk of splenic rupture. There is no specific treatment or vaccine — care is supportive (rest, hydration, antipyretics).
What's the key difference between acute and chronic leukemias in how they present?
Acute leukemias have a sudden, "stormy" onset with symptoms from decreased mature white cells, red cells, and platelets (bone pain, bruising, fever, infection). Chronic leukemias have an insidious onset and are often found incidentally on routine labs, without dramatic symptoms.
What cell type is diagnostic for Hodgkin disease, and how does its spread pattern differ from non-Hodgkin lymphoma?
Reed-Sternberg cells (originating from B cells) are diagnostic for Hodgkin disease. Hodgkin disease spreads in a predictable pattern (supporting a better prognosis), while non-Hodgkin lymphoma spreads unpredictably and often early to the liver, spleen, and bone marrow, giving a less certain prognosis.
A patient presents with new-onset back pain, is later diagnosed with a vertebral fracture, and workup reveals hypercalcemia and Bence Jones protein in the urine. What condition fits, and why does hypercalcemia occur?
Multiple myeloma. Malignant plasma cells invade bone and increase osteoclast activity, breaking down bone and releasing stored calcium into the bloodstream — causing hypercalcemia. Bone pain/fracture is often the first presenting symptom.
Why do eosinophils rise with both parasitic infections and allergic reactions?
Eosinophils play a role in phagocytosis related to antigen-antibody reactions, which is the shared mechanism behind both allergic responses and the body's response to parasites. Mnemonic: eosinophils rise with "worms, wheezes, and weird diseases."
What is myelodysplastic syndrome, and what is its hallmark lab finding?
A group of bone marrow failure disorders (most common in adults 65+) where blood cells fail to mature properly, accumulate abnormally, or have a shortened lifespan. The hallmark finding is cytopenias — low counts across blood cell types — which can cause anemia, infection, and spontaneous bleeding/bruising.
