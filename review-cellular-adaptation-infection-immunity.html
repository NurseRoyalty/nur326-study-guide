Cellular Adaptation, Infection & Immunity

NUR 325 review, covering: pathophysiology basics, causes and staging of disease, cellular adaptation, necrosis/gangrene, inflammation, adaptive immunity, and infection.

What Pathophysiology Is

Breaking Down the Word

  • Physiology is normal functioning — how the body works when there aren't any problems, at the molecular, cellular, and whole-organ-system level.
  • Pathology is the study of disease and injury — what abnormal processes are happening to disrupt the body from normal functioning.
  • Pathophysiology combines the two: the mechanisms of disease — how and why changes in body structure and function happen, and how those changes produce the signs and symptoms of a given disease.

Disease is a disruption in homeostasis — "homeo" (same) + "stasis" (balance/stability). This disruption can be physical (e.g., heart failure — the heart can't pump adequately, so the body doesn't get the blood/oxygen it needs), mental (e.g., chronic stress wearing down the body's compensatory mechanisms over time), or social (e.g., chronic social isolation, which is itself linked to disease).

DirectionCompensatory Response
Temperature RisesBlood vessels dilate (flushed/red skin) and sweat glands secrete fluid — both mechanisms lose heat to the environment, cooling the body back toward normal.
Temperature DropsBlood vessels constrict (pale skin) and sweat glands stop producing fluid, conserving heat. Shivering — involuntary muscle contraction — generates additional heat.

Causes of Disease — Intrinsic & Extrinsic Factors

TermDetail
Intrinsic FactorsCome from inside the body: genetics/genetic mutations, immune status (immunocompromised patients are more susceptible to disease), age, sex, autoimmune disorders (the body attacking itself), and over- or under-production of hormones/chemicals (e.g., insulin underproduction in type 1 diabetes, thyroid hormone imbalance).
Extrinsic FactorsCome from outside the body: pathogens (bacteria, viruses, parasites), environmental exposures (toxins, pollutants), lifestyle/behaviors (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol), and stressors that overwhelm the body's defenses.

The Epidemiologic Triangle (Epi-Triad)

  • Pathogen (agent): the causative factor responsible for disease — a bacteria, virus, or parasite.
  • Host: typically the human — susceptibility depends on genetics, age, and immune status.
  • Environment: the external conditions that influence how the pathogen and host interact (e.g., some bacteria can't survive heat, which is why certain foods are cooked to specific temperatures).
  • Disease risk emerges when all three components come together.

Identifying, Describing & Staging Disease

TermDetail
SignsObjective data — what can be seen, heard, or touched (e.g., wheezes on auscultation, a strong or weak pulse).
SymptomsSubjective data — what the patient reports (e.g., pain, nausea, dizziness).
DiagnosisPutting a name to the disease (e.g., pneumonia, heart failure, hypertension).
EtiologyThe cause of the disease — not always known.
PrognosisThe expected disease course or outcome — the likelihood of returning to baseline.
TermDetail
ExposureContact with the agent or pathogen that sets off the disease cascade.
Sudden OnsetSigns/symptoms start quickly (e.g., a myocardial infarction, an asthma attack).
Insidious OnsetSlow and gradual — patients often adjust to symptoms until the disease has progressed significantly before it's caught (e.g., a slow GI bleed).
Latent OnsetThe disease process is present but inactive, and can become active later (e.g., the chickenpox virus lying dormant before reactivating as shingles).
ProdromalEarly, nonspecific signs that a disease/condition is beginning (e.g., chills or a mild headache before a cold or flu fully sets in).
Remission / ConvalescenceRemission: the disease is no longer active. Convalescence: recovering from the disease, back toward baseline.
IdiopathicThe cause of the disease is unknown (e.g., idiopathic cardiomyopathy).
IatrogenicCaused by the healthcare field itself — a treatment, drug, or action (or inaction) caused the disease (e.g., a colon perforation during a colonoscopy).
ExacerbationA worsening of disease — often an acute decline in a chronic condition (e.g., an asthma attack triggered by allergen exposure).
Prefixes/suffixes worth knowing: hypo- (low) vs. hyper- (high); -penia (too few) vs. -cytosis (too many); -osis (a process/condition); -itis (inflammation, e.g. hepatitis); -pathy (disease, e.g. myopathy). Same root, opposite direction is a common exam trap.

Cellular Adaptation — Atrophy to Neoplasia

Cells adapt to survive stress or injury. Some adaptations are normal/physiologic (muscle growth from exercise, uterine growth in pregnancy); others are pathologic, usually triggered by things like radiation, medications, or lack of oxygen.

TermDetail
AtrophyCells shrink in size; the whole organ can become atrophic. Physiologic atrophy is a developmental problem at birth (an organ/gland that never develops properly). Pathologic atrophy comes from decreased workload, poor blood supply, nutritional deficiency, hormonal problems, prolonged immobility, or aging — e.g., a limb muscle shrinking after weeks in a cast. Atrophied cells have decreased protein synthesis (and sometimes increased protein breakdown) and decreased function.
HypertrophyCells get bigger (not more numerous), usually increasing function too. Typically from mechanical stimuli — repetitive stretching, chronic pressure, prolonged volume overload (e.g., quad muscle growth from repeated exercise is a normal, healthy hypertrophy). The heart and kidneys are most prone to a negative hypertrophy, usually from hypertension forcing the heart to pump harder over time.
HyperplasiaAn increase in the number of cells (cell size stays the same) — only cells capable of dividing can do this (commonly skin, intestinal epithelium, glandular cells). Usually pathologic — most often cancers or abnormal hormonal stimulation (endometrial hyperplasia, benign prostatic hyperplasia, thyroid hyperplasia/goiter). A few situations are normal, like breast lactating-cell hyperplasia during breastfeeding, or hyperplasia during wound healing.
TermDetail
DysplasiaAbnormal changes in the size, shape, or organization of mature cells — often follows atypical hyperplasia. Classified mild/moderate/severe, and is reversible (e.g., tobacco-related mouth/mucosal cell dysplasia can normalize after quitting). Dysplasia is not cancer, but can be a precursor, especially with chronic inflammation/irritation.
MetaplasiaA reversible replacement of one mature cell type with another, less-differentiated type — related to chronic irritation/inflammation, and more strongly associated with cancer risk than dysplasia alone. It's an adaptation that helps cells survive in a hostile environment, but can progress toward cancer.
Neoplasia / AnaplasiaCellular changes that no longer respond to normal regulatory processes — usually from a gene mutation. Anaplastic cells are cancer cells that have differentiated back to immature/embryonic forms; seeing "anaplasia" should signal a neoplasm/malignant tumor. True cancer is uncontrolled, rapid proliferation of these non-differentiated cells.

Benign Tumors

  • Differentiated cells, less anaplastic (more mature).
  • Reproduce faster than normal cells, but cannot metastasize — stay contained and grow slowly.
  • Usually encapsulated.
  • Mainly a problem if located somewhere sensitive to compression, like the brain.

Malignant Tumors

  • Very anaplastic, non-differentiated, immature cells.
  • Reproduce rapidly with abnormal cells.
  • Metastasize — spread to other sites, near or distant, depending on proximity to lymphatic/blood systems.
  • Typically not encapsulated.

Necrosis & Gangrene

TermDetail
NecrosisCell death from disease, injury, or failure of blood supply — irreversible, with inflammation, swelling, redness, and cell bursting in the area.
Ischemic NecrosisNecrosis from infarction and prolonged ischemia — an absence of blood supply to the area.
Liquefactive NecrosisDead cells/tissue are digested by enzymes and become liquefied, often forming an abscess with a liquid center. Typical in organs with a lot of lipids (lungs, brain) or where there's an abscess with heavy acute inflammatory cell activity.

Necrotic tissue becomes a breeding ground for bacteria — when bacterial invasion follows disruption of a major blood supply, that's gangrene. Most common in the lower extremities (toes, feet) of patients with poor circulation, such as those with peripheral vascular disease or diabetes.

TypeDetail
Dry GangreneMinimal bacteria, minimal inflammatory response. Tissue turns black, dry, and wrinkled. Spreads slowly (weeks to months); a clear demarcation line separates dead from healthy tissue.
Wet GangreneLiquefactive necrosis with extensive bacterial/WBC damage producing a liquid wound. Skin is moist, dark, cool, pulseless, and swollen, with blisters and a foul odor. No demarcation line; spreads much faster than dry gangrene and can cause systemic symptoms (tachycardia, fever, confusion, decreased kidney function) that can lead to death.
Gas GangreneA specialized, severe form of wet gangrene caused by Clostridium perfringens (an anaerobic, spore-forming organism found in soil), typically from severe trauma or a compound fracture. Destroys connective tissue and cellular membranes; characterized by gas bubbles from the spores. Very serious.

Inflammation Basics

What Inflammation Is

  • Occurs with cell injury, regardless of cause — initially a protective mechanism that begins healing, working to destroy invading/harmful agents, limit their spread, and prepare damaged tissue for repair.
  • The five cardinal signs: redness, swelling, heat, pain, and loss of function.
  • Exogenous causes come from outside (surgery, trauma, burns); endogenous causes come from inside (e.g., tissue ischemia).
  • Acute inflammation lasts less than 2 weeks and is helpful. Chronic inflammation extends much longer, can form scar tissue, and is no longer a helpful process.

Mechanism: tissue injury releases chemical mediators that cause vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, letting white blood cells (mainly neutrophils and monocytes) move out of the blood vessel and into the injured tissue — a recruitment process called chemotaxis. Once there, these cells perform phagocytosis — engulfing and destroying bacteria/debris.

Exudate TypeDetail
SerousMild inflammation — watery, low protein (e.g., clear fluid leaking from a healing surgical incision).
SerosanguineousSimilar to serous but pink-tinged, with small amounts of RBCs — normal immediately post-op.
PurulentSevere inflammation, typically bacterial infection — thick, sticky, high in neutrophils/protein/debris, often discolored and foul-smelling; may need drainage (an abscess).
HemorrhagicLarge amounts of RBCs — the most severe type, from badly damaged/necrotic blood vessels. Signals active bleeding, not just a wound.

If inflammation isn't contained, it can become systemic — driven by cytokines (interleukin-1, interleukin-6, TNF-alpha). Systemic inflammation presents as fever, an elevated white blood cell count, fatigue, and muscle catabolism (breakdown) — it can look a lot like infection, so finding an infection source is often what differentiates the two.

Adaptive Immunity — B Cells, T Cells & Antibodies

Self vs. Non-Self

  • The major histocompatibility complex (MHC), also called the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) in humans, is a gene cluster on chromosome 6 that makes "self" proteins displayed on nearly every cell surface.
  • Specific adaptive immunity recognizes foreign invaders, destroys them, and remembers the encounter for a faster response next time.
  • B cells and T cells are lymphocytes responsible for this memory immunity. B cells provide humoral immunity (antibodies in body fluids); T cells provide cell-mediated immunity (recognizing antigens on cell surfaces).
  • An antigen is essentially a "name tag" on a foreign/bad cell (cancer, bacteria, fungal, inflammatory) that marks it for attack.
  • Memory B cells remember a prior antigen exposure and respond rapidly on re-exposure. Plasma cells are short-lived B cells that secrete antibodies against the triggering antigen.
AntibodyDetail
IgGMost prevalent (~75–80% of all antibodies). Easily crosses into interstitial fluid; produced later in an infection (or after a vaccine) — protects against bacterial and viral infection.
IgM~10% of antibodies. First produced after exposure/immunization, activating complement — a signal that something is newly/acutely happening.
IgAFound in saliva, tears, secretions, breast milk, GI, and GU tract — protects at secretory/excretory sites.
IgDTrace amounts in serum and on B cell surfaces — stimulates B cells to multiply, differentiate, and secrete other immunoglobulins.
IgEPlays a major role in immunity against parasites and in allergic reactions (works with mast cells).
TermDetail
Passive ImmunityTransfer of antibody-containing plasma from a protected person to one who isn't — e.g., mother-to-fetus (IgG crosses the placenta; IgA is passed via breast milk), or injection of antibodies/immune globulins (not a vaccine).
Active ImmunityThe result of an active infection or a vaccine — the body creates its own antibodies (usually via memory B cells), typically much faster on a second exposure, which is how vaccines prevent illness.
Vaccine TypeDetail
TraditionalAn inactivated or killed part of the virus/bacteria/organism.
AttenuatedA weakened, still-live version — should not be given to immunocompromised patients or those with conditions putting them at risk for complications.
ToxoidAn inactivated toxin that stimulates antitoxin production (e.g., tetanus vaccine).
ConjugateA protein/toxoid from one organism attached to a disease-causing organism to stimulate a response (e.g., Hib).
mRNAUses a snippet of the virus's genetic code to teach the body's antibodies to respond.

Infection Basics — Microorganisms, Transmission & Portals of Entry

MicroorganismDetail
VirusDNA or RNA with a protein shell; requires a host cell to replicate. Responsible for COVID-19, influenza, chickenpox, and more.
BacteriaLarger than a virus, at least single-celled, usually with one strand of DNA; can reproduce inside or outside cells and suppress the body's defenses (e.g., strep throat, TB, UTIs).
FungalSpore-forming organisms (e.g., yeast infections).
ProtozoaTypically live in water environments (e.g., malaria).
HelminthsParasitic worms (roundworms, hookworms).
PrionsMisfolded proteins that attach to other proteins, even smaller than viruses — cause devastating diseases like Creutzfeldt-Jakob (mad cow) disease.
TransmissionDetail
Direct ContactKissing, sex, contact with contaminated soil/vegetation, or droplet spread (short-range aerosols from sneezing/coughing, e.g., pertussis, meningitis).
Indirect — AirborneDroplet nuclei stay suspended in air (e.g., measles).
Indirect — Vehicle-BorneInfectious material via food, water, or blood (e.g., hepatitis A from contaminated food).
Indirect — Vector-BorneCarried by another organism — mosquitoes, fleas, ticks (e.g., malaria, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Lyme disease).

Portals of Entry

  • Respiratory (airways/lungs) and GI (via eating/the stomach).
  • Genitourinary tract (e.g., STDs, catheters).
  • Skin — normally a strong barrier (tight cell junctions), but skin integrity issues are a major vulnerability. Translocation is the movement of bacteria across the intestinal lining (most often the peritoneal cavity) when the gut becomes "leaky."
  • Blood — transfusion contamination or needle sticks.
  • Maternal-fetal transmission — some organisms (e.g., Zika, Listeria) cross the placental barrier or transmit during childbirth.

Stages of Infection & the Infectious Process

StageDetail
IncubationTime from pathogen entry to first symptoms — no symptoms yet, just growth/multiplication.
ProdromalOnset of nonspecific symptoms (mild fatigue, low fever) — not yet fully symptomatic.
Acute StageFull-blown, specific signs/symptoms of the illness.
ConvalescenceSymptoms improving, illness disappearing.
ResolutionThe pathogen is completely eliminated. Some pathogens never reach this stage (e.g., chickenpox virus staying dormant for life).

The 6-Stage Infectious/Inflammatory Process

  • 1. Injury — the initial insult to the area.
  • 2. Brief vasoconstriction — stops bleeding, limits organism movement.
  • 3. Vasodilation — blood flows freely into the area, bringing the five cardinal signs of inflammation.
  • 4. Increased permeability — fluid leaks from vessels into tissue.
  • 5. Emigration of leukocytes & phagocytosis — neutrophils (plus eosinophils, NK cells, monocytes) migrate in and engulf invaders, producing exudate.
  • 6. Systemic symptoms (if the process doesn't stay localized) — a total-body response driven by the hypothalamic fever set point, which is ultimately protective (helps conserve heat, boosts immune cell/antibody function, and slows some bacteria).

Diagnosing Infection & Healthcare-Associated Infections

TermDetail
ColonizationA pathogen inhabits a body site without causing signs/symptoms of infection (e.g., asymptomatic staph on the skin). Can turn into an active infection with immune compromise or tissue disruption.
Gram StainIdentifies bacteria as gram-positive or gram-negative and their shape/arrangement. Gram-negative bacteria are more dangerous — an outer membrane/capsule hides their antigens, making them harder for the body to fight.
SensitivityTakes 24–72 hours; identifies the exact bacteria and which antibiotics it's sensitive/resistant to.
Urinalysis — Normal ValuespH 5–9; nitrates negative (positive suggests bacterial infection); leukocyte esterase negative (positive suggests infection); RBCs less than 5 (more suggests infection); epithelial cells suggest a contaminated sample (not infection).

Nosocomial (Healthcare-Associated) Infections

  • Acquired in the hospital, not present at home — typically more virulent and more likely to be drug-resistant.
  • MRSA — methicillin-resistant Staph aureus. CRE — carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (resistant to an entire antibiotic class). MDRO — multi-drug-resistant organism (resistant to multiple drug types).
  • Superinfection: a new infection that develops during treatment for another one — broad-spectrum antibiotics kill off normal flora that normally keep opportunistic pathogens in check.
SuperinfectionDetail
C. diff (Clostridium difficile)Normal intestinal flora killed by antibiotics lets C. diff overgrow — causes frequent, watery (often orange-tinged) diarrhea and abdominal cramping, sometimes months after treatment. Diagnosed by stool PCR (not culture — too slow). Requires contact isolation, gowns/gloves, and soap-and-water hand washing (alcohol sanitizer doesn't kill it). Treated with PO/IV metronidazole (Flagyl) or PO vancomycin; antidiarrheals are avoided so the toxin can clear. Can progress to life-threatening pseudomembranous colitis.
CandidiasisA yeast/fungal overgrowth after antibiotics kill normal skin/mucosal flora — thrush (oral), or infections in moist skin folds (under the breast, etc.). Untreated oral thrush can descend into the esophagus. Prevented/treated with nystatin (antifungal), including vaginal suppositories or topical powders/sprays.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the difference between physiology, pathology, and pathophysiology?
Physiology is normal body function. Pathology is the study of disease/injury. Pathophysiology combines both — the mechanisms of how and why disease disrupts normal function.
What's the difference between a sign and a symptom?
A sign is objective data (seen, heard, or touched by the clinician). A symptom is subjective data (what the patient reports).
What three components make up the epidemiologic triangle?
Pathogen (agent), host, and environment — disease risk emerges when all three come together.
What's the key difference between atrophy, hypertrophy, and hyperplasia?
Atrophy: cells shrink. Hypertrophy: cells get bigger (same number). Hyperplasia: more cells (same size) — only possible in cells capable of dividing.
What does seeing the word "anaplasia" signal, and how does it relate to dysplasia and metaplasia?
Anaplasia = cancer cells (immature/embryonic, uncontrolled growth). Dysplasia and metaplasia are earlier, reversible abnormal-cell changes that can (but don't always) progress toward that cancerous end point.
What distinguishes a benign tumor from a malignant one?
Benign: differentiated, more mature cells, cannot metastasize, usually encapsulated, grows slowly. Malignant: anaplastic, non-differentiated cells, reproduces rapidly, metastasizes, typically not encapsulated.
What are the five cardinal signs of inflammation, and what's the acute-vs-chronic time cutoff?
Redness, swelling, heat, pain, loss of function. Acute inflammation lasts less than 2 weeks; chronic extends longer and is no longer a helpful process.
Which antibody is first produced after a new exposure, and which is most prevalent overall?
IgM is first produced after a new exposure/immunization. IgG is the most prevalent overall (~75-80%) and protects against bacterial/viral infection.
What's the difference between passive and active immunity? Give an example of each.
Passive: transferred antibodies (e.g., mother-to-fetus via placenta/breast milk). Active: the body makes its own antibodies after infection or vaccination.
What's the difference between colonization and infection?
Colonization: a pathogen is present at a body site but causes no signs/symptoms. Infection: the pathogen causes actual clinical signs/symptoms and tissue damage.
What is a superinfection, and give one example?
A new infection that develops during treatment for another, because antibiotics killed off normal protective flora — e.g., C. diff colitis or candidiasis (thrush) after antibiotic treatment.
What do MRSA, CRE, and MDRO each stand for?
MRSA: methicillin-resistant Staph aureus. CRE: carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (resistant to an entire antibiotic class). MDRO: multi-drug-resistant organism (resistant to multiple drug types).
