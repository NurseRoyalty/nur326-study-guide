Part 1 — Renal Disorders

Pyelonephritis (Upper UTI / Kidney Infection)

TermDetail
DefinitionInflammation of one or both kidneys, focused on the upper urinary tract. Usually ascending — a lower UTI climbs up the urinary tract — but can also come from a systemic bloodstream infection reaching the kidney.
PathophysiologyE. coli is the most common pathogen. The kidney becomes swollen and fills with infectious exudate; if severe, this can compress the renal artery. The kidney can become necrotic or form abscesses.
Acute vs. ChronicUsually acute. Untreated or recurrent pyelonephritis can become chronic — a cause of chronic kidney disease and a risk for sepsis down the road.
Risk FactorsPregnancy, recurrent lower UTIs, and an antibiotic-resistant strain (a lower UTI treated with an antibiotic it's resistant to stays untreated and can ascend).
Lower UTIPyelonephritis (Upper UTI)
OnsetGradual — "do I have a UTI, do I not."Sudden — wakes up or develops within an afternoon.
How They LookOtherwise well.Look and feel systemically sick — this is a systemic infection.
Key FindingsDysuria, frequency, urgency.Fever, chills, CVA (costovertebral angle) tenderness over the back — plus the same frequency/urgency/dysuria, since the lower tract is usually still involved. Hematuria and nausea/vomiting/anorexia can occur with either.

Treatment

  • Community-acquired, low resistance risk: Bactrim (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) first-line, or ciprofloxacin. Nitrofurantoin (Macrobid) is used clinically but isn't on the drug matrix.
  • Uncomplicated community cases get a short course; recurrent UTIs get a more conventional 7–14 day course.
  • More severe cases may need inpatient IV antibiotics. A urinalysis and culture identify the organism and its sensitivity.
  • Pregnancy complicates treatment — Bactrim and ciprofloxacin are avoided, narrowing options right when pregnant patients are already at higher risk for pyelonephritis.
Urosepsis: in elderly or already-sick patients, a UTI (upper or lower) can progress to sepsis — a severe systemic response with a high mortality rate.

Urinary Obstruction

A blockage can occur anywhere along the urinary tract — the renal pelvis, the ureter, or the bladder/urethra — and whatever is downstream of the blockage backs up, the same way a clogged pipe backs up.

LevelMost Likely Cause
Renal PelvisKidney stones (renal calculi) — most common cause at this level.
UreterA stone traveling down, or external compression (e.g., a tumor).
Bladder / UrethraBPH is the classic cause in men (see Part 3).

Infection Risk

  • Stagnant urine behind an obstruction is a breeding ground for bacteria.
  • Raises the risk of UTI and pyelonephritis.

Back-Pressure Risk

  • Hydronephrosis (kidney swelling) and hydroureter (ureter swelling) from backed-up urine.
  • Prolonged pressure can permanently damage kidney tissue.
Trick — 3 things that determine how obstruction shows up: (1) site — where along the tract; (2) speed of onset — sudden vs. gradual; (3) internal vs. external — a stone inside the lumen vs. something pressing from outside.

Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)

Mechanism

  • Supersaturation of urine with stone-forming substances leads to crystal formation.
  • Stones always form at the kidney level, even if they cause symptoms lower down as they travel.
Risk FactorWhy It Matters
Low Fluid IntakeConcentrates urine, promoting supersaturation.
DietHigh sodium, oxalate, or animal protein intake raises risk depending on stone type.
Family History / Metabolic ConditionsPredisposes to specific stone types.
Recurrent UTIsCertain bacteria promote struvite stone formation.
Stone TypeDetail
CalciumMost common — about 70–80% of all kidney stones.
Struvite (Staghorn)Associated with recurrent UTIs from urease-producing bacteria; can grow to fill the renal pelvis (the "staghorn" shape).
Uric AcidAssociated with high-purine diets and conditions like gout.
CystineRare — less than 1% of stones; a genetic/metabolic cause.

Renal Colic & Treatment

  • Classic presentation: sudden, severe flank pain that can radiate to the groin, often with nausea/vomiting and hematuria.
  • Treatment goals: pain control, hydration to help pass the stone, and prevention tailored to the specific stone type (e.g., dietary changes, urine alkalinization/acidification depending on stone chemistry).

Urologic Cancers

Kidney Cancer

  • About 85% are renal cell carcinoma.
  • Smoking is the #1 risk factor.
  • No early manifestations — often found incidentally.
  • Late findings: CVA tenderness, hematuria, a palpable mass.
  • Metastasizes to bone and lung.
  • Treatment: nephrectomy, or chemo/radiation for advanced/metastatic disease.

Bladder Cancer

  • 4th most common cancer in men; urothelial carcinoma is the main type.
  • Risk factors: smoking, occupational toxin exposure, low fluid intake.
  • Early: painless hematuria. Later: UTI-like symptoms (frequency, urgency, dysuria).
BCG TherapyDetail
MechanismIntravesical (instilled directly into the bladder) immunotherapy that stimulates a local immune response against tumor cells.
AdministrationInstilled into the bladder via catheter and held for a period of time before voiding.
Adverse EffectsBladder irritation, flu-like symptoms, hematuria.
Nursing CarePatient education on handling urine after treatment (it contains live attenuated bacteria) and monitoring for systemic BCG infection.
BCG is contraindicated in immunocompromised patients — because it uses a live attenuated organism, it can cause a disseminated infection in someone who can't contain it.

Glomerular Disorders

TermDetail
AnatomyThe glomerulus and its basement membrane (GBM) are the kidney's filtering unit.
GlomerulonephritisInflammation of the glomerulus, often from a Type II or Type III hypersensitivity reaction.
Common TriggersPost-streptococcal infection, IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease), lupus, Goodpasture syndrome (anti-GBM antibodies attacking the glomerular basement membrane directly).
Chronic FormCan progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) if untreated or recurrent.

The Type II vs. Type III hypersensitivity mechanisms behind glomerulonephritis are covered in full on GI, GU, Skin & Hypersensitivity

HARP — the acute presentation of glomerulonephritis: Hematuria, Azotemia (rising BUN/creatinine), Red cell casts, Proteinuria.
TermDetail
Nephrotic SyndromeDefined by more than 3g of protein in urine per 24 hours. Albumin loss causes edema. Loss of antithrombin III and plasminogen causes hypercoagulability. Also causes hyperlipidemia and RAAS-driven hypertension.
GlomerulopathyChronic damage from diabetes (thickens the GBM) or hypertension (causes glomerular sclerosis/scarring) — distinct from the inflammatory process of glomerulonephritis, though it can present similarly (acute-looking findings from a chronic process).

Diabetic microvascular damage is covered in full on Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome

Hypertension's long-term organ damage is covered in full on Hypertension & Hyperlipidemia

Part 2 — Acute Kidney Injury & Chronic Kidney Disease

The Kidneys' Five Key Jobs

  • Fluid and electrolyte homeostasis.
  • Elimination of water-soluble waste.
  • Producing erythropoietin (drives RBC production).
  • Activating vitamin D (needed for calcium absorption).
  • Producing renin (blood pressure regulation).
Trick: almost every CKD manifestation traces back to one of these five jobs failing — anemia (no erythropoietin), bone disease (no activated vitamin D), hypertension (renin dysregulation), fluid overload and electrolyte chaos (homeostasis job), and uremia (waste elimination job).
GFR MarkerMeaning
Greater than 90Normal kidney function.
About 25% of normal (roughly 25–30 mL/hr)Marks renal insufficiency — the shared staging indicator for both AKI and CKD.

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

AKI is fundamentally an ischemic/volume-loss problem — the kidney isn't getting adequate blood flow or is directly injured, and it happens quickly.

ClassificationCause
PrerenalA volume problem before the kidney (hypotension, dehydration, hemorrhage, heart failure) — the most common classification.
IntrarenalDirect damage inside the kidney itself, e.g. acute tubular necrosis (ATN) from prolonged ischemia or nephrotoxins.
PostrenalAn obstruction after the kidney (e.g., BPH, a stone) — the least common classification.
Trick: think of AKI's three classifications like a traffic jam — prerenal is "not enough cars getting to the intersection" (volume), intrarenal is "the intersection itself is broken" (direct damage), postrenal is "something's blocking the road after the intersection" (obstruction).
FindingDetail
OliguriaLess than 400 mL of urine in 24 hours (about less than 30 mL/hour), typically beginning about one day after a hypotensive event.

Treatment Principles

  • Identify and correct the underlying cause (restore volume for prerenal, relieve obstruction for postrenal, stop nephrotoxic agents for intrarenal).
  • Supportive care while the kidney heals — AKI is usually reversible, though prevention is still the goal since severity and duration affect recovery.

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

Staging & Prevention

  • Staged by GFR — progressively lower GFR means a higher (worse) stage.
  • Uremia (buildup of nitrogenous waste with systemic effects) appears at stage 5, the most severe stage.
  • Management is prevention-focused — slowing progression matters more than any single "fix," since CKD is generally not reversible.
Cause of ESRDApproximate Share
Diabetes~50%
Hypertension~30%
Other Causes~20%

Cyclical Pathogenesis

  • Proteinuria and increased angiotensin II drive ongoing glomerular damage.
  • That damage worsens proteinuria and angiotensin II activity further — a self-perpetuating cycle that is the biggest driver of CKD progression.
Body SystemManifestation
NeurologicConfusion, lethargy, peripheral neuropathy as uremic toxins accumulate.
CardiovascularHypertension (renin dysregulation), increased cardiovascular disease risk.
GastrointestinalNausea, anorexia, uremic fetor (ammonia-like breath odor).
Pulmonary / FluidFluid overload and pulmonary edema — often the trigger that pushes a patient to start dialysis.
IntegumentaryUremic frost — visible crystallized urea on the skin in very advanced, untreated disease.
HematologicChronic anemia from loss of erythropoietin production.
MusculoskeletalRenal osteodystrophy — weakened, fracture-prone bones from loss of vitamin D activation.

CKD Pharmacotherapy

ComplicationTreatment
Blood PressureGoal systolic under 140 — not too aggressive, since overly tight control can compromise kidney perfusion. ACE inhibitors and ARBs are used, but never combined together.
CholesterolStatins; goal cholesterol under 200.
Volume OverloadA diuretic (usually furosemide) plus a lower-sodium diet (around 2 g/day).
HyperkalemiaA diuretic to lower potassium; other agents such as Kayexalate; dialysis at more severe stages also corrects this.
Metabolic AcidosisSodium bicarbonate — an alkalinizing agent, goal bicarb 18–20.
Hyperphosphatemia / Renal OsteodystrophyCalcium carbonate (binds phosphate — calcium and phosphate move inversely, like a teeter-totter) and calcitriol (activated vitamin D, since the kidney can no longer activate it itself).
AnemiaErythropoietin — has a black-box warning (see below).
Erythropoietin black-box warning: overcorrecting hemoglobin (giving it when hemoglobin is already above 10 g/dL) raises the risk of stroke, MI, and thromboembolism.

The full erythropoiesis-stimulating agent black-box warning is covered on Anemia

Renal Dosing

  • The kidneys are the primary filter for most medications — when kidney function drops, drug elimination slows and doses/frequency often need adjusting downward.
  • Common examples: digoxin, diabetic medications (glyburide, metformin), vancomycin (its own dedicated renal dosing protocol), and opioids.
Opioids in severe CKD: a patient with severe, poorly-filtering CKD will retain opioids far longer than normal, risking significant respiratory depression — the classic "most concerning medication" answer for a CKD patient in pain.

Part 3 — Male Reproductive Disorders

Testicular Cancer

TermDetail
How CommonRare overall (~1% of cancers in men), but the most common solid tumor in young men, ages 15–34.
Biggest Risk FactorCryptorchidism (undescended testicle) — risk stays elevated for life even after surgical correction.
Tumor OriginThe vast majority arise from germ cells inside the testicle.
PresentationA painless mass or lump — patients often find it themselves; painlessness can delay reporting.
PrognosisVery high survival rates, even in later-stage disease.

Seminoma

  • Grows more slowly.
  • More radiosensitive, somewhat more favorable prognosis.

Nonseminoma

  • Grows and spreads more quickly.
  • Still highly treatable overall.

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

TermDetail
What BPH IsA non-malignant overgrowth of the prostate's epithelial and smooth muscle tissue — not cancer, does not become cancer.
Proposed MechanismsHormone imbalance (shifting androgen/estrogen ratio with age) and DHT accumulation in prostate tissue.
SymptomsThe enlarged prostate squeezes the urethra: hesitancy, weak stream, frequency, urgency, nocturia, incomplete emptying.
ComplicationObstructive nephropathy — urine can back up all the way to the kidneys if severe/prolonged.
ApproachHow It Works
Watchful WaitingFor mild symptoms.
5-Alpha Reductase InhibitorsFinasteride, dutasteride — block conversion of testosterone to DHT, shrink the prostate over months.
Alpha-1 BlockersTamsulosin (Flomax) — relaxes smooth muscle for fast symptom relief, doesn't shrink the gland.
Combination TherapyBoth classes together for moderate-to-severe symptoms.
Trick: "-osteride shrinks it, -osin relaxes it fast." Finasteride/dutasteride (-osteride) shrink slowly; tamsulosin (-osin) relaxes fast.

Prostate Cancer

TermDetail
How CommonThe most common cancer diagnosed in men in the U.S.
Risk FactorsAge, race (higher incidence/mortality in Black men), family history.
PresentationMostly asymptomatic early — often not found until it metastasizes, classically to bone.
PSA ScreeningControversial — PSA isn't cancer-specific (BPH/prostatitis also raise it), and screening drives overdiagnosis/overtreatment of slow-growing cancers.
Gleason ScoreGrading system based on microscopic tissue abnormality — higher score means more aggressive cancer.

Erectile Dysfunction (ED)

TermDetail
Primary (Organic) CausesVascular disease, diabetes, low testosterone, medications, pelvic surgery/injury.
Secondary (Psychogenic) CausesPerformance anxiety, depression, relationship stress.
Normal PhysiologyNitric oxide raises cGMP, relaxing penile smooth muscle and allowing blood flow in.
PDE5 InhibitorsSildenafil (Viagra) blocks the enzyme that breaks down cGMP, sustaining the erection. Taken before sexual activity. Adverse effects: headache, flushing, visual changes.
Nitrate contraindication: never combine PDE5 inhibitors with nitrates — both lower blood pressure through the same NO/cGMP pathway, risking a severe, life-threatening blood pressure drop.
Priapism: a painful erection lasting more than 4 hours unrelated to stimulation — a true urologic emergency that can cause permanent damage if untreated.

Part 4 — Visual & Sensory Disorders

Cataracts

  • Clouding of the lens, strongly associated with aging.
  • Painless, gradual blurry vision.
  • Treatment is surgical only.

Retinopathy

Diabetic

  • Non-proliferative — earlier, small vessel changes.
  • Proliferative — advanced, fragile new vessels that bleed easily.

Hypertensive

  • Vessel damage from chronic high blood pressure.
  • Reversible with good BP control.

Retinal Detachment

TermDetail
PresentationSudden, painless, unilateral. Floaters, flashing lights, a curtain/shadow over the visual field.
Risk FactorsMyopia, older age, trauma.
Why It MattersAn emergency — delayed treatment risks permanent vision loss.

Risk factors: UV exposure, hyperopia, smoking. Irreversible — management slows progression.

Glaucoma

Open-AngleClosed-Angle
OnsetGradualSudden — emergency
MechanismTrabecular meshwork dysfunction — slow drainageIris blocks the drainage angle outright
TriggerChronic process, no single triggerAnticholinergic drugs can precipitate an attack
Class / DrugHow It Works
Beta Blockers (timolol, betaxolol)Reduce aqueous humor production — the instructor's "chill out drugs."
Prostaglandin Analogs (latanoprost)Increase outflow through an alternate pathway.

Meniere's Disease

TermDetail
MechanismEndolymphatic hydrops — abnormal endolymph buildup in the inner ear.
PresentationEpisodic vertigo, hearing loss, tinnitus, fullness in the ear.
ManagementDietary/lifestyle trigger management (sodium, caffeine) — no direct pharmacologic cure.

Whole-Week Quick Reference

One more trick for tying the whole week together: three of Week 3's four topics have a genuine emergency buried inside them that's easy to miss because nothing hurts (or, for closed-angle glaucoma, because it hurts but looks like something else). Keep an eye out for "sudden + painless" and "sudden + severe pain out of nowhere" as your two red flags all week.

TopicThe Emergency to Recognize
Renal DisordersUrosepsis from an untreated/progressing UTI or pyelonephritis.
AKI & CKDSevere hyperkalemia and pulmonary edema from fluid overload.
Male ReproductivePriapism (>4 hours) and the PDE5-inhibitor/nitrate combination.
Visual & SensoryRetinal detachment and acute closed-angle glaucoma.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer. Three cards from each of the four parts above — a whole-week spot check.

What's the key difference in how a lower UTI and pyelonephritis present?
Lower UTI: gradual onset, patient otherwise well, dysuria/frequency/urgency. Pyelonephritis: sudden onset, systemically sick, fever/chills/CVA tenderness.
What is HARP, and what does it stand for?
The acute presentation of glomerulonephritis: Hematuria, Azotemia, Red cell casts, Proteinuria.
Why is BCG intravesical therapy contraindicated in immunocompromised patients?
It's a live attenuated organism — an immunocompromised patient can't contain it and risks a disseminated infection.
What are the three classifications of AKI?
Prerenal (a volume problem before the kidney, most common), intrarenal (direct damage inside the kidney), postrenal (an obstruction after the kidney, least common).
What two conditions cause about 80% of end-stage renal disease combined?
Diabetes (~50%) and hypertension (~30%).
Why is erythropoietin dangerous if given when hemoglobin is already above 10 g/dL?
Overcorrecting hemoglobin raises the risk of stroke, MI, and thromboembolism — its black-box warning.
What is the single biggest risk factor for testicular cancer?
Cryptorchidism (an undescended testicle) — risk stays elevated for life even after surgical correction.
How do 5-alpha reductase inhibitors and alpha-1 blockers differ in treating BPH?
5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride/dutasteride) block DHT and shrink the prostate over months. Alpha-1 blockers (tamsulosin) relax smooth muscle for fast symptom relief without shrinking the gland.
Why can't PDE5 inhibitors be combined with nitrates?
Both lower blood pressure through the same nitric oxide/cGMP pathway — combining them risks a severe, life-threatening blood pressure drop.
What are the classic symptoms of retinal detachment?
Sudden, painless, unilateral floaters, flashing lights, and a curtain/shadow across the visual field — an emergency.
What's the mechanism difference between open-angle and closed-angle glaucoma?
Open-angle: trabecular meshwork dysfunction, slow gradual pressure buildup. Closed-angle: the iris blocks the drainage angle outright, causing a sudden dangerous pressure spike.
What causes Meniere's disease, and how is it managed?
Endolymphatic hydrops (abnormal endolymph buildup). Managed mainly through diet/lifestyle trigger management (sodium, caffeine) — no direct pharmacologic cure.
