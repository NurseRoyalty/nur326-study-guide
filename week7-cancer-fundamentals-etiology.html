Cancer Fundamentals, Metastasis & Etiology

This is the first of several cancer topics: cancer in general comes first, then the specific cancers you are responsible for knowing, then chemotherapies. This page covers what cancer is, how cancer cells differ from normal cells in function and appearance, benign versus malignant tumors (a starred topic), the cell growth cycle and why it matters for chemotherapy, tumor grading (1 through 3), the TNM classification, and cancer stages I through IV. It then covers metastasis (primary versus secondary tumors and the routes cancer uses to spread, including seeding, implantation, lymphatic, and vascular spread), the common sites of metastasis, and etiology: general risk factors, tumor suppressor genes versus proto-oncogenes/oncogenes (including P53), and carcinogens versus promoters.

Cross-reference: the instructor tells you to recall the cellular changes taught in week 1 of the earlier course, which is the background for this topic. See Cellular Adaptation, Infection & Immunity Review

What Is Cancer?

Cancer overall is a group of diseases characterized by cells that show cellular transformation, uncontrolled cellular growth, and possible invasion into other organs, with metastasis to other tissues or organs.

Three hallmarks in the definition

  • Cellular transformation: the example given is a genetic mutation that occurs in a cell.
  • Uncontrolled cellular growth.
  • Possible invasion into other organs and metastasis to other tissues or organs.

In general, cancer cells usually lack

  • Cellular differentiation: they are not as specific as our normal body cells.
  • Cellular checkpoints in the normal cellular process: they may not have the checkpoint that recognizes DNA errors.
  • The ability to undergo apoptosis: apoptosis is where a cell self-destructs when it recognizes that it is not doing what it is supposed to do.
  • Response to signals from other cells: they disregard signals such as "stop invading my space."
  • The result: the cancer cells continue to proliferate.

The instructor stresses that it is very important to understand what makes normal cells and cancer cells very different. The next section lays out that comparison.

  • How intense the cancer becomes, or where it goes, depends on how successful the immune attack is, what kind of cancer it is, how fast it is proliferating, and other factors.

Benign vs. Malignant Tumors

Starred by the instructor: "You can put a little star here." Know benign versus malignant. You are not asked to tell leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma, but you are expected to understand the differences between the types of cells at the benign level and the malignant level.

Terminology

  • The terms malignant tumor, malignancy, neoplasm, and tumor are used to indicate cancerous cells.
  • Tumors, meaning groups of abnormal cells, can be benign (not likely to be associated with cancer) or malignant (associated with cancer).

Benign tumor

  • Not associated with cancer.
  • Well differentiated: the cells resemble the tissues of origin in the area, but they are "just kind of inappropriate."
  • Grow at a progressive or slow rate, closer to a normal rate.
  • Usually do not invade significantly.
  • Cohesive cell walls.
  • Demarcated tumor, often encapsulated, which makes it movable. The instructor's example is a fatty tumor on a dog.
  • No metastasis is seen.
  • No necrotic tumor core.
  • Generally taken to be harmless, which is usually the case.

Malignant tumor

  • What we associate with cancer; these are cancer cells.
  • Poorly differentiated: the cells do not resemble the tissue of origin, meaning they are anaplastic, "completely inappropriate cells for the area."
  • Growth is usually erratic: it can be slow, but it can also be very, very rapid.
  • Generally invasive: they infiltrate surrounding normal tissues.
  • Cancer cells that invade surrounding tissues are considered to metastasize, which is frequently seen with malignant tumors.
  • The tumor core can be necrotic.

A benign tumor can still be lethal. Benign tumors are generally taken to be harmless, but they can be lethal if they grow large enough to mechanically interrupt the normal function of a critical tissue or organ. The instructor's examples are a dangerous benign tumor in the brain, or a significant portion taking over a lung and interrupting normal processes.

Example: Endometrium (leiomyoma vs. malignant tumor)

The instructor uses a slide picture of the endometrium to contrast a benign tumor with a malignant one. Only her spoken description is captured here.

Benign leiomyoma

  • Small.
  • Well demarcated.
  • Non-invasive.
  • Non-metastatic.
  • Well differentiated.

  • Large and poorly demarcated: it is not clear, and there are no nice lines containing it.

  • If it gets into the lymph node area, it can be metastatic.
  • Often poorly differentiated.

The Cell Growth Cycle

This goes back to biology. Normal cells in our body divide or proliferate in a very controlled and organized fashion, which is regulated by mechanisms and through the cell growth cycle. Cell growth characteristics of normal and neoplastic cells are similar: it is not that the cycles look completely different. Both types of cell pass through distinct phases.

Generation time

  • The time it takes to go through the cell cycle is called the generation time.
  • It can range from hours to days, depending on what kind of tumor is involved.

The phases, as listed in the lecture

PhaseWhat the lecture says
G0The cell is at rest and not actively engaged in the cell cycle. Most of the human cells in the body exist in this phase right now. G0 is seen in the normal cell cycle but not in the cancer cell cycle in the slide picture. Cancer cells in this phase are not susceptible to the toxic effects of cell-cycle-specific drugs.
G1The cells enter the cell cycle and enzymes for DNA synthesis are produced. This is the first post-mitosis phase.
SDescribed as "synthesis of structure." The structures move to opposite poles in preparation for division into two separate cells. The 46 chromosomes in our DNA reorganize into two separate sets of 23 pairs at opposite poles. Two nuclear membranes develop around the two separate sets of 23 pairs.
G2The cells are preparing to divide. This is where RNA and specialized proteins are being created.
MMitosis, or division, is completed, and two daughter cells (two other new cells) are created.

Why the cell cycle matters for chemotherapy

  • Chemotherapy agents can impact specific phases of the cancer cell cycle. Certain chemotherapy medications are specific to certain parts of the cell cycle.
  • This is why the instructor cares so much about the cell growth cycle. Understanding it will help you understand chemotherapy agents better.
  • Cancer cells in G0 (the resting phase) are not susceptible to the toxic effects of the cell-cycle-specific drugs.

How the cancer cell cycle differs from normal

  • Cancer cells do not rest and do not go through restriction checkpoints. They go through cell cycles constantly.
  • They are actively proliferating without control and without an active restriction point.
  • Without a restriction point, DNA repairs do not often occur, and the cell can continuously mutate.
  • This is why we see so many mistakes in cancer cells: they do not have a rest, and they keep mutating even if they are not doing what they are supposed to.

About the TNM system

  • Used internationally; diagnostic; and very specific.
  • "This is what you will see in your patient's charts."
  • It is a series of letters and numbers to describe the cancer.
  • Cancer therapy is based in part on this classification.
  • It requires a biopsy; much of it is seen under the microscope.
  • All of it requires microscopic confirmation of malignancy.
  • There are many additional subcategories, and the system is much more complex than what is introduced here.

What Metastasis Means

The instructor opens by defining the terms. Malignant cancer cells are invasive, penetrative, and destructive to their surrounding tissues. They generally metastasize from a primary site through the lymph nodes or through the blood.

Establishing a tumor in a target organ

  • For a tumor to establish itself in a target organ, it must develop its own blood vessels and connect to the pre-existing blood supply.
  • The reason given: tumors and their cells need oxygen and nutrients to grow.
  • The instructor later ties this to how fast a secondary tumor grows: it all depends on the angiogenesis rate — is the secondary tumor developing blood vessels (vasculature) to obtain oxygen and nutrients?

Primary Tumor vs. Secondary Tumor

Primary tumor

  • The location / site of origin — where the original cellular division problem started.
  • The original site of the cancer.

Secondary tumor

  • Sometimes there are secondary tumors in addition to the primary.
  • Generally not the site of origin.
  • Results from seeding of malignant cells that travel to distant sites — this is due to metastasis.
  • "METs" means a metastasis, or a secondary tumor.

What a secondary tumor looks like under the microscope

  • Under a microscope, the cancer cells at the secondary site look like the cells from the primary site.
  • Example from the lecture: a breast cancer that travels to the bone. There may be a tumor in the bone; when it is biopsied, the cells look like the cancerous breast cells seen in the primary breast tumor.

Routes of Cancer Spread

The instructor lists three words to be familiar with: seeding, implantation, and metastasis (cancer spread). Seeding and implantation are defined first; metastasis then has two primary routes.

The two primary routes of metastasis

  • Lymphatic spread
  • Vascular spread (blood drainage)

Which is the "first stop"?

  • Lymphatic spread is generally common; the lymph system is described as the "first stop in the system."
  • For vascular (blood) spread, the first stop is generally the liver.

Lymphatic Spread

Cancer cells can become trapped in lymph nodes. The instructor says three things can happen to cells in the lymph system:

Fate of the trapped cellsWhat it means
The cells die"Obviously hopeful."
The cells go dormantThey sit and wait to grow. You may not know you have cancer at this point.
The cells flourish and proliferate"Unfortunately," sometimes the cells flourish and multiply.

If the cells survive

  • Cancer cells that survive get out of the lymph node and into the lymph system.
  • They can spread through channels to the next set of lymph nodes they encounter.
  • This is how you get secondary tumor locations, or secondary spread.

Vascular Spread

Vascular spread is spread by vascular (blood) drainage. The lecture describes it as a sequence:

Steps of vascular spread

  • Cancer cells have to penetrate the blood vessels draining the tumor site — they penetrate these local veins.
  • Once the cancer cells are in the veins, they travel away from the primary site.
  • First stop: the liver. The instructor's reason: most of the blood goes through the portal vein before it goes into general circulation.
  • As cancer cells flow through the liver, they clump and get trapped in the liver capillaries.
  • They then begin to proliferate in those liver capillaries.
  • As a result, the liver is often a secondary location when we associate things with vascular spread.

Genetic Mechanisms: Tumor Suppressor Genes vs. Proto-Oncogenes / Oncogenes

Our bodies have ways of trying to make sure we do not develop cancer: signaling systems and pathways that try to stop cancer cells from proliferating and growing. The lecture describes two major classes of cancer genes / genetic mechanisms: tumor suppressor genes, and proto-oncogenes/oncogenes. The instructor uses a car analogy — think of a cell as a car; for it to work properly, there need to be ways to control how fast it grows.

Tumor suppressor gene = the brake pedal

  • Normally keeps the cell from dividing too quickly, just as a brake keeps a car from going too fast.
  • Normally functions to restrain cell growth.
  • When something goes wrong with the gene, cell division can get out of control.
  • They can become defective and lose their ability to inhibit cell growth, thus allowing cancer formation.
  • One way cancer cells can develop is if they lack this tumor suppressor gene.

Proto-oncogene = the gas pedal; oncogene = a stuck gas pedal

  • A proto-oncogene normally works much like a gas pedal: it helps the cell grow and divide normally.
  • Proto-oncogenes are genes that stimulate and regulate a cell's movement through the cell cycle, resulting in cellular growth and proliferation.
  • An oncogene can be compared with a gas pedal that is stuck down — stuck to the floor, "stuck in the drive."
  • When a proto-oncogene is mutated ("messed up"), it is stimulating constantly: the cell is never in the rest cycle and is constantly reproducing.

P53 — the specific gene named

  • P53 is a specific gene named in lecture.
  • It is a tumor suppressor gene.
  • It controls cellular apoptosis — natural cell death — which should lead to the natural death of cells that have damaged DNA.

Carcinogens

Why do these genes sometimes get messed up? The instructor says "I wish we knew," but we do know some things that can cause problems. Carcinogens are one of them.

What a carcinogen is and how it works

  • Carcinogens are substances that cause cancer by altering a cell's DNA — so they can alter the genetic mechanisms described above.
  • They often require prolonged exposure over many years.
  • Carcinogens have actually been demonstrated to cause cancer: causation, not just association or correlation.
  • The ability of some substances to alter DNA is what makes them carcinogens; they can cause cells to mutate and change inappropriately.
  • Generally the damage is cumulative: the more exposure to the carcinogen, the more likely the initiated cells will undergo further mutation.

Known, Probable, and Possible Carcinogens

Promoters

Promoters are agents that help mutated genes proliferate. The instructor says this is where "sometimes the line gets a little messy."

Examples of promoters given

  • Diet — for example, a high-fat diet can stimulate further proliferation of a cancer cell.
  • Alcoholic beverages
  • Tobacco
  • Hormones — "big promoters" that can be very important.

Hormones as Promoters — Estrogen Exposure Example

The lecture's reasoning

  • For women, the more menstrual cycles you experience, the more you are exposed to estrogen, and this can increase your risk of cancer.
  • That is why starting your period earlier can increase your risk of cancer.
  • Women at higher risk include those who had their first menstrual period at a young age, had a very late first pregnancy, did not breastfeed, or only breastfed for a few months.
  • The instructor says it is estimated that in developed countries, we could cut our incidence of breast cancer by half if breastfeeding time increased. She sets aside any breastfeeding argument and says the point is simply that this is an exposure issue.

Promoter vs. Carcinogen

Carcinogen

  • Alters the cell's DNA.
  • Can cause cells to mutate and change inappropriately.

Promoter

  • Helps already-mutated genes / already-damaged cells proliferate.
  • Example: it is not the alcohol causing the DNA change and mutation, but it can proliferate cells that are already damaged from whatever else has happened.

Things can be both

  • There is a difference between a promoter and a carcinogen, but you can have things that are both carcinogens and promoters.
  • Estrogen therapy: it can act as a carcinogen by altering the cell DNA, but it can also help mutated genes proliferate.
  • Tobacco is another example of something that does both.

What the instructor expects on the exam

  • She says she is not going to "get in the weeds" and ask for an example of a promoter.
  • She wants you to know what the different definitions are, so that when you read a study describing a cancer risk you can tell whether it describes something associated with promoting development versus something that alters cell DNA.
  • In short: "I want you to know the differences between them."

How does the lecture define cancer overall, and which terms indicate cancerous cells?
A group of diseases characterized by cells with cellular transformation (for example a genetic mutation), uncontrolled cellular growth, and possible invasion into other organs and metastasis to other tissues or organs. Malignant tumor, malignancy, neoplasm, and tumor are the terms used to indicate cancerous cells; tumors (groups of abnormal cells) can be benign (not associated with cancer) or malignant (associated with cancer).
What do cancer cells usually lack, in general?
Cellular differentiation (they are not as specific as normal cells); cellular checkpoints (including the one that recognizes DNA errors); the ability to undergo apoptosis (self-destruction); and response to signals from other cells such as "stop invading my space." The result is that they continue to proliferate.
Contrast normal and cancer cells on contact inhibition, cohesiveness, and cellular communication.
Normal cells have contact inhibition (they stay out of others' space), are cohesive (similar cells stick together), and communicate through cellular signals. Cancer cells lack contact inhibition and invade others' space, are not cohesive and leave, and do not communicate or respond to signals.
Contrast normal and cancer cells on proliferation control, apoptosis, and proliferation rate.
Normal cells are controlled (the goal is zero population growth), undergo apoptosis when no longer needed, and divide at an expected rate that depends on cell type (skin cells faster than cardiomyocytes). Cancer cells significantly lack growth control, are sometimes immortal or die unpredictably, and have an unpredictable rate that depends on their level of differentiation and their DNA makeup.
What does the lecture say about HLA antigens in normal cells versus cancer cells?
The body recognizes the HLA antigen on normal cells as "my cell, don't hurt me." Cancer cells carry a non-self marker, so the immune system may attack them. How intense the cancer becomes or where it goes depends on how successful that attack is, the kind of cancer, how fast it is proliferating, and other factors.
What morphologic features of cancer cells does the lecture describe?
Abnormally shaped nuclei, usually larger than normal; a nucleus that takes up much more of the cell than the cytoplasm; a lot of variation in cell size and shape; loss of some normal cellular features (the "sick neuron" example); disorganized arrangement with cells stacked on top of each other; and a poorly defined tumor boundary.
STARRED: Can a benign tumor be lethal, and what are the characteristics of a benign tumor?
Not associated with cancer; well differentiated and resembles the tissue of origin (but inappropriate); slow or progressive growth closer to normal; usually no significant invasion; cohesive; demarcated and often encapsulated, so movable (like a fatty tumor on a dog); no metastasis; no necrotic tumor core. Yes. Benign tumors are generally taken to be harmless, but they can be lethal if they grow large enough to mechanically interrupt the normal function of a critical tissue or organ, for example a dangerous benign tumor in the brain or one taking over a significant portion of a lung.
STARRED: List the characteristics of a malignant tumor, and what does "anaplastic" mean here?
Poorly differentiated, not resembling the tissue of origin, which is what anaplastic means ("completely inappropriate cells for the area"); erratic growth (slow or very rapid); generally invasive and infiltrating surrounding normal tissue; frequently metastasizes; can have a necrotic core.
Compare the benign leiomyoma and the malignant tumor in the endometrium example.
The benign leiomyoma is small, well demarcated, non-invasive, non-metastatic, and well differentiated, and is usually removed laparoscopically (or left alone if not causing a problem). The malignant tumor is large and poorly demarcated, with rapid cell growth plus hemorrhage and necrosis, locally invasive, able to become metastatic if it reaches the lymph node area, and often poorly differentiated. The instructor will not ask you the leiomyoma versus leiomyosarcoma difference, but expects you to understand benign versus malignant cell differences.
What is generation time, and what are the cell cycle phases in the order listed in lecture (including G0)?
Generation time is the time it takes to go through the cell cycle; it ranges from hours to days depending on the tumor. G0 is the resting phase in which the cell is not actively engaged in the cycle; most human cells are in it. It appears in the normal cycle but not in the cancer cell cycle. G0, G1, S, G2, M. G1: cells enter the cycle, enzymes for DNA synthesis are produced, first post-mitosis phase. G2: cells prepare to divide, RNA and specialized proteins are created. M: mitosis is completed and two daughter cells are created. (S is described as the synthesis of structure, with structures moving to opposite poles and 46 chromosomes reorganizing into two sets of 23 at opposite poles.)
Why does the cell cycle matter for chemotherapy, and how does the cancer cell cycle differ from normal?
Certain chemotherapy medications are specific to certain phases of the cancer cell cycle, so understanding the cycle helps you understand the drugs. Cancer cells in G0 (resting) are not susceptible to the toxic effects of cell-cycle-specific drugs. Cancer cells do not rest or go through restriction checkpoints; they cycle constantly and proliferate without control. With no active restriction point, DNA repairs do not often occur and the cell continuously mutates, which is why we see so many mistakes in cancer cells.
Describe tumor grades 1, 2, and 3.
Based on the microscope appearance of the cells from a malignant tumor: grade 1 = well differentiated; grade 2 = moderately differentiated; grade 3 = poorly differentiated or anaplastic.
What do T, N, and M stand for in the TNM system, and what is the system used for?
T = tumor size, location, and involvement; N = lymph node involvement; M = metastasis to different organs. It is used internationally, is diagnostic and very specific, is what you will see in patient charts, requires a biopsy and microscopic confirmation of malignancy, and therapy is based in part on it.
Explain T0, Tis, T1 to T4, N0, N1, N2, M0, and M1.
T0: no evidence of a primary tumor. Tis: tumor in situ, early cancer with cells only in the layer in which it began. T1 to T4: the higher the number, the larger the tumor or the more it has grown into nearby tissues (for this course, T1 is about 1 cm, T2 is 2, and so on). N0: no spread to regional lymph nodes. N1: spread to the closest regional node or a small number of regional nodes. N2: spread to the most distant or numerous nodes. M0: no metastasis. M1: metastasis present.
What do cancer stages I, II, III, and IV mean, and what are they generally based on?
Stage I: confined to the organ of origin (usually very treatable, the best stage to catch it in). Stage II: locally invasive. Stage III: regional spread. Stage IV: distant sites. Staging is generally based on lymph node spread and metastasis. If given a TNM or stage classification, you must understand what it means for the patient.
How does the lecture describe malignant cancer cells, how do they generally metastasize, and what must a tumor do to establish itself in a target organ?
They are invasive, penetrative, and destructive to surrounding tissues. They generally metastasize from a primary site through the lymph nodes or through the blood. To establish itself in a target organ, a tumor must develop its own blood vessels and connect to the pre-existing blood supply, because tumors and their cells need oxygen and nutrients to grow.
Distinguish a primary tumor from a secondary tumor.
The primary tumor is the site of origin, where the original cellular division problem started. A secondary tumor is generally not the site of origin; it results from seeding of malignant cells that travel to distant sites, due to metastasis. "METs" means a metastasis or secondary tumor.
What do secondary-tumor cells look like under the microscope, and what example did the instructor give?
They look like the cells from the primary site. Example: a breast cancer that travels to the bone; a biopsy of the bone tumor shows cells that look like the cancerous breast cells of the primary breast tumor.
What are the three words listed for cancer spread, and how are seeding and implantation defined?
Seeding, implantation, and metastasis. Seeding: tumors erode and shed into the body cavities (example: intraperitoneal seeding, where colon cancer invades the colon but also puts out seeds that can implant in other parts of the abdominal cavity). Implantation: direct expansion of the tumor to adjoining tissues as it grows (example: prostate cancer that spreads into the bladder, directly connected because of close proximity).
What three things can happen to cancer cells trapped in a lymph node, and what happens if they survive?
They can die (hopeful), go dormant (sit and wait to grow; you may not know you have cancer), or flourish and proliferate. Survivors get out of the node into the lymph system and spread through channels to the next set of lymph nodes, creating secondary tumor locations.
What are the two primary routes of metastasis, and how does vascular spread work (why is the liver the first stop)?
The two primary routes are lymphatic spread and vascular (blood) spread. Cancer cells penetrate the local veins draining the tumor site and travel away from the primary site. Most blood goes through the portal vein before general circulation, so the cells flow through the liver, clump and get trapped in its capillaries, and begin to proliferate there. The liver is often a secondary location with vascular spread.
Where do lung, colon, breast, prostate, and melanoma cancers generally metastasize, and how much do you need to know about why?
Lung: bone and brain. Colon: liver. Breast: bone, brain, liver, or lung. Prostate: vertebrae. Melanoma: brain. Not in depth — she will not ask "in the weeds" about why; that is higher level than expected. You should be familiar with the metastatic-sites slide.
What determines how fast a secondary tumor grows, according to the lecture?
The angiogenesis rate: whether the secondary tumor is developing blood vessels/vasculature to obtain the oxygen and nutrients it needs.
What does etiology mean, and how does the instructor characterize what we know about the etiology of cancer?
Etiology means cause. The etiology of cancer in general is still kind of a mystery, but we have made a lot of progress over the last several decades and have identified many factors that are risks for certain cancers.
What does the lecture say about age and cancer risk, and what is the exception?
For most cancers, risk increases as we age. This is not seen with reproductive cancers such as cervical cancer or uterine cancer.
Name the other risk factors the lecture gives (UV, diet, sex, genetic/ethnic, viruses, occupational).
Sun/UV exposure and skin cancer; low-fiber diet and GI cancers; lung and urinary cancers higher risk in males (possibly lifestyle and environment); Burkitt lymphoma in young African children and children of African descent; nasopharyngeal cancer in persons of Chinese descent; oncogenic viruses (HPV, HIV, herpes virus, Epstein-Barr); occupational/environmental carcinogens and mutagens (e.g., certain factory chemicals in Kentucky linked to lung cancers).
Compare a tumor suppressor gene and a proto-oncogene using the lecture's car analogy, and say what an oncogene is.
A tumor suppressor gene is the brake pedal: it restrains cell division, and if defective, division can get out of control. A proto-oncogene is the gas pedal: it stimulates and regulates the cell through the cell cycle so the cell grows and divides normally. An oncogene is a gas pedal stuck to the floor: the mutated gene stimulates constantly, so the cell never rests and constantly reproduces.
What is P53?
A specific tumor suppressor gene that controls cellular apoptosis (natural cell death), which should lead to the natural death of cells that have damaged DNA.
What is a carcinogen, and what are the known, probable, and possible examples given?
A carcinogen is a substance that causes cancer by altering a cell's DNA, often requiring prolonged exposure over many years, with cumulative damage; it is shown to cause cancer (causation, not just correlation). Known: tobacco, asbestos, alcohol, and some viruses like HPV and HIV. Probable: night shift work (women who worked night shift in the past 10 years have a 60% higher risk of breast cancer). Possible: engine exhaust (insufficient evidence to conclude).
What is a promoter, how does it differ from a carcinogen, and what does the instructor expect you to know?
A promoter helps already-mutated genes/damaged cells proliferate (examples: diet such as a high-fat diet, alcoholic beverages, tobacco, hormones such as estrogen). A carcinogen alters the cell DNA. Some things act as both (estrogen therapy, tobacco). She will not ask for promoter examples; she wants you to know the definitions so you can tell something that promotes development from something that alters cell DNA.
