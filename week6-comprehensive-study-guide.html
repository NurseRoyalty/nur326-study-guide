Part 1 — Ischemic Heart Disease & Heart Failure

Cardiovascular disease breaks into three broad categories: "electrical" (dysrhythmias), "plumbing" (the vessels — ischemic heart disease), and "pump" (the heart muscle — heart failure). This part covers plumbing and pump; electrical is its own part below.

Coronary Artery Disease (CAD)

Driven by atherosclerosis: plaque narrows the coronary arteries, reducing myocardial blood flow. Endothelial dysfunction (damage to the vessel's inner lining) is the earliest step. The left anterior descending (LAD) artery is nicknamed the "widow maker" because it supplies a large portion of the heart muscle, so a blockage there is especially lethal.

Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

  • Age
  • Family history
  • Gender — estrogen is cardioprotective before menopause
  • Ethnicity — Black, Hispanic, and Native American populations have higher risk, with a documented death disparity linked to structural racism
  • Genetics/genetic variants (SNPs)

Modifiable Risk Factors

  • Hypertension — roughly 2–3x the risk
  • Smoking
  • Diabetes
  • Obesity/physical inactivity
  • Diet — a DASH-style diet is protective
  • Hyperlipidemia
  • Depression/stress/anxiety

Angina & Myocardial Infarction (MI)

Stable Angina

  • Triggered by exertion, relieved by rest
  • Lasts 2–5 minutes
  • Treated with nitrates

Unstable Angina

  • A more dangerous pattern than stable angina
  • Not reliably relieved by rest — a step closer to a full MI

MI symptom pattern: pain not relieved by rest (the key distinction from stable angina), pain that radiates, nausea, vomiting, dyspnea, and diaphoresis. Women often present atypically compared to this "classic" picture.

Call 911 if chest pain is not resolved within about 5 minutes — the concern point for unstable angina or a full MI.

Heart Failure — Definition & Core Physiology

By the AHA's framing, heart failure (HF) is the heart's inability to pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. CO = HR × SV; stroke volume is determined by preload (fluid volume), afterload (pressure/resistance), and contractility (squeeze strength). In HF: contractility decreases first → preload increases (fluid backs up) → afterload increases — the chain behind HF's hallmark fluid volume overload.

Causes of HF: repeated ischemic episodes/prior MI, papillary muscle rupture, chronic hypertension, COPD (→ right-sided HF), dysrhythmias, valve disorders, pulmonary emboli. Stable angina by itself is NOT a risk factor for HF. Risk factors: hypertension (the single greatest), diabetes, prior MI (22% of men / 46% of women who have an MI develop HF), age over 65 (most common cause of hospitalization in this group), Black/African-American incidence, strong genetic link, COPD, severe anemia, congenital heart defects, viral myocarditis, alcohol/cocaine/crack abuse, kidney disease.

Left-Sided HF

  • Pulmonary congestion — cough, crackles, wheezes, frothy/blood-tinged sputum, PND, orthopnea, tachycardia, fatigue, cyanosis
  • Most common cause: poorly controlled hypertension

Right-Sided HF

  • Systemic congestion — JVD, dependent edema, weight gain, hepatosplenomegaly, GI distress
  • Most common cause: COPD
MNEMONIC — "Left = lungs, right = body."

HFrEF ("Systolic")

  • EF under 40%
  • Impaired contractile function

HFpEF ("Diastolic")

  • EF 40–50% (normal 55–65%)
  • Ventricles can't relax/fill; associated with older age, female sex, diabetes, obesity, chronic HTN

Ventricular remodeling: angiotensin II, aldosterone, endothelin, TNF-alpha, and catecholamines are inappropriately released, driving apoptosis, hypertrophy, collagen deposits, and myocardial fibrosis — progressively worsening HF. S3 gallop: a low-pitched sound just after S2 in early diastole; in adults over 40, it indicates heart failure.

Part 2 — Cardiac Dysrhythmias

Four cardiac cell properties: automaticity, excitability, conductivity, contractility. Depolarization triggers systole; repolarization is the cell recharging. P wave = atrial depolarization, QRS = ventricular depolarization, T wave = ventricular repolarization (atrial repolarization is buried in the QRS). Conduction pathway: SA node → AV node → bundle of His → Purkinje fibers. Normal sinus rhythm: rate 60–100 bpm, regular, PR interval 0.12–0.20 sec, QRS under 0.12 sec (narrow). Three mechanisms behind dysrhythmias: inappropriate automaticity (electrolytes, especially K+), triggered activity (digoxin toxicity, sympathetic overstimulation), and re-entry (ischemia, electrolyte imbalance).

AFib loses the "atrial kick" (~20% of cardiac output) and is "irregularly irregular." Untreated AFib roughly doubles cardiac-death risk and raises stroke risk 4–5x (present in ~15–20% of all strokes); treatment is rate control (beta blockers, CCBs, digitalis, amiodarone) plus stroke prevention (anticoagulants/antiplatelets). PVC patterns: bigeminy (every other beat), trigeminy (every third), quadrigeminy (every fourth) — no direct drug treats PVCs themselves.

V-tach and V-fib are deadly rhythms requiring an immediate pulse check. V-tach with a pulse follows the ACLS/antiarrhythmic pathway; pulseless V-tach or any V-fib means starting CPR immediately. Sustained V-tach (>10–15 sec) is a rapid-response emergency even with a pulse.

Part 3 — Peripheral Vascular Disease

Peripheral vascular disease (PVD) is an umbrella term for peripheral artery disease (PAD) and peripheral venous disease (chronic venous insufficiency). Atherosclerosis in the lower extremities is the same disease process as CAD in a different vascular bed. PAD risk factors: smoking (the single biggest — >80% of PAD patients are current/former smokers, doubling amputation risk), uncontrolled diabetes, high cholesterol, existing heart disease/stroke, age over 50 — affects men and women equally. Other causes of arterial occlusion: thrombus formation, thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease — autoimmune, smokers), and vasospasm (Raynaud's phenomenon — scleroderma/pulmonary HTN association).

PAD (Arterial)

  • Intermittent claudication — exertional cramping, relieved by rest ("angina of the lower extremity"); femoral artery is the most common site
  • Pallor with elevation; dependent rubor when hanging down
  • Diminished/absent pulses; trophic skin changes (shiny, thick, hair loss)
  • Ulcers typically at the toes/dorsum of the foot

Chronic Venous Insufficiency (Venous)

  • Aching and tiredness — NOT burning or heaviness
  • Edema; leathery, dark/tan, flaking skin
  • Affects ~40% of the U.S. population; varicose veins commonly associated
  • Stasis ulcers typically at the ankles
MNEMONIC — The 5 P's: Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesias, Paralysis (weakness).

ABI (ankle-brachial index) compares ankle to arm blood pressure: ~1.0–1.4 is normal; ≤0.5 indicates severe arterial disease. Shared treatment: smoking cessation (the single most important intervention), increased activity (collateral circulation), weight loss, stress reduction, controlling diabetes/HTN, and for severe occlusion, bypass/angioplasty/stent. Claudication-specific drugs: cilostazol (Pletal — platelet inhibitor + vasodilator, CYP450 interactions) and pentoxifylline (increases blood flow/O2, TID). Most PAD patients are also on a statin plus an antiplatelet/anticoagulant (aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin, apixaban).

Part 4 — Valve Disease

Blood flows tricuspid → pulmonic → mitral → aortic ("TP-MA," or "tricycle before bicycle"). Tricuspid, pulmonic, and aortic each have 3 leaflets; mitral has 2. Tricuspid + mitral = AV valves; pulmonic + aortic = semilunar valves. Tricuspid and aortic open during systole. Valve damage mechanisms: wear and tear, calcium deposits, annulus problems, leaflet malcoaptation, endocarditis, clot formation, and papillary muscle rupture (AV valves only).

Stenosis

  • Leaflets too tight/stiff — restricts forward flow
  • Most common in the aortic valve

Regurgitation

  • Leaflets too floppy/loose — blood flows backward
  • Most common in the mitral valve

Left-sided valve disease is emphasized since the left heart works at higher pressure. Aortic stenosis: classic triad of syncope, lightheadedness, and chest pain (coronary arteries starved of forward flow); can progress to pulmonary edema. Mitral regurgitation: fatigue from reduced forward output, plus pulmonary symptoms/dyspnea from backward flow into the left atrium/pulmonary veins. Diagnosis: TTE (standard echo) or TEE (more invasive, more detailed).

Infective endocarditis: vegetations impair valve closure and can embolize. The tricuspid valve is most commonly affected (first valve blood reaches — especially relevant to IV drug users). Organisms: Staph aureus and Staph epidermidis. Risk groups: prosthetic-valve patients (need prophylactic antibiotics before dental work) and IV drug users. Symptoms: fever, chills, weight loss, muscle/joint pain, murmurs. Septic emboli travel to the lungs (from tricuspid) or systemically to brain/coronary arteries/spleen/kidney/bowel (from left-sided valves) — ~40% develop neurologic/embolic stroke symptoms.

Rheumatic heart disease (from childhood rheumatic illness) is another valve-damage cause. Treatment for significant valve disease is valve replacement; active infection must be cleared with antibiotics before and after surgery.

Part 5 — Cardiac Pharmacology

Several drug classes this week are "pull-forward" drugs from a prior pharmacology course (beta blockers, CCBs, statins, aspirin/clopidogrel, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, warfarin) — review your existing drug matrix for their full profile. This section covers only what's genuinely new plus each drug's specific role in this week's cardiac contexts.

Antianginal Drugs

Nitrates decrease preload via venodilation; beta blockers decrease HR/contractility; CCBs decrease afterload and HR/contractility; ranolazine improves myocardial energy efficiency (mechanism not fully detailed). Stepped approach: beta blocker or CCB plus a nitrate, reassess in ~3 months.

Nitrate side effects: headache, hypotension, reflex tachycardia; dangerous interaction with sildenafil/antihypertensives/alcohol. Ranolazine (Ranexa): oral only, can prolong QT, CYP3A4 inhibitor (avoid grapefruit juice), doesn't cause the BP/HR drop nitrates do.

Heart Failure Drugs

Core categories: an ACE inhibitor/ARB/ARNI, a beta blocker, and an MRA — with SGLT2 inhibitors increasingly added as a fourth. Only one RAS-inhibitor type at a time. ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan) is currently thought most effective due to its added neprilysin-inhibitor effect on remodeling, but is newer/costlier. Carvedilol is the beta blocker used most (beta + alpha blockade). Spironolactone (MRA) offloads the LV via sodium/water retention suppression, not primarily diuresis — watch potassium. SGLT2 inhibitors ("-gliflozins") help ventricular unloading through a mechanism distinct from traditional diuretics. Loop diuretics (furosemide) treat volume overload symptomatically — no survival benefit despite near-universal use.

TermDetail
Digoxin (digitalis)Inhibits the Na+/K+ ATPase pump, raising intracellular calcium and contractility; decreases sympathetic stimulation. Toxicity risk highest in older adults, women, and patients on diuretics (hypokalemia). Hallmark toxicity sign: bradycardia, plus headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, visual disturbances. Antidote: digoxin immune Fab (for severe/symptomatic cases).
Take a full 1-minute apical pulse before giving digoxin — hold the dose if under 60 bpm.

Rate & Rhythm Control Drugs

DrugKey Points
AmiodaroneUsed for any dysrhythmia; prolongs action potential/refractory period, blocks alpha/beta receptors. ~75% adverse-effect rate: thyroid alterations, corneal deposits, pulmonary toxicity (black box, ~10% fatal), hepatotoxicity, pro-arrhythmic effect. Increases digoxin toxicity risk and warfarin INR by 50–100%. Very long half-life.
AtropineSymptomatic sinus bradycardia only; anticholinergic, blocks the vagus nerve; works ~30% of the time. IV push, 1 mg q3–5min, max 3 mg. Requires continuous cardiac monitoring.
AdenosineSVT/PSVT; slows AV-node conduction; extremely short half-life; can cause brief asystole (shock pads on standby). IV push through a three-way stopcock + rapid saline flush; 6 mg, then up to 12 mg, then 12 mg again if needed.
DofetilideConverts/maintains sinus rhythm in AFib/flutter; blocks potassium channels; high torsades de pointes risk — always started in-hospital on telemetry; avoided with prolonged QT.

AFib/flutter patients also generally need warfarin (or a similar anticoagulant) for stroke prevention — review warfarin from prior pharm coursework.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer. Four cards from each of the five parts above — a whole-week spot check.

Why is the LAD artery nicknamed the "widow maker"?
It supplies a large portion of the heart muscle, so a blockage there is especially dangerous and lethal.
What is the hemodynamic chain that produces heart failure's hallmark fluid overload?
Contractility decreases first → preload increases (fluid backs up) → afterload increases.
What's the mnemonic for left- vs. right-sided heart failure, and what does it mean?
"Left = lungs, right = body" — left-sided HF backs up into the lungs (pulmonary congestion); right-sided HF backs up into the rest of the body (systemic congestion).
Distinguish HFrEF from HFpEF.
HFrEF ("systolic"): EF under 40%, impaired contractile function. HFpEF ("diastolic"): EF 40–50% (normal 55–65%), the muscle squeezes fine but the ventricles can't relax/fill.
What's the first rhythm in the narrow-to-wide QRS sequence where the QRS becomes wide, and why?
PVCs — they originate from an ectopic ventricular focus, producing a QRS over 0.12 seconds instead of the normal narrow QRS.
What is the "atrial kick," and what happens to it in AFib?
The atria's contribution to ventricular filling. In AFib the atria quiver instead of contracting, so the atrial kick is lost — about 20% of cardiac output.
What is the immediate nursing action for V-tach or V-fib?
Check for a pulse immediately. V-tach with a pulse follows the ACLS/antiarrhythmic pathway; pulseless V-tach or any V-fib means starting CPR right away.
Define bigeminy, trigeminy, and quadrigeminy.
Bigeminy = a PVC every other beat. Trigeminy = every third beat. Quadrigeminy = every fourth beat.
What is the single biggest risk factor for PAD, and why?
Smoking — more than 80% of PAD patients are current or former smokers, and it doubles amputation risk.
Where do stasis (venous) ulcers typically form, compared to arterial ulcers?
Stasis ulcers typically form at the ankles; arterial ulcers typically form at the toes or the top (dorsum) of the foot.
What ABI value indicates severe arterial disease?
0.5 or below (normal is roughly 1.0–1.4).
What is the "5 P's" mnemonic for significant arterial compromise?
Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesias, and Paralysis (weakness).
What is the blood-flow order through the heart's four valves, and the mnemonic for it?
Tricuspid → pulmonic → mitral → aortic ("TP-MA," or "tricycle before bicycle").
What is the classic triad of aortic stenosis?
Syncope, lightheadedness, and chest pain.
Which valve is most commonly affected by infective endocarditis, and why?
The tricuspid valve — it's the first valve blood encounters entering the heart, making it especially relevant to IV drug users.
Distinguish Osler's nodes from Janeway lesions.
Osler's nodes are tender, raised fingertip nodules; Janeway lesions are non-tender, flat spots on the palms or soles.
What should a patient do if chest pain isn't relieved within 5 minutes of sublingual nitroglycerin?
Call 911 — this raises concern for unstable angina or STEMI.
What is the hallmark sign of digoxin toxicity, and what should the nurse do before giving a dose?
Bradycardia is the hallmark sign; take a full 1-minute apical pulse before giving digoxin and hold the dose if it's below 60 bpm.
Why must adenosine be given through a three-way stopcock with an immediate saline flush?
Its half-life is so short that without an immediate rapid flush, the drug won't reach systemic circulation quickly enough to be effective.
Why is dofetilide always started in the hospital on continuous telemetry?
Because of its high risk of causing torsades de pointes, a dangerous polymorphic form of V-tach.
