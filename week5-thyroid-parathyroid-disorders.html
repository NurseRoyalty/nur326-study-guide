Thyroid & Parathyroid Disorders

Hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism are a mirror pair, much like Cushing's and Addison's disease — one is too little hormone, the other too much, and the manifestations largely run in opposite directions. The parathyroid glands, though physically embedded in the thyroid, regulate a completely different hormone (calcium via PTH), which is why they're covered together in this topic but as their own separate disorder.

Thyroid Background & the HPT Axis

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland located in the neck, about 2 inches wide. It secretes two major hormones, T3 and T4, and its overall job is to set the pace of body metabolism.

MNEMONIC: think of thyroid hormone as the "volume knob" for the body's metabolism — it can be turned up (hyperthyroidism) or turned down (hypothyroidism).
TermDetail
Triiodothyronine (T3)The ACTIVE form of thyroid hormone — it's what actually causes thyroid hormone's effects in the body.
Thyroxine (T4)Circulates in bound and unbound forms; when the body senses it needs more thyroid hormone activity, T4 becomes unbound and is converted into the active T3 form. T4 is the regulator of body metabolism and influences almost every body system.
IodineA necessary component in the synthesis of thyroid hormones — this is why iodine deficiency causes thyroid problems.

The HPT feedback loop

  • When the hypothalamus senses the body needs more metabolism ("turn the volume up"), it releases thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH).
  • TRH tells the anterior pituitary to produce thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH).
  • TSH is sensed by the thyroid gland and stimulates it to produce T4 (thyroxine, the largely inactive/prohormone form), which is then converted into T3 (the active form) out in the body.
  • Once the body senses enough T3 is circulating, it signals the hypothalamus to turn production back down (negative feedback).

Why this matters clinically: measuring TSH, T3, and T4 tells you WHERE the problem is — a problem above the thyroid gland (hypothalamus/pituitary) or a problem at the level of the thyroid gland itself. Thyroid dysfunction is common and can be hypothyroidism (not enough thyroid hormone) or hyperthyroidism (too much). Primary thyroid disorders — meaning the problem is at the level of the thyroid gland itself — are the most common type, and are much more common in women than men. Both hypo- and hyperthyroidism can cause an enlarged thyroid gland (goiter).

Goiter

An enlargement of the thyroid gland, which may or may not be accompanied by symptoms of thyroid dysfunction. If there are no symptoms of thyroid disease, it's called a "nontoxic" goiter.

TermDetail
Excess TSH stimulationSeen for example in populations/regions with iodine deficiency — low iodine means the thyroid can't make enough thyroid hormone even though the pituitary keeps increasing TSH trying to compensate; the gland enlarges but still can't produce more hormone because it lacks the iodine building block.
GoitrogensFoods or medications that promote thyroid gland enlargement, such as lithium (used for bipolar disorder) — this requires close monitoring of thyroid hormone levels in patients taking it.

Hypothyroidism

Not enough thyroid hormone (insufficient T3/T4).

Primary hypothyroidism (~95% of cases)

  • The most common type — the thyroid gland itself fails to secrete enough T3 and T4.
  • Labs: INCREASED TSH (the pituitary is trying to compensate) with DECREASED T3 and T4.

Secondary hypothyroidism

  • The pituitary itself fails to secrete enough TSH, so there isn't enough stimulus telling the thyroid to produce hormone in the first place.
  • Labs: DECREASED TSH with DECREASED T3 and T4.

Hashimoto's thyroiditis

  • An autoimmune disorder and the MOST COMMON cause of (primary) hypothyroidism.
  • Antibodies attack the thyroid gland directly.
  • Labs show low T3/T4 with high TSH (the pituitary correctly senses the body needs more, but the thyroid can't respond because of the autoimmune damage).
  • Diagnostic antibody tests: anti-thyroglobulin antibody, and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody — the anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody is considered the hallmark test for Hashimoto's.

Risk factors

  • Women older than 50
  • Caucasian
  • Pregnancy
  • Having another autoimmune disorder (such as lupus or rheumatoid arthritis)
  • Family history
  • Certain medications: amiodarone and lithium
  • Prior treatment for hyperthyroidism (such as radiation to the neck or radioactive iodine) that ends up "working too well" and pushes the patient into a hypothyroid state instead

Manifestations can be thought of as the metabolic "volume knob" being turned DOWN.

Early / nonspecific manifestations

  • Cold intolerance
  • Weight gain
  • Fatigue, lethargy
  • Memory deficits, poor attention span
  • Increased cholesterol
  • Muscle cramping
  • Constipation

Later / more severe manifestations

  • Puffy face
  • Hair loss, brittle nails
  • Decreased fertility (menstrual disturbances)
  • Skin yellowing
  • Very low body temperature
  • Low heart rate (bradycardia)
  • Significant weight gain
  • Decreased level of consciousness
  • Thick skin
  • Cardiac complications

Also commonly seen

  • Hyperlipidemia (often managed with a statin)
  • Anemia
  • Decreased kidney filtration
  • Increased risk of medication toxicity
  • Hoarse voice
Myxedema coma is a life-threatening complication of severe, prolonged, untreated hypothyroidism, causing loss of brain function. Myxedema is the term for SEVERE hypothyroidism that has gone undiagnosed or untreated for a long period of time; it can progress to myxedema coma, which is on the extreme end of the hypothyroidism spectrum and not commonly seen. The term "myxedema" is also used to describe specific dermatologic changes (thickening of the skin and extreme hair loss) more consistently associated with hypothyroidism, though it can technically be seen in hyperthyroidism too.

Hypothyroidism — Treatment

Levothyroxine (Synthroid)

  • A synthetic T4 hormone that the body converts to active T3 as needed.
  • Has a very long half-life, so dosing consistency between doses is important.
  • Takes time to work — patients typically don't see improved symptoms until about a month after starting.
  • Too high a dose can cause hyperthyroid symptoms; too low a dose means persistent hypothyroid symptoms.
  • Administration: taken on an empty stomach, usually about 30 minutes before eating, first thing in the morning (food and other drugs can reduce its absorption) — most patients are taught to keep it at their bedside and take it right when they wake up.
  • Needed for LIFE (with rare exception, such as if the thyroid gland is surgically removed for another reason).
  • Important drug interaction: levothyroxine increases the effect of warfarin (an anticoagulant), increasing the risk of bleeding — this combination needs closer monitoring.

Hyperthyroidism (Thyrotoxicosis)

Too much T3/T4 (excessive secretion); historically called thyrotoxicosis.

Classifications

  • Primary: the thyroid gland itself overproduces T3/T4, even while the pituitary is telling it (via low TSH) that it doesn't need to — the gland simply doesn't "listen" to that feedback and keeps overproducing. Graves' disease is the classic example and the most common cause overall.
  • Secondary: very uncommon — a pituitary problem (the pituitary is secreting too much TSH).
  • Tertiary: very uncommon — a hypothalamic problem (excess secretion of TRH).
  • Other causes: a thyroid adenoma, subacute thyroiditis, toxic multinodular goiter, or excessive thyroid hormone replacement (overmedication with levothyroxine).

Risk factors

  • Family history of Graves' disease
  • Age over 40, Caucasian
  • Amiodarone (contains iodine) can cause EITHER hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism — same drug, either direction
  • Excessive iodine intake
  • Pregnancy (pregnancy hormones can stimulate TSH receptors)

Graves' disease

  • An autoimmune condition — thyroid-stimulating antibodies bind to and activate TSH receptors on the thyroid, causing continuous overproduction and enlargement of the gland.
  • This is a primary condition (the thyroid itself is excessively secreting) with a feedback loop that lowers TSH, but the thyroid doesn't listen to it.

Manifestations can be thought of as the metabolic "volume knob" turned UP — essentially the opposite of hypothyroidism.

Manifestations

  • Nervousness, insomnia (CNS amped up)
  • Heat sensitivity/intolerance
  • Weight loss (accelerated metabolism)
  • Thyroid gland usually enlarged and palpable; a bruit (an abnormal sound from high blood flow) may be heard over the gland
  • Enhanced sensitivity to catecholamines puts these patients at risk for cardiac arrhythmias (especially atrial fibrillation) and heart failure

Graves' ophthalmopathy

  • Exophthalmos (bulging eyes) with periorbital edema — an inflammatory eye disorder that develops around the eyes and can cause vision impairment.
  • Common with Graves' disease specifically; women are more often affected than men.
  • Teaching point: any patient with a history of hyperthyroidism/Graves' disease needs an eye exam.

Hyperthyroidism — Treatment & Thyroid Storm

Antithyroid medication: PTU

  • Propylthiouracil (PTU) blocks thyroid hormone synthesis and suppresses the conversion of T4 to T3.
  • Carries a black box warning for hepatotoxicity (liver toxicity).
  • Can be used cautiously during the FIRST trimester of pregnancy, but needs to be changed to a different medication for the second and third trimesters because of the hepatotoxicity risk.

Radioactive iodine & surgery

  • Radioactive iodine treatment is the MORE COMMON treatment — the thyroid gland takes up the radioactive iodine, which suppresses its activity.
  • Surgery (thyroidectomy) removes the gland — the patient will need lifelong levothyroxine/thyroid hormone replacement afterward, since there's no thyroid gland left to produce it.
Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis) can result in death within 48 hours if not treated. This is the extreme, life-threatening end of the hyperthyroid spectrum — similar in concept to myxedema coma being the extreme end of hypothyroidism. It is typically triggered by a major stressor: a severe infection, major surgery, or any major release of stress hormones. This causes an overwhelming release of thyroid hormone that places intense stimulus on the body's metabolism — everything is "revved up," and the body cannot tolerate this state for long.

Parathyroid Disorders

The parathyroid glands are four small glands located within/near the actual thyroid tissue. They produce and secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH). PTH is released in response to LOW calcium levels (hypocalcemia) and works to control calcium levels in the body — it promotes vitamin D production/activation by the kidney, which helps raise calcium.

Hypoparathyroidism

  • Insufficient PTH secretion, leading to hypocalcemia.
  • A rare disorder, usually caused by trauma or damage to the parathyroid glands during thyroid surgery (thyroidectomy) — since the parathyroid glands sit right on/near the thyroid, they can be accidentally removed or damaged along with the thyroid gland during that surgery.
  • Any patient who has had their thyroid gland removed should be monitored for resulting hypocalcemia.
  • Manifestations of the resulting hypocalcemia: muscle cramping, irritability, tetany, and convulsions; also check for Trousseau's sign and Chvostek's sign.
  • Also see an INCREASE in phosphate levels (since PTH normally increases phosphate excretion by the kidneys — low PTH means the kidneys retain more phosphate); phosphate levels should also be monitored.
  • Treatment: synthetic parathyroid hormone replacement, normalizing serum calcium and vitamin D levels — this is lifelong treatment.

Hyperparathyroidism

  • Excess PTH secretion, resulting in hypercalcemia and bone breakdown.
  • Manifestations: muscle weakness, poor concentration, hypertension, and kidney stones — all related to the excessive circulating calcium levels.
  • Treatment: diuretics (to help excrete more calcium), calcitonin (helps regulate/lower calcium and phosphate levels in the blood), bisphosphonates (help reduce bone breakdown — the same drug class used for osteoporosis), vitamin D (helps calcium be more effectively absorbed), or surgical intervention if the cause is something like a tumor releasing excess parathyroid hormone.

Both hypo- and hyperparathyroidism are considered rare conditions overall.

Which thyroid hormone, T3 or T4, is the ACTIVE form?
T3 (triiodothyronine) is the active form; T4 (thyroxine) circulates largely as a prohormone and is converted to T3 when the body needs more thyroid hormone activity.
What is the order of the HPT feedback loop, and why does checking TSH/T3/T4 matter clinically?
Hypothalamus releases TRH → anterior pituitary releases TSH → thyroid produces T4 → T4 converts to T3 in the body. Measuring TSH, T3, and T4 tells you whether the problem is above the thyroid (hypothalamus/pituitary) or at the thyroid gland itself.
Compare the TSH pattern in primary vs. secondary hypothyroidism.
Primary (thyroid gland failure, ~95% of cases): INCREASED TSH with decreased T3/T4. Secondary (pituitary failure to secrete TSH): DECREASED TSH with decreased T3/T4.
What is the hallmark antibody test for Hashimoto's thyroiditis?
Anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody (anti-thyroglobulin antibody is also checked, but anti-thyroid peroxidase is considered the hallmark test).
Name three early/nonspecific manifestations of hypothyroidism.
Any three of: cold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue/lethargy, memory deficits/poor attention span, increased cholesterol, muscle cramping, constipation.
What is myxedema coma?
A life-threatening complication of severe, prolonged, untreated hypothyroidism, causing loss of brain function — the extreme end of the hypothyroidism spectrum.
How should a patient take levothyroxine?
On an empty stomach, about 30 minutes before eating, first thing in the morning — food and other drugs reduce its absorption. It's often kept at the bedside and taken right on waking, and is needed for life.
What is the mechanism behind Graves' disease?
Thyroid-stimulating autoantibodies bind to and activate TSH receptors on the thyroid, causing continuous overproduction of T3/T4 and enlargement of the gland, even though the resulting low TSH feedback should be telling it to stop.
What is Graves' ophthalmopathy?
An inflammatory eye disorder causing exophthalmos (bulging eyes) with periorbital edema that can impair vision; it's common with Graves' disease and more frequent in women — any patient with a history of Graves' needs an eye exam.
Why is thyroid storm dangerous?
Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis) is the extreme end of the hyperthyroid spectrum, usually triggered by a major stressor (severe infection, major surgery), and can result in death within 48 hours if not treated.
Why should a patient who had a thyroidectomy be monitored for hypocalcemia, and what two bedside signs would you check?
The parathyroid glands sit on/near the thyroid and can be accidentally damaged or removed during thyroid surgery, causing hypoparathyroidism and hypocalcemia. Check Trousseau's sign and Chvostek's sign.
What are the manifestations and treatments of hyperparathyroidism?
Excess PTH causes hypercalcemia and bone breakdown: muscle weakness, poor concentration, hypertension, and kidney stones. Treatment: diuretics, calcitonin, bisphosphonates, vitamin D, or surgery if caused by a tumor.
Why does iodine deficiency cause goiter?
Iodine is a necessary building block for thyroid hormone synthesis. Without enough of it, the thyroid can't make enough hormone even as the pituitary keeps raising TSH to compensate — the gland enlarges without actually producing more hormone.
What is a goitrogen? Give an example.
A food or medication that promotes thyroid gland enlargement. Example: lithium, which requires close monitoring of thyroid hormone levels.
Name three risk factors for Hashimoto's thyroiditis.
Any three of: women over 50, Caucasian, pregnancy, having another autoimmune disorder, family history, amiodarone or lithium use, or prior treatment for hyperthyroidism.
Name three later/more severe manifestations of hypothyroidism.
Any three of: puffy face, hair loss/brittle nails, decreased fertility, skin yellowing, very low body temperature, bradycardia, significant weight gain, decreased level of consciousness, thick skin, or cardiac complications.
What important drug interaction should you watch for with levothyroxine?
It increases the effect of warfarin, increasing bleeding risk — this combination needs closer monitoring.
Among the three classifications of hyperthyroidism, which one does Graves' disease represent, and how common is it?
Primary hyperthyroidism — the thyroid itself overproduces T3/T4 despite low-TSH feedback telling it to stop. Graves' disease is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism overall.
What does a radioactive iodine uptake scan show in Graves' disease?
Increased uptake, diffusely distributed over the entire gland — as opposed to a focal area, which would instead point toward a more localized process like a cancerous nodule.
Why does hypoparathyroidism cause an increase in phosphate levels?
PTH normally increases phosphate excretion by the kidneys, so with low PTH the kidneys retain more phosphate.
