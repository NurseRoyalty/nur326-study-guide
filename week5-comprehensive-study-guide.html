Part 1 — Esophageal & Gastric Disorders (Upper GI)

These conditions are grouped together because they're all "upper GI" disorders — involving the esophagus and stomach — and they share overlapping symptoms (heartburn, epigastric pain, dysphagia) and overlapping pharmacologic treatments.

Dysphagia

Dysphagia means difficulty swallowing. It typically affects swallowing solids first, then progresses to also affect liquids.

Mechanical Causes

  • Stenosis
  • Strictures
  • Diverticula
  • Tumors — physically block the esophagus

Neuromuscular Causes

  • Stroke — the esophagus loses proper innervation
  • Achalasia — the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) can't open properly

Patients who have been intubated or have a tracheostomy are also a common population with dysphagia; it can either resolve or progress.

GERD (Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease)

In GERD, the LES doesn't close properly, allowing highly acidic stomach contents to reflux backward into the esophagus, causing heartburn. It occurs from things that either weaken LES closure strength or increase abdominal pressure — fatty/spicy/tomato-based foods, citrus, caffeine, alcohol, smoking, certain sleep positions, obesity, pregnancy, and certain drugs.

SymptomDetail
PyrosisThe medical term for heartburn — a burning sensation.
DyspepsiaIndigestion.
RegurgitationFeeling like burping up hot, acidic material.
Chest pain / dysphagia / pulmonary symptomsCan occur with reflux, especially if severe or chronic.

Complications of Chronic GERD

  • Esophageal ulcerations
  • Scarring and strictures within the esophagus
  • Barrett's esophagus — the most severe complication; abnormal, metaplastic (precancerous) cell changes develop in the esophageal lining
Barrett's esophagus carries a threefold risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma, with very low survival if it develops — providers monitor for it in long-term GERD patients. There is no real pharmacologic treatment for existing strictures or Barrett's esophagus itself; management focuses on preventing these complications by treating the underlying GERD and avoiding triggers.

Hiatal Hernia

A hiatal hernia is a defect in the diaphragm that allows part of the stomach to pass upward into the thoracic (chest) cavity.

Sliding Hernia

  • The peritoneum stays intact, which restrains/limits the size of the hernia
  • Smaller, more common, and less severe

Paraesophageal ("Rolling") Hernia

  • Part of the stomach pushes through the diaphragm and stays there permanently, above the diaphragm
  • The peritoneal membrane becomes thin/defective, forming a true sac
  • More severe than a sliding hernia

Associated with aging, diaphragm-weakening injury, chronic pressure (coughing, vomiting, straining), and obesity. Many patients are asymptomatic; others have belching, dysphagia, or GERD-like chest/epigastric pain, since GERD and hiatal hernia commonly coexist. Treatment is mostly conservative (small frequent meals, avoid lying down after eating, weight control, GERD pharmacotherapy), with surgery reserved for cases where conservative treatment isn't adhered to or isn't effective.

Gastritis

Gastritis is a non-specific inflammatory condition of the stomach — the "-itis" suffix means inflammation, a suffix worth watching for across this week's GI conditions.

Acute Gastritis

  • Temporary inflammation of the stomach lining only, generally lasting 2–10 days
  • Related to irritating substances/drugs/infectious agents: alcohol, NSAIDs, H. pylori, stomach viruses

Chronic Gastritis

  • A progressive disorder lasting weeks to years
  • Autoimmune (parietal cells attacked) or from chronic H. pylori infection
  • Can lead to peptic ulcer disease, bleeding ulcers, anemia, and increased gastric cancer risk

NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis; prostaglandins normally stimulate protective mucus secretion and inhibit gastric acid production, so NSAIDs remove that protection and increase acid. H. pylori is a gram-negative spiral bacterium that thrives in an acidic environment, transmitted via saliva, fecal matter, vomit, or contaminated food/water — it can cause chronic gastritis and PUD and increases stomach cancer risk. Symptoms: vomiting, epigastric burning, upset stomach, loss of appetite; severe cases show vomiting blood or black stool; classic finding is postprandial discomfort.

Acute Gastroenteritis

Involves inflammation of both the stomach and the small intestine — unlike gastritis, which is stomach only. Caused by stomach viruses (norovirus, rotavirus — the classic "stomach flu"), bacterial infections (E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter), or parasites. Usually lasts 1–3 days, up to 10. Symptoms: watery diarrhea (possibly bloody if bacterial), abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, fever, malaise.

The biggest concern is fluid volume deficit/dehydration, since the patient is losing fluid from both ends. Usually managed supportively; if symptoms last more than 48–72 hours, the patient should see a provider, and antibiotics may be needed if a bacterial cause is suspected.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

PUD is ulceration of the upper GI tract — esophageal, gastric, or duodenal (the first part of the small intestine). It develops when aggressive factors (H. pylori, NSAID use, excess acid/pepsin, smoking) overwhelm protective/defensive factors (mucus secretion, bicarbonate secretion, adequate blood flow, prostaglandins).

Stress does NOT directly cause peptic ulcers — this outdated belief has been re-thought. However, stress can worsen an existing ulcer, since the stress response increases gastric acid secretion. Pathophysiology: mucosal damage triggers an immune response; histamine increases acid/pepsin secretion, causing further damage and vasodilation/edema; if blood vessels near the ulcer are destroyed, this causes bleeding ulcers.

Gastric Ulcer

  • Tends to occur later in life (age 50–70), tied to chronic risk factors (long-term NSAID/corticosteroid/anticoagulant use)
  • Pain occurs 1–2 hours after eating, as the stomach empties

Duodenal Ulcer

  • The most common location for peptic ulcers overall; can occur at any age, often earlier adulthood
  • Pain occurs 2–4 hours after eating, once food has passed through
MNEMONIC — HOP (Complications of PUD): Hemorrhage, Obstruction, Perforation.

Pharmacology for GERD & Peptic Ulcer Disease

Treatment works by either decreasing aggressive factors (H. pylori treatment, H2 blockers, PPIs) or increasing protective factors (sucralfate, antacids).

TermDetail
H. pylori treatmentCombination antibiotic therapy plus a gastric acid inhibitor, ~10–14 days, only after a confirmed breath test. Can cost ~$200 with up to 12 pills/day, so adherence is often poor.
H2 receptor blockersFamotidine (preferred) and cimetidine (older, CYP450 interactions with warfarin/phenytoin/theophylline) reduce gastric acid ~60–70%. Give IV slowly (bradycardia risk); separate from antacids by at least 1 hour.
Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)The "-prazole" drugs (omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole) irreversibly inhibit the H+/K+ ATPase "proton pump." Work better than H2 blockers; long-term use linked to pneumonia, bone loss/fractures, and stomach cancer risk.
SucralfateMucosal protectant forming a sticky gel barrier; FDA-approved for duodenal ulcers. PO only, before meals; separate other drugs by ~2 hours (decreases their absorption).
AntacidsNeutralize ~50% of stomach acid. Intense rebound heartburn after stopping. Aluminum/calcium bases → constipation; magnesium base → diarrhea. Separate from other drugs by 1–2 hours (chelation).

Part 2 — Lower GI Disorders

These conditions are grouped as "lower GI" because they involve the small intestine and below, as opposed to the esophagus and stomach covered in Part 1.

Appendicitis

Inflammation of the appendix that becomes obstructed. The obstruction itself isn't the biggest concern — it's the potential complications: rupture, gangrene, abscess, and ultimately peritonitis. Classic pain: right lower quadrant (RLQ) and periumbilical area, with rebound tenderness. Peak incidence age 10–12; usually begins as dull, steady pain progressing quickly over 4–6 hours to localize in the RLQ, with fever, nausea, and anorexia.

Sudden, complete relief of severe pain is NOT a good sign. It can indicate the appendix has ruptured, putting the patient at very high risk for peritonitis.

Peritonitis

Inflammation of the peritoneum, the serous membrane lining the abdominal cavity and covering the visceral organs. Causes: perforated gastric/duodenal ulcers, ruptured gallbladder, pancreatitis, ruptured spleen/bladder/appendix — notice the theme of rupture or perforation letting contents that belong inside the gut/organ get outside it. Pathophysiology: inflammation causes fluid shifts (third spacing) that can lead to hypovolemic shock and sepsis, and decreased peristalsis that can cause paralytic ileus and obstruction. Presentation: sudden, severe abdominal pain, a rigid board-like abdomen, nausea/vomiting, fever, elevated WBC, and increased heart rate with decreased blood pressure (mostly from the fluid shifts). Treatment is difficult — the priority is fixing the underlying cause, which then decreases the inflammation.

Pancreatitis and ruptured gallbladder are two of the most common causes of peritonitis — both are covered in full on Gallbladder & Pancreatic Disorders (Week 4); this page does not re-teach them

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

A chronic condition of altered bowel pattern from changes in intestinal motility, associated with chronic/frequent constipation (IBS-C) or diarrhea (IBS-D). Symptoms: abdominal distension/bloating, intermittent pain exacerbated by stress and relieved by defecation, bowel urgency, food intolerances (sorbitol, lactose, gluten), sometimes mucus in stool. Cause is unknown, thought to be triggered by stress, food, hormonal changes, GI infections, and menstrual cycle changes. Psychosocial stress is almost never a primary cause but can exacerbate symptoms — and the relationship runs both directions, since IBS itself is stressful to live with.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD): Crohn's Disease & Ulcerative Colitis

IBD is an umbrella term for Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — chronic illnesses with periods of exacerbation and remission, more common in women, Caucasians, people of Jewish descent, and smokers, generally thought of as autoimmune conditions activated by infection.

Toxic megacolon is a rapid, life-threatening dilation of the large intestine — a serious complication of ulcerative colitis.

Both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis put patients at high risk of VTE/DVT.

Diverticular Disease

Diverticulosis is the development of diverticula — small pouches bulging outward through a weak spot in the colon wall, thought to be caused by a low-fiber diet leading to chronic constipation. Usually occurs in the descending colon and is generally asymptomatic. Diverticulitis occurs when a pouch becomes inflamed, usually from lodged fecal material — presenting with left lower quadrant (LLQ) pain (the opposite side from appendicitis's classic RLQ), fever, elevated WBC, abnormal bowel patterns, sometimes frank blood, and risk of perforation/peritonitis/obstruction if severe.

Pharmacology for Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Drug ClassDetail
5-ASA (Sulfasalazine)Converts in the intestines into 5-aminosalicylic acid (active anti-inflammatory) and sulfapyridine (causes most side effects). Contraindicated with a sulfa allergy. Used for mild-to-moderate IBD; mesalamine is an alternative in the same family.

Part 3 — GI Symptom Pharmacology

These drug classes treat nausea/vomiting and diarrhea as symptoms that show up across many different GI conditions, rather than belonging to any single disease.

DrugMechanism & Key Points
Ondansetron (Zofran)Serotonin (5-HT3) receptor blocker acting on the chemoreceptor trigger zone; affects afferent vagal nerves. PO or IV. Watch for serotonin syndrome with SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs/MAOIs/buspirone/tramadol, especially with daily combined use.
Dimenhydrinate & HydroxyzineH1 receptor blockers (NOT H2) acting in the inner ear — used for motion sickness, dizziness, and nausea. Cause sedation and anticholinergic effects (elderly fall risk, similar to diphenhydramine/Benadryl). Never give hydroxyzine IV — can cause tissue damage, thrombosis, gangrene (IM only).
Metoclopramide (Reglan)Blocks dopamine receptors, increases LES tone, increases GI peristalsis. Used as an antiemetic (chemo/radiation/opioid-induced nausea) and a prokinetic (CF, post-op ileus). Rare but severe risk of EPS, more common with regular/prolonged use or combined with antipsychotics.
MNEMONIC: Anticholinergic toxidrome — "Mad as a hatter" (CNS changes), "blind as a bat" (blurred vision), "dry as a bone" (dry secretions), "red as a beet" (flushing), "hot as a hare" (hyperthermia).
EPS TypeDetail
AkathisiaRestlessness — a constant urge to move.
Acute dystoniaInvoluntary muscle contractions.
ParkinsonismTremors and rigid limb muscles, like Parkinson's disease.
Tardive dyskinesiaLate-onset repetitive involuntary facial movements (tongue twisting, chewing, lip smacking, grimacing).
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is the worst-case EPS complication — rare but life-threatening. First signs: rigid muscles and fever, then drowsiness/confusion, which can progress to seizures.

Antidiarrheal drugs work by decreasing intestinal peristalsis, reducing fluid output. Main concerns: drowsiness and constipation. Atropine-containing combination products can cause bradycardia (anticholinergic, drying effect) — use caution with driving/alertness-requiring activities.

Part 4 — Endocrine Disorders (Adrenal Cortex, Adrenal Medulla & ADH)

This section covers the adrenal glands end to end: the adrenal cortex (Cushing's syndrome and Addison's disease — a cortisol excess/deficiency mirror pair) and the adrenal medulla plus ADH disorders (pheochromocytoma, SIADH, and diabetes insipidus).

MNEMONIC: The "3 S's" of adrenal cortex hormones — Sugar = cortisol, Salt = aldosterone, Sex = androgens.

Cortisol's normal functions: increases glucose availability, maintains vascular tone/BP, breaks down fat/protein for energy, suppresses the immune/inflammatory response, and activates CNS alertness. Nearly every Cushing's and Addison's finding traces back to one of these functions being pushed into excess or deficit.

FeatureCushing's Syndrome (Cortisol Excess)Addison's Disease (Cortisol Deficiency)
CausePrimary (adrenal tumor), secondary (pituitary excess ACTH), or exogenous (chronic steroid use — most common cause in the U.S.)Idiopathic, or (most common) autoimmune destruction of the adrenal cortex
Glucose effectGlucose intolerance, hyperglycemiaHypoglycemia, weakness, fatigue
BP / electrolyte effectHypertension; sodium/fluid retentionLow BP/vascular tone/cardiac output; hyponatremia and hyperkalemia
Body habitusMoon face, buffalo hump, central obesity with thin limbs, striae, easy bruising, thin skinHyperpigmentation/tanned appearance (excess ACTH + MSH co-secretion)
TreatmentTreat the cause (surgery/radiation) or slowly taper exogenous steroids; aminoglutethimide or ketoconazole as a temporary bridgeLifelong hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone; never abruptly stop

Manifestations typically don't become evident in Addison's until about 90% of the adrenal cortex has been destroyed. Sick-day teaching uses the "3-by-3 rule" — triple the usual glucocorticoid dose for three days during any stress event. Corticosteroid therapy in Addison's disease can NEVER be abruptly stopped — doing so can precipitate an Addisonian crisis, a life-threatening emergency. The mirror-image acute emergency, "severe Cushing syndrome," presents as massively elevated cortisol with severe hypokalemia, sepsis risk, uncontrolled hypertension, and heart failure.

Pheochromocytoma

A rare tumor of the adrenal medulla causing excessive catecholamine (epinephrine/norepinephrine) release — about 90% benign (the "-oma" suffix typically signals a benign tumor). Hallmark: hypertension (intermittent or persistent) with the classic triad of headache, diaphoresis, and tachycardia, triggered by anything that stimulates the sympathetic nervous system.

Benign does not mean safe: the resulting severe hypertension puts the patient at high risk for stroke or cardiac events.

Surgical removal is definitive treatment. Phenoxybenzamine, a noncompetitive (irreversible) alpha blocker, is given ~10–14 days before surgery to control blood pressure — irreversibility is desirable here since tumor manipulation during surgery can release a massive catecholamine burst.

Part 5 — Thyroid & Parathyroid Disorders

Hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism are a mirror pair, much like Cushing's and Addison's disease. The parathyroid glands, though physically embedded in the thyroid, regulate a completely different hormone (calcium via PTH).

MNEMONIC: Thyroid hormone is the "volume knob" for body metabolism — turned up (hyperthyroidism) or down (hypothyroidism).

The HPT feedback loop: hypothalamus releases TRH → anterior pituitary releases TSH → thyroid produces T4 (largely inactive/prohormone) → T4 converts to T3 (the active form) in the body. Iodine is a necessary building block for thyroid hormone synthesis — iodine deficiency causes goiter, since the thyroid can't make enough hormone even as TSH keeps rising to compensate. Goitrogens (like lithium) also promote thyroid enlargement.

TSHIncreased (primary, ~95% of cases) / decreased (secondary)Decreased (primary)
T3 / T4DecreasedIncreased
Most common causeHashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune) — hallmark test: anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodyGraves' disease (autoimmune — thyroid-stimulating antibodies activate TSH receptors)
Key manifestationsCold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue, bradycardia, constipation; later: puffy face, hair loss, decreased LOCHeat intolerance, weight loss, nervousness/insomnia, tachyarrhythmias; Graves' ophthalmopathy (exophthalmos)
EmergencyMyxedema coma — loss of brain function from severe, prolonged, untreated hypothyroidismThyroid storm — can cause death within 48 hours if untreated, usually triggered by major stress

Amiodarone can cause EITHER hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism — same drug, either direction. Hashimoto's risk factors: women over 50, Caucasian, pregnancy, another autoimmune disorder, family history, amiodarone/lithium, or prior hyperthyroidism treatment.

Parathyroid Disorders

The four parathyroid glands secrete PTH in response to low calcium; PTH promotes vitamin D activation by the kidney to help raise calcium.

Hypoparathyroidism

  • Usually from thyroidectomy trauma/damage to the parathyroid glands.
  • Causes hypocalcemia (cramping, tetany, convulsions; check Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs) and increased phosphate (PTH normally increases phosphate excretion).
  • Treatment: lifelong synthetic PTH replacement, normalized calcium and vitamin D.

Hyperparathyroidism

  • Excess PTH causes hypercalcemia and bone breakdown: muscle weakness, poor concentration, hypertension, kidney stones.
  • Treatment: diuretics, calcitonin, bisphosphonates, vitamin D, or surgery if caused by a tumor.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer. Four cards from each of the five parts above — a whole-week spot check.

Why is Barrett's esophagus considered dangerous?
It involves abnormal, precancerous (metaplastic) cell changes in the esophageal lining and carries a threefold risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma, with very low survival if it develops.
What does the mnemonic HOP stand for in PUD complications?
Hemorrhage, Obstruction, Perforation.
How does gastric ulcer pain timing differ from duodenal ulcer pain timing?
Gastric ulcer pain occurs 1–2 hours after eating (as the stomach empties); duodenal ulcer pain occurs 2–4 hours after eating (once food reaches that area).
How do proton pump inhibitors reduce stomach acid?
They irreversibly inhibit the H+/K+ ATPase ("proton pump") enzyme in gastric parietal cells, blocking acid secretion.
A patient's severe appendicitis pain suddenly and completely resolves. What should this raise concern for?
A ruptured appendix, putting the patient at very high risk for peritonitis.
What is the key transmural-vs-mucosal distinction between Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis?
Crohn's is transmural (affects all layers of the bowel wall); ulcerative colitis affects the mucosa only.
What is toxic megacolon, and which IBD condition is it associated with?
A rapid, life-threatening dilation of the large intestine — a serious complication of ulcerative colitis.
Diverticulitis pain classically occurs in which quadrant, and how does that compare to appendicitis?
The left lower quadrant (LLQ) — the opposite side from appendicitis's classic right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain.
Why should H1-blocking antiemetics like dimenhydrinate and hydroxyzine be used cautiously in elderly patients?
They cause significant sedation and have anticholinergic effects, both of which raise fall risk.
What are the first signs of neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), and what drug used for nausea can trigger it?
Rigid muscles and fever, progressing to drowsiness/confusion and potentially seizures. Metoclopramide can trigger it because it blocks dopamine.
Why is ondansetron risky when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, or MAOIs?
All are serotonergic — combining them raises the risk of serotonin syndrome, especially with daily combined use.
What is the mnemonic for the anticholinergic toxidrome?
"Mad as a hatter, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, hot as a hare."
What is the "3 S's" mnemonic for adrenal cortex hormones?
Sugar = cortisol, Salt = aldosterone, Sex = androgens.
What must never be done with corticosteroid therapy in Addison's disease, and why?
It must never be abruptly stopped — doing so can precipitate an Addisonian crisis, a life-threatening emergency.
What is the classic triad of pheochromocytoma?
Headache, diaphoresis, and tachycardia, occurring with episodes of hypertension.
Why doesn't desmopressin (DDAVP) work for nephrogenic diabetes insipidus?
Because the kidneys themselves can't respond to ADH even when it's present — the problem isn't the ADH supply. A thiazide diuretic (HCTZ) is used instead.
Compare the TSH pattern in primary vs. secondary hypothyroidism.
Primary (thyroid gland failure, ~95% of cases): increased TSH with decreased T3/T4. Secondary (pituitary failure): decreased TSH with decreased T3/T4.
What is the mechanism behind Graves' disease?
Thyroid-stimulating autoantibodies bind to and activate TSH receptors, causing continuous overproduction of T3/T4 despite low-TSH feedback that should tell the gland to stop.
Why is thyroid storm dangerous?
It's the extreme end of the hyperthyroid spectrum and can result in death within 48 hours if not treated, typically triggered by a major stressor like severe infection or surgery.
Why should a patient who had a thyroidectomy be monitored for hypocalcemia?
The parathyroid glands sit on/near the thyroid and can be accidentally damaged or removed during thyroid surgery, causing hypoparathyroidism and hypocalcemia — check Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs.
