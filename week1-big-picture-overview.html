How the Three Topics Cluster — The "When" Framework

Week 1 is really teaching you to match drugs to timing and indication. The three topics show this progression:

ScenarioTopic & Approach
"I have symptoms NOW — bacteria or virus?"Antimicrobials & Antibiotics: Get a culture first if safe to delay. If not (meningitis, sepsis), treat empirically based on the most likely organism. Distinguish bacterial from viral by the clinical presentation, then pick the drug that hits the organism's vulnerable point.
"It's a virus — which one, and how fast must I act?"Antivirals: Recognize that antivirals work during replication only. Oseltamivir has a 48-hour window; herpes drugs have a wider window. CMV is special — only in immunocompromised patients. The drug class is small; you need to know specifically which virus you're treating.
"It's HIV — now we're planning years, not days."HIV & ART: A single drug fails; combination therapy (HAART) is standard. CD4 count tells you how immune-depleted the patient is. Adherence is life-or-death — missing doses drives resistance. This is a chronic condition with acute complications, not an infection to be "cured" in the classic sense.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

Every danger callout across the three pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Antimicrobials & AntibioticsFluoroquinolones can rupture tendons — especially in older patients, those on corticosteroids, and with quinolone use in the past 3 months. Watch for tendon pain and report it immediately.
Antimicrobials & AntibioticsAminoglycosides are nephrotoxic and ototoxic — check SCr and trough levels before each dose; hearing loss can be permanent; use once-daily dosing when possible to reduce toxicity.
Antimicrobials & AntibioticsCarbapenems (imipenem, meropenem) lower seizure threshold — especially risky in patients with renal impairment, CNS infection, or history of seizures. Never combine with ganciclovir.
AntiviralsIV acyclovir causes tissue necrosis if it extravasates — ensure the IV line is very patent, or use a central line; watch for pain, swelling, or blanching at the site.
AntiviralsOseltamivir MUST start within 48 hours of symptom onset — after that window, the virus has usually finished replicating and the drug won't help.
AntiviralsGanciclovir has FOUR black-box warnings: hematologic toxicity (monitor labs for pancytopenia), fertility impairment (both men and women), fetal toxicity/birth defects, and possible carcinogenicity. Avoid combining with imipenem (seizure risk).
HIV & ARTMissing doses of ART drives resistance — resistant strains can emerge within DAYS. Adherence is the difference between viral suppression and treatment failure. Missed doses early in therapy are more dangerous than missed doses later.
HIV & ARTIRIS (Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome) can occur within weeks of starting ART — the immune system suddenly becomes strong enough to mount an inflammatory response to opportunistic infections. Can be life-threatening if not recognized.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Know the organism first, or start empirically with the broadest option." ABX teach this first — culture if you can wait, treat empirically if you can't. Antivirals narrow this to specific viruses; HIV requires the broadest combination (HAART) to prevent resistance.
  • "Timing windows matter." Meningitis can't wait for culture (ABX). Oseltamivir has a 48-hour window (Antivirals). ART adherence is measured in days/hours — resistance emerges fast (HIV/ART).
  • "The same drug can cause multiple serious toxicities." Aminoglycosides are both nephrotoxic and ototoxic. Ganciclovir has four black-box warnings. Several ART drugs carry hepatotoxicity, rash, and lipodystrophy warnings together.
  • "Drug interactions can be deadly." Imipenem + ganciclovir = seizures. Many ART drugs interact with each other or with other medications; the combination matters more than any single drug.
  • "Extravasation, poor penetration, and renal clearance come up across all three topics." They're mechanisms that determine whether the drug reaches the right place and at the right dose — central to why some drugs work and others fail.
