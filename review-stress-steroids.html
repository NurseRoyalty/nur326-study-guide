Stress & Steroids

NUR 325 review, covering: homeostasis and allostasis, stress versus stressors, General Adaptation Syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion), the sympathetic/adrenergic and HPA-axis stress pathways, allostatic load and overload, chronic stress effects, and corticosteroid pharmacology (indications, dosing, drugs, side effects, and patient safety teaching).

Homeostasis vs. Allostasis

TermMeaning
HomeostasisFrom Greek homeo (same/similar) + stasis (stable/standing still). The state where all body systems are balanced around an ideal set point (temperature, vital signs, blood pH). Each person's normal set point falls within a range — one person's resting heart rate of 55 and another's of 75 can both be a normal, healthy set point.
AllostasisFrom Greek allo (variable/different) + stasis (stable/standing still). The dynamic process the body uses to achieve and return to a steady state — the body's overall attempt to get itself back to homeostasis. Allostasis recognizes that a set point may need to shift temporarily to meet an anticipated demand (e.g., respiratory rate rising during exercise to maintain the oxygenation set point) and may involve multiple physiologic systems at once.
Word roots: homeo-stasis = staying the same (the balanced set point itself); allo-stasis = the variable, active process that gets the body back to — or adjusts — that set point.

Examples of homeostasis in action: blood pH depends on CO₂ concentration, which the respiratory system regulates; core temperature is hypothalamus-mediated; and blood pressure is sensed by stretch receptors called baroreceptors, which trigger sympathetic activity to raise heart rate and stroke volume when pressure drops (the full baroreceptor and blood-pressure regulation picture is covered in Hypertension & Hyperlipidemia).

Stress, Stressors, Eustress & Distress

TermMeaning
StressorThe actual force or challenge causing stress (e.g., an exam).
StressThe physical, chemical, or emotional tension the body and mind feel in response to a stressor (e.g., how you feel in your body before that exam).
EustressPositive stress — feeling happy, accomplished, excited, or nervous in a good way. Perceived as within one's coping ability; can motivate, sharpen focus, and improve performance.
DistressNegative stress — causes anxiety and concern, can be short- or long-term, and decreases performance. Perceived as outside one's coping ability.

The stress response is meant to be helpful in the short term — its job is to get the body through the stressor and back to homeostasis. Problems develop when the stress response is activated repeatedly or does not let up: chronic stress lasting months or years wears down the effectiveness of the body's compensatory mechanisms.

Factors Influencing Coping & Adaptation

What shapes a person's coping ability

  • Genetics — some inherent difference in how efficiently a person relaxes and responds to stress.
  • Culture and environment — different values, viewpoints, and surroundings shape how stress is handled; a child raised by a parent who panics under stress can learn to panic too.
  • Prior experience — a bad past experience (e.g., at the dentist) creates anticipatory stress at future encounters.
  • Pre-existing health status — an existing acute or chronic disease adds extra allostatic demand, pushing a person closer to the limit of what they can manage.
  • Ability to manage stress — support systems, coping mechanisms, and logical thinking that help a person work through stress.

Coping mechanisms are strategies directed at the stressor that help a person withstand it; a coping strategy is effective if it resolves the situation and the feelings around it. Healthy examples: meditation, exercise, social support, talking to family or friends. Unhealthy examples: drug or alcohol use. Managing stress ultimately comes down to avoiding the stressor, changing one's reaction to it, or using healthy coping skills.

Alarm Stage — Fight-or-Flight & the Two Activation Pathways

General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) is the body's similar physiologic response to a stressor regardless of what the stressor actually is (physical, emotional, or psychological) and unfolds in three stages: alarm, resistance, and exhaustion. Ideally, the stressor resolves during resistance, before the body reaches exhaustion — but that isn't always the case.

The alarm stage is the initial burst of arousal and energy that helps the body evade or confront a stressor — the "fight-or-flight" response. The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which activates two pathways simultaneously:

PathwaySequence
Sympathetic (adrenergic) pathwayCRH activates the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) → SNS stimulates the adrenal medulla → adrenal medulla releases catecholamines (norepinephrine and epinephrine) — the "adrenergic response."
HPA axisCRH stimulates the anterior pituitary → anterior pituitary releases adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) → ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex → adrenal cortex releases glucocorticoids (cortisol).

SNS vs. PNS — Fight-or-Flight vs. Rest-and-Digest Effects

Sympathetic (SNS) — "Fight or Flight," Adrenergic

  • Pupils dilate (better see the threat)
  • Salivation stops (dry mouth)
  • Heart rate increases
  • Bronchioles dilate (more oxygenation to muscles)
  • Peristalsis and gastric secretions decrease (digestion isn't needed right now)
  • Liver breaks down glycogen for glucose (glycogenolysis) — muscles need fuel
  • Bladder contraction is inhibited

Parasympathetic (PNS) — "Rest and Digest," Cholinergic

  • Saliva production stimulated
  • Bronchioles constrict
  • Peristalsis picks back up
  • Bile is released
  • Bladder contracts

Catecholamines — Norepinephrine & Epinephrine

HormoneKey Actions
NorepinephrinePrimary constrictor of smooth muscle in blood vessels — regulates blood flow distribution to organs and maintains blood pressure via vasoconstriction. Dilates pupils (improves night and far-distance vision). Decreases gastric secretion and inhibits insulin secretion.
EpinephrineEnhances heart contractility, increases heart rate and venous return (raising cardiac output). Relaxes bronchial smooth muscle to dilate airways. Increases glycogenolysis (glucose release from the liver) and inhibits insulin secretion from the pancreas.

Together, norepinephrine and epinephrine also influence the immune system through cytokine production.

HPA Axis — Cortisol & Aldosterone

The HPA axis (hypothalamus → anterior pituitary → adrenal cortex) works on a negative feedback loop that normally keeps cortisol within a normal range. The adrenal cortex releases both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids in response to ACTH.

HormoneKey Actions
Cortisol (primary glucocorticoid; the body's endogenous steroid)Works with catecholamines to maintain normal blood pressure and cardiac output. Inhibits reproductive hormones (male and female) — in times of stress, the body shunts energy away from reproduction. Breaks down muscle, lymphoid, and adipose protein, raising blood amino acid levels; stimulates gluconeogenesis (amino acids → glucose) in the liver for fuel. Suppresses the immune response — increased infection/inflammation risk, lymph tissue atrophy, and a limited white blood cell response.
Aldosterone (primary mineralocorticoid)Acts in the kidney to reabsorb sodium and excrete potassium and hydrogen ions; water follows sodium, so aldosterone promotes fluid retention, raising extracellular volume and blood pressure.

Aldosterone's role in the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and its blocking drugs — covered in Hypertension & Hyperlipidemia

Glucocorticoids and catecholamines also work together in the brain to help form memories of a stressor — useful because it teaches the brain how to respond to that stressor in the future, but this same mechanism can create a traumatic memory from a severe or prolonged stressor (e.g., a long ICU stay), contributing to conditions like post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

Resistance Stage

In the resistance stage, stress is ongoing and the body keeps trying to cope and adjust — but this response takes a lot of energy and is not an unlimited supply. One of two things happens:

Stressor resolves

  • The parasympathetic nervous system takes over — a cholinergic response replaces the adrenergic one.
  • Heart rate and blood pressure lower; immune function, sleep, and digestion improve.
  • Hormone levels still take time to drop back to normal — a person may feel jittery or anxious for a short while afterward (e.g., after narrowly avoiding a car accident).

Stressor continues

  • The body starts running out of supplies — blood sugar runs low, and norepinephrine, epinephrine, and cortisol stores are depleted.
  • The body can no longer sustain the same response and moves toward exhaustion.

Exhaustion Stage & Allostatic Overload

In the exhaustion stage, the body is no longer able to return to homeostasis. Constant HPA-axis stimulation can cause the adrenal cortex to hypertrophy, while the underused lymph tissue atrophies — raising infection risk. There are no energy stores left, and the body loses its ability to adapt; this is the point at which disease states, and even emotional breakdown, can take over.

Chronic stress (living in a war zone, ongoing racism or discrimination, poverty, or being a caregiver for a sick family member) can drive the body to this exhaustion point. If stress continues unrelieved for long enough, the adrenal cortex eventually loses the ability to produce cortisol and the adrenal medulla stops producing catecholamines — the body cannot reach homeostasis, energy stores are gone, and at this extreme point, exhaustion can lead to bodily collapse and death.

Note: it was once believed that stress directly causes bleeding ulcers. That's not accurate — stress can worsen an existing ulcer, but it does not cause bleeding ulcers to develop in the first place.

Allostatic Load vs. Allostatic Overload

  • Allostatic load — the normal wear-and-tear demand that daily life and stress place on the HPA axis, sympathetic nervous system, and immune system as they work to maintain the body and adapt to stressors.
  • Allostatic overload — when those systems are chronically over- or under-activated beyond their capacity, reaching a point of exhaustion that damages organs and tissues and allows stress-induced disease to develop.

Chronic Stress — Signs, Symptoms & Disease Associations

Physical signs & symptoms of allostatic overload

  • Hair loss; tension and stiff neck; tension headaches
  • Asthma triggers; palpitations; tics (involuntary movements)
  • Digestive complaints — upset stomach, nausea, diarrhea, constipation, loss of appetite
  • Irritable bladder; acne; irregular menstrual cycles or impotence
  • Sleep disturbances (either insomnia or excessive sleep)

Sleep deprivation — the inability to get quality sleep — itself increases stress levels, and it is common in hospitalized patients (a noisy, interrupted, privacy-poor environment). It can cause irritability, apathy, difficulty with memory, poor judgment, and even hallucinations or mental status changes.

Chronic overstress is strongly associated with higher rates of coronary artery disease, hypertension, stroke, tension headaches, rheumatoid arthritis, other autoimmune disease, irritable bowel syndrome, ulcers, sexual disorders, and type 2 diabetes. Overproduction of stress hormones can also impair memory and contribute to major depressive disorder, and the immunosuppression from excess glucocorticoids raises the risk of infection, autoimmune disorders, and prolonged/poor wound healing. Reactivation of dormant viruses under stress is also common — shingles, cold sores, and colds classically flare up during periods of high stress.

Cortisol's Metabolic Effects — Glucose, Protein & Fat Redistribution

EffectWhat Happens
Glucose metabolismCortisol raises blood glucose — the opposite of insulin. It promotes gluconeogenesis, decreases peripheral glucose utilization, inhibits glucose uptake by muscle and adipose tissue, and promotes glucose storage as glycogen. Prolonged excess cortisol causes hyperglycemia.
Protein metabolismBreaks down muscle tissue (muscle wasting), breaks down skin integrity (skin tears more easily), and breaks down bone tissue (fragile bones/osteoporosis).
Fat redistributionBreaks down fat from the extremities and redistributes it to the face and trunk. With chronic cortisol exposure, this produces a characteristic "moon face" and "buffalo hump."

Steroid Pharmacology — Indications & Routes

We can also give steroids as medications — systemic corticosteroids given orally or IV, or inhaled steroids for a more localized effect with little to no systemic action.

PurposeExample Use
ReplacementThe adrenal gland isn't producing enough on its own — either from primary adrenal dysfunction or because it can no longer keep up after prolonged stress.
Anti-inflammatoryVery effective at decreasing inflammation — used for systemic inflammatory responses, allergies, asthma (typically inhaled, for a localized lung effect), COPD exacerbations (often IV, transitioned to oral), chronic inflammatory bowel disease, and rheumatic disorders.
ImmunosuppressionSmaller doses given to intentionally reduce immune response — e.g., after organ transplant to help prevent rejection. Patients are typically weaned off if no rejection issues develop.

Steroid Replacement Dosing Strategies

Steroid replacement is dosed to mimic the body's normal, physiologic pattern of cortisol release, which peaks in a burst around dawn with several smaller bumps through the day. Common approaches include alternate-day therapy, multiple small daily doses, or splitting a daily dose into two-thirds in the morning and one-third in the evening to mimic the natural dawn cortisol surge. The exact method depends on the patient and how their body responds to therapy. Mineralocorticoid replacement, when used, is typically dosed once per day.

The body's natural early-morning cortisol/growth hormone surge and its effect on blood glucose (dawn phenomenon) — covered in Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome

Sick-day dosing: a patient on long-term steroid replacement needs an increased dose during illness, surgery, or any major physiologic stress — their own adrenal glands cannot ramp up cortisol production the way a healthy body would. Failing to increase the dose during these times can precipitate an adrenal crisis.

Glucocorticoid Drugs — Hydrocortisone, Prednisone & Dexamethasone

DrugRouteKey Points
HydrocortisonePO or IV (parenteral)Biochemically identical to endogenous cortisol — a blood test cannot distinguish a patient's own cortisol from the hydrocortisone given as a drug. Has some mineralocorticoid (aldosterone-like) activity in addition to its glucocorticoid effect. Low doses cause minimal to no side effects; high doses can cause toxic effects.
PrednisonePOTypically dosed around 5–10 mg/day for chronic disease states (e.g., long-term low-dose use in COPD).
DexamethasonePO or IVUsed to assess adrenal function — unlike hydrocortisone, dexamethasone does not cross-react with the cortisol lab assay. Any cortisol measured in a patient taking dexamethasone reflects only their own (endogenous) adrenal production, making it possible to test the adrenal gland while the patient is on therapy.
Rule of thumb: give hydrocortisone to replace cortisol (it looks identical to the body's own on a lab test); give dexamethasone to test the adrenal gland (it's invisible to the cortisol assay, so any result reflects only the patient's own production).

Corticosteroid Side Effects — Acute & Long-Term

Acute Effects

  • Increased intraocular pressure — a particular concern in patients with glaucoma
  • Fluid retention and hypertension — a concern in patients with heart failure or other fluid-sensitive conditions
  • Mood swings
  • Weight gain with central fat deposits (abdomen, face, back of the neck)
  • Increased hunger (from cortisol's catabolic state)

Long-Term Effects (months to years of use)

  • Cataracts (clouded lens)
  • Hyperglycemia — can worsen or unmask diabetes
  • Increased infection risk (immunosuppression)
  • Osteoporosis and fractures
  • Suppressed adrenal gland hormone production — causes fatigue, appetite loss, nausea, and muscle weakness
  • Thin, papery skin with easy bruising and delayed wound healing

Corticosteroid Patient Education

Never stop long-term corticosteroids abruptly. This is why patients are placed on a taper — a gradually decreasing dose over time. Long-term steroid use suppresses the body's own adrenal hormone production, and abruptly withdrawing the drug can cause serious, potentially deadly consequences (adrenal crisis).

Additional teaching points for long-term steroid therapy

  • Take the medication at the same time each day — consistency helps mimic the body's natural hormonal pattern.
  • Wear a medical alert bracelet.
  • Monitor weight and blood glucose regularly, since both can increase with extended steroid use.
  • Report illness or major stress promptly so the dose can be adjusted (see sick-day dosing above).

Mineralocorticoid Replacement — Fludrocortisone

Mineralocorticoids aren't often replaced on their own, because hydrocortisone already carries some aldosterone-like activity. But prednisone and dexamethasone do not have that aldosterone effect — so patients on those drugs can develop salt wasting: the body is unable to maintain sodium levels, and potassium rises despite kidneys that are working normally.

Fludrocortisone is the only mineralocorticoid replacement available — it's the aldosterone substitute, used to augment glucocorticoid replacement (with prednisone or dexamethasone) when salt wasting develops.

What's the difference between homeostasis and allostasis?
Homeostasis is the stable, balanced set point itself ("homeo" = same + "stasis" = standing still). Allostasis is the active process the body uses to get back to — or adjust — that set point in response to a stressor.
Distinguish eustress from distress.
Eustress is positive stress perceived as within your coping ability — it can motivate and improve performance. Distress is negative stress perceived as beyond your coping ability — it causes anxiety/concern and decreases performance.
Name the three stages of General Adaptation Syndrome, in order.
Alarm, resistance, exhaustion.
In the alarm stage, what two pathways does the hypothalamus's CRH trigger?
(1) The sympathetic/adrenergic pathway: CRH activates the SNS, which stimulates the adrenal medulla to release catecholamines. (2) The HPA axis: CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release ACTH, which stimulates the adrenal cortex to release cortisol.
How do epinephrine and norepinephrine differ in their primary actions?
Norepinephrine mainly constricts blood vessel smooth muscle to maintain blood pressure. Epinephrine mainly enhances heart contractility/rate/cardiac output and relaxes bronchial smooth muscle to dilate airways. Both inhibit insulin secretion and raise glucose availability.
What happens to the adrenal cortex and to lymph tissue during the exhaustion stage?
The overstimulated adrenal cortex hypertrophies; the underused lymph tissue atrophies — raising infection risk.
What's the difference between allostatic load and allostatic overload?
Allostatic load is the normal wear-and-tear demand of daily stress on the HPA axis, SNS, and immune system. Allostatic overload is when those systems are chronically over- or under-activated beyond capacity, reaching exhaustion and causing stress-induced disease.
How does chronic excess cortisol redistribute body fat, and what two signs does this produce?
It breaks down fat from the extremities and redistributes it to the face and trunk, producing a "moon face" and a "buffalo hump."
Why can't you distinguish a patient's own cortisol from hydrocortisone on a blood test, but you can with dexamethasone?
Hydrocortisone is biochemically identical to endogenous cortisol, so the assay can't tell them apart. Dexamethasone doesn't cross-react with the cortisol assay, so any cortisol measured reflects only the patient's own adrenal production — which is why it's used to test adrenal function.
Why must long-term corticosteroids never be stopped abruptly?
Long-term use suppresses the patient's own adrenal gland; stopping abruptly leaves the body without enough glucocorticoid, which can cause a life-threatening adrenal crisis — steroids must be tapered.
Why does a patient on long-term steroid replacement need an increased dose during illness or major stress?
Their own adrenal glands can't ramp up cortisol production the way a healthy body would, so the exogenous dose must be increased to cover the extra demand — "sick-day dosing."
What is fludrocortisone used for?
It's the only mineralocorticoid (aldosterone) replacement — given to correct salt wasting (inability to retain sodium, with hyperkalemia despite normal kidney function) that can occur in patients on prednisone or dexamethasone, which lack aldosterone activity.
