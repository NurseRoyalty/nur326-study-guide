Antimicrobials & Antibiotics (ABX)

Antimicrobial Basics

TermMeaning
BactericidalKills the bacteria outright.
BacteriostaticOnly inhibits bacterial growth/reproduction — doesn't kill it.
Broad SpectrumEffective against many organism types, gram-positive and gram-negative — a "shotgun approach." Used when the organism isn't known yet or hasn't cultured out.
Narrow SpectrumEffective against only a few species — a "BB gun approach." Requires knowing the organism and what it's sensitive to.
Selective ToxicityToxic to the target bacterial cell while sparing the patient's own normal cells nearby.
Innate ResistanceThe organism was never susceptible to begin with — it's always been that way.
Acquired ResistanceThe organism mutated, or picked up resistance genes from another organism, after antimicrobial exposure.
SuperinfectionA new infection that develops during treatment for a different one — e.g., candidiasis or C. diff while on antibiotics for something else.

Five Ways an Antimicrobial Can Work

  • Inhibit cell wall synthesis.
  • Increase cell wall permeability.
  • Inhibit protein synthesis (stops the bacteria from functioning, without necessarily killing it).
  • Inhibit DNA/RNA synthesis, or alter DNA function.
  • Disrupt a specific metabolic or biochemical reaction (e.g., folic acid synthesis).

Prophylactic antibiotics are sometimes given to prevent infection in higher-risk groups: certain surgeries (knee replacement, cardiac, abdominal), patients with endocarditis or a history of it, and immunocompromised patients — especially those who are neutropenic (a low neutrophil count), such as patients with HIV or on chemotherapy.

Choosing & Starting an Antimicrobial

Get Cultures First — Whenever Possible

  • The goal is always to get cultures and a sensitivity result before starting an antimicrobial. Giving the drug first can prevent the organism from growing on culture, making it harder to identify.
  • A culture order is a stat priority — go get it, even if that means delegating it to another nurse.
  • Exceptions: a critically ill or rapidly declining patient, meningitis (start something that crosses the blood-brain barrier), or severe sepsis — these can't wait on culture results.
  • Blood cultures: draw two sets, one aerobic bottle and one anaerobic bottle. Always try for a peripheral stick rather than a central line or port — surface bacteria on those devices commonly contaminate the sample. Clean the skin and the bottle tops well.
FactorWhy It Matters
Community- vs. Hospital-AcquiredCommunity-acquired infections are typically easier to treat and more often viral. Hospital-acquired infections are more often bacterial and more often resistant.
Susceptibility PatternWhat the organism is actually sensitive to, per culture results.
Site of InfectionCan the drug penetrate that specific site — bone, lung, kidney, CNS? Some drugs concentrate in specific tissues better than others.
Suspected OrganismMatching the drug to the likely organism is the number one way to limit resistance.

Reading a Culture

  • Disk diffusion test: antibiotic disks are placed on a plate smeared with the culture. A clear zone (no bacterial growth) around a disk means that drug is effective against that organism.
  • Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC): the lowest concentration of a drug that stops bacterial growth — doesn't necessarily kill it.
  • Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC): the lowest concentration that decreases the bacterial colony by 99.9%. Not shown directly on a culture report — it's reported simply as susceptible or resistant.

Patient Factors & Dosing

Allergy vs. Adverse Effect

  • Nausea and vomiting are typically an adverse effect, not a true allergy.
  • A rash or hives can indicate a true allergy, but doesn't necessarily mean anaphylaxis.
  • Angioedema or anaphylaxis is a true, life-threatening allergy.
  • Cross-reactivity: a patient allergic to penicillin may also react to cephalosporins — more likely if the penicillin reaction was anaphylactic rather than just a rash. Sulfa allergy is its own, separate common category.
  • Allergies can develop at any age, including new penicillin or latex allergies later in life.
FactorEffect on Dosing
Age ExtremesVery young and very old patients typically need lower concentrations — their bodies are less able to handle standard doses.
Renal FunctionImpaired kidneys reduce clearance of nephrotoxic drugs — may not tolerate standard dosing.
Hepatic FunctionImpaired liver reduces clearance of hepatotoxic drugs.
Site of InfectionAn abscess — isolated and walled off — takes longer to treat than a UTI, which a drug can reach more easily.
Host DefensesDose and length of treatment also depend on how well the patient's own immune system is fighting the infection.
PEAK after, TROUGH before: draw a peak level about 30 minutes after an IV infusion finishes (peaks are only drawn for IV drugs). Draw a trough level about 30 minutes before the next dose is due.

Antimicrobial Resistance & Stewardship

Why Hospital Patients Are At Higher Risk

  • Decreased immune response from underlying illness.
  • Invasive devices break the normal skin barrier: IV lines (central line–associated bloodstream infection), catheters (catheter-associated UTI), G-tubes (abdominal infection).
  • Post-op risk: atelectasis (collapsed alveoli) increases pneumonia risk; wound dehiscence or evisceration carries a high infection risk. Catheters are also common post-operatively, raising UTI risk.
OrganismWhat It Means
MRSAMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus — resistant to methicillin specifically.
VREVancomycin-resistant Enterococcus. Can contaminate surfaces like chairs and clothing, spreading to other patients or family members.
CRECarbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae — a whole bacterial class resistant to all carbapenems. See the danger callout under Carbapenems below.
MDROMultidrug-resistant organism — resistant to multiple drugs across multiple classes.

C. diff spores can survive 5+ months (reports of years) on surfaces. And on antibiotic overprescribing: for an uncomplicated upper respiratory infection, an antibiotic helps only about 1 in 4,000 times — but causes a reaction (rash, allergy, severe diarrhea) about 1 in 1,000 times.

Why Antibiotics Fail

  • Wrong drug chosen for the organism.
  • Started too late.
  • Dose too low, or not continued long enough — the infection can appear to improve, then return worse.
  • The drug can't physically reach the infection site (e.g., doesn't cross the blood-brain barrier for a CNS infection).

Cell Wall Synthesis Inhibitors

All of the drugs in this section work by damaging the bacterial cell wall — weakening it so fluid rushes in, the cell swells, and it bursts (cell lysis). Many are beta-lactam drugs, named for a specific ring structure; bacteria fight back by producing beta-lactamase, an enzyme that breaks that ring and inactivates the drug.

"-bactam" or "clavulanic acid" in a drug name means a beta-lactamase inhibitor is riding along with the actual antibiotic — sulbactam, tazobactam, clavulanic acid, and avibactam are never given alone. Examples: amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin), ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam (Zosyn).

Cross-reactivity with a penicillin allergy is rare, about 1–4% — but still avoided when the penicillin reaction was anaphylactic. Cephalosporins are pregnancy category B (considered safe). Common adverse effects across all generations: mild diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and rash (the most common).

DrugKey Points
Imipenem-CilastatinBroadest-spectrum antibiotic class. Cilastatin blocks a kidney enzyme (dehydropeptidase) that would otherwise break imipenem down too fast; crosses the blood-brain barrier.
MeropenemSlightly less coverage than imipenem, but doesn't need cilastatin — used more often when possible for its lower seizure risk. Can cause GI upset.

Ertapenem and doripenem also exist but aren't emphasized here. All carbapenems are IV only, infused over 60 minutes, and saved as a last resort to limit resistance.

Carbapenems & CRE: carbapenems carry a real risk of drug-induced seizures — watch closely in patients with a seizure history. When bacteria become carbapenem-resistant (CRE), it's a public health emergency: carbapenems are the broadest-spectrum antibiotics available, so CRE leaves very few remaining options, and it's fatal in roughly half of the patients who develop it.

Vancomycin

  • A glycopeptide antibiotic — kills the gram-positive cell wall in a different way than the beta-lactams (not autolysis). Good for MRSA and other drug-resistant gram-positive infections.
  • Oral vancomycin only works locally in the gut — used for C. diff/pseudomembranous colitis, not systemic infections.
  • Toxic effects: nephrotoxic (monitor renal levels on IV vancomycin), can cause reversible hearing loss and thrombocytopenia (monitor platelets). Extra caution alongside other nephrotoxic drugs (e.g., IV contrast) or neuromuscular blockers.
  • Red Man syndrome: flushing, itching, rash — sometimes tachycardia/hypotension — from infusing it too fast. Fix by slowing the infusion, not stopping the drug; can premedicate with Benadryl.
  • Peak/trough monitoring: trough about 30 minutes before the next dose; peak about 30 minutes after the infusion finishes.

Protein & DNA/RNA Synthesis Inhibitors

These drugs don't touch the cell wall — instead they interfere with transcription or translation, so the bacteria can't build proteins or copy its DNA. That either kills the cell or stops it from reproducing.

Class name patterns: "-mycin/-micin" = aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin). "-floxacin" = fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin). "-cycline" = tetracyclines (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline).

Aminoglycosides — Gentamicin, Tobramycin

  • Potent, especially against gram-negative organisms; used for complicated or resistant infections — UTIs, pyelonephritis, endocarditis, pneumonia, osteomyelitis.
  • Two major toxicities to monitor: nephrotoxicity (usually reversible — monitor creatinine) and ototoxicity (can be permanent — ask about ringing or buzzing in the ears).
  • Peak and trough levels are drawn; dosing is adjusted for renal function.
  • Often combined with a beta-lactam or vancomycin for a synergistic effect — give the beta-lactamase inhibitor first so the aminoglycoside can get into the bacterial cell.
  • Gentamicin plus a neuromuscular blocker (paralytic) can cause profound respiratory distress. Also watch for CNS effects (confusion, disorientation, numbness/tingling) and permanent cochlear damage.

Lincosamides — Clindamycin

  • Bactericidal or bacteriostatic depending on concentration; binds ribosomes to stop protein synthesis.
  • Used for anaerobic infections — bone, GU, intra-abdominal, aspiration pneumonia. All Enterobacteriaceae are resistant to it.
  • PO or IV; monitored with peak/trough levels; same neuromuscular-blocker/respiratory-distress caution as gentamicin.
  • Notable risk: C. diff → pseudomembranous colitis. Watch for diarrhea, bloody stool, or severe GI distress.

Macrolides are bacteriostatic in general (bactericidal at high concentrations) and inhibit protein synthesis via ribosomes, like the aminoglycosides. Used for STIs (especially gonorrhea), respiratory and skin infections, and some unusual organisms other antibiotics struggle with — Legionella, Listeria, Mycoplasma pneumoniae.

All tetracyclines are bacteriostatic, inhibit protein synthesis by binding ribosomes, and are broad spectrum despite growing resistance. They still work on some unusual organisms: Rickettsia (Rocky Mountain spotted fever), chlamydia, trichomoniasis, Lyme disease, cholera. Class-wide side effects: photosensitivity (sunburn risk), diarrhea, yeast infections, and occasionally thrombocytopenia.

Tetracyclines & pregnancy/pediatrics: never give to pregnant or nursing women, or to children under 8. They cause enamel hypoplasia — permanent discoloration of developing teeth.
DrugKey Points
Ciprofloxacin (Cipro)Used for complicated UTIs, some STIs, respiratory infections, gonorrhea, and anthrax. Can be given PO, IV, or topically; poor CNS penetration — avoid for meningitis.
Levofloxacin (Levaquin)Most widely used fluoroquinolone; once daily, 100% oral bioavailability. Good for sinusitis, chronic bronchitis flares, community-acquired pneumonia. Can prolong the QT interval and lower the seizure threshold; watch renal function.

Fluoroquinolones work by altering bacterial DNA (via the enzymes gyrase and topoisomerase) rather than inhibiting protein synthesis — effective against both rapid- and slow-growing organisms, with a prolonged post-antibiotic effect. They concentrate in bone, skin, and joints, which can cause arthropathy (potentially irreversible joint damage) — this class is generally avoided in patients under 18 or over 60 for that reason. Also causes photosensitivity.

Sulfonamides — Sulfamethoxazole-Trimethoprim (Bactrim)

  • Bacteriostatic; blocks folic acid synthesis.
  • First-line for uncomplicated UTIs and respiratory infections; also used for salmonella and shigellosis.
  • Often given to patients with HIV, who have a higher rate of adverse reactions.
  • Contains a sulfa component — contraindicated with a sulfa allergy. Reactions typically start with fever, then a skin rash.

Metronidazole (Flagyl)

  • Works against both bacterial and protozoal infections: amebiasis, giardiasis, trichomoniasis, intra-abdominal infections, H. pylori, Crohn's-related infections.
  • The go-to drug (with oral vancomycin) for C. diff/antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Only works against anaerobic bacteria.
  • Works by inhibiting DNA synthesis, similar to the fluoroquinolones.
  • Other adverse effects seen across most antibiotics: nausea/vomiting, xerostomia (dry mouth), vaginal candidiasis (yeast infection).
Metronidazole (Flagyl) & alcohol: no alcohol — none for 24 hours before starting and at least 36 hours after the last dose. Mixing the two creates a toxic metabolite (a disulfiram-like reaction).

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the difference between bactericidal and bacteriostatic?
Bactericidal kills the bacteria; bacteriostatic only stops it from growing or reproducing.
A patient had an anaphylactic reaction to penicillin — is a cephalosporin safe to give?
Not without caution — there's a 1–4% cross-reactivity risk, and cephalosporins are avoided when the penicillin reaction was anaphylactic.
When do you draw a vancomycin trough level?
About 30 minutes before the next scheduled dose.
When do you draw a peak level for an IV antibiotic?
About 30 minutes after the infusion finishes.
A patient develops flushing, itching, and a rash during a vancomycin infusion — what do you do?
Slow the infusion rate. This is Red Man syndrome, not a true allergy, and it resolves with a slower rate (can premedicate with Benadryl).
Why can't tetracyclines be given to a pregnant patient or a child under 8?
They cause enamel hypoplasia — permanent discoloration of developing teeth.
What can't a patient have while taking metronidazole (Flagyl)?
Alcohol — none for 24 hours before and 36 hours after, or it causes a toxic reaction.
What are the two major toxicities to monitor with aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin)?
Nephrotoxicity (usually reversible) and ototoxicity (can be permanent).
What does CRE stand for, and why is it dangerous?
Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae — carbapenems are the broadest-spectrum antibiotics available, so CRE leaves very few treatment options and is fatal in roughly half of patients who develop it.
Why give a beta-lactamase inhibitor (like tazobactam or clavulanic acid) alongside a penicillin?
It's never given alone — it protects the penicillin from being broken down by bacteria that secrete beta-lactamase.
A patient with C. diff needs an antibiotic — which two drugs are the go-to?
Oral vancomycin and metronidazole (Flagyl).
Why get cultures before starting an antibiotic whenever possible?
Giving the antibiotic first can prevent the organism from growing on the culture, making it harder to identify what's actually causing the infection.
