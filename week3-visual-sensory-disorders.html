Week 3 · Topic 5

Visual & Sensory Disorders

This page covers six conditions in the order the lecture teaches them: cataracts, retinopathy (diabetic and hypertensive), retinal detachment, macular degeneration, glaucoma (open-angle and closed-angle, plus its pharmacology), and Meniere's disease.

Cataracts

TermDetail
What It IsClouding of the lens, strongly associated with aging.
PresentationPainless, gradual blurry vision that worsens slowly over time.
TreatmentSurgical only — there is no medication that reverses lens clouding. Surgery removes the clouded lens and replaces it with an artificial one.

Retinopathy

Diabetic Retinopathy

  • Non-proliferative — the earlier stage; small vessel changes (microaneurysms, leaking) in the retina.
  • Proliferative — the more advanced stage; the retina responds to poor blood flow by growing new, fragile blood vessels that bleed easily and threaten vision more seriously.

Hypertensive Retinopathy

  • Damage to retinal blood vessels from chronic high blood pressure.
  • Reversible with good blood pressure control, unlike most of the other conditions on this page.
Trick: retinopathy is the one category on this page that's actually reversible if you fix the root cause — get blood pressure and blood glucose under control and the vessel damage can improve, unlike cataracts, retinal detachment, or macular degeneration.

Retinal Detachment

TermDetail
PresentationSudden, painless, and unilateral (one eye). Classic symptoms: floaters, flashing lights, and a curtain or shadow moving across the visual field.
Risk FactorsMyopia (nearsightedness), older age, and trauma to the eye.
Retinal detachment is an emergency: the "curtain" description should immediately raise suspicion — delayed treatment risks permanent vision loss.

Macular Degeneration

Dry (Non-Neovascular)Wet (Neovascular)
How CommonAbout 90% of cases — more common formAbout 10% of cases
MechanismGradual thinning/breakdown of the macula over timeAbnormal new blood vessels grow under the macula and leak

Risk Factors

  • UV light exposure over a lifetime.
  • Hyperopia (farsightedness).
  • Smoking.

Macular degeneration is irreversible — management focuses on slowing progression and preserving remaining central vision, not restoring what's lost.

Glaucoma

Open-Angle GlaucomaClosed-Angle Glaucoma
OnsetGradual, progressiveSudden — a true emergency
MechanismDysfunction of the trabecular meshwork (the eye's normal drainage pathway) — fluid drains too slowly, pressure builds up slowly over timeThe iris blocks the drainage angle outright, causing a rapid, dangerous spike in eye pressure
TriggerNo single trigger — a chronic processCan be precipitated by anticholinergic drugs, which dilate the pupil and can push the iris into the drainage angle
Trick: "open" is the slow leak, "closed" is the sudden clog. Open-angle sneaks up gradually because the drain is just sluggish; closed-angle is an emergency because the drain gets physically blocked all at once.
Acute closed-angle glaucoma is an emergency: the sudden pressure spike can cause permanent vision loss within hours — it needs immediate treatment to lower intraocular pressure, not a routine eye-clinic follow-up.

Glaucoma Pharmacology

Class / DrugHow It Works
Beta Blockers (timolol, betaxolol)Reduce the amount of aqueous humor (the eye's internal fluid) produced. The instructor's own memorable phrase for this class: "chill out drugs" — they calm down fluid production the way beta blockers calm down the heart elsewhere in the body.
Prostaglandin Analogs (latanoprost)Increase outflow of aqueous humor through an alternate drainage pathway.
Alpha-2 Agonists (brimonidine)Both decrease aqueous humor production and increase its outflow.
Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors (dorzolamide)Decrease aqueous humor production by inhibiting the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in the eye.

Nasolacrimal Occlusion — Administration Technique

  • After instilling eye drops, press gently on the inner corner of the eye (over the tear duct) for about a minute.
  • This prevents the drop from draining into the nasolacrimal duct and being absorbed systemically — reducing systemic side effects (this matters especially for the beta blocker eye drops, since systemic beta-blockade is possible if too much is absorbed that way).

Meniere's Disease

TermDetail
MechanismEndolymphatic hydrops — an abnormal buildup of endolymph (inner ear fluid) that disrupts normal inner ear function.
PresentationEpisodic attacks of vertigo, hearing loss, tinnitus, and a feeling of fullness/pressure in the affected ear.
ManagementPrimarily dietary/lifestyle — trigger management (commonly reducing sodium and caffeine). There is no direct pharmacologic cure; treatment focuses on managing symptoms and triggers during episodes.

Quick Comparison — Sudden vs. Gradual, Painful vs. Painless

A study trick for this whole topic: sort every condition by how fast it comes on and whether it hurts. The emergencies are the sudden, painless ones — that combination is easy to underestimate because there's no pain to prompt urgency.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What is a cataract, and how is it treated?
Clouding of the lens causing painless, gradual blurry vision. Treated surgically only — no medication reverses it.
What's the difference between non-proliferative and proliferative diabetic retinopathy?
Non-proliferative is the earlier stage (small vessel changes/leaking). Proliferative is more advanced — fragile new blood vessels grow in response to poor blood flow and bleed easily.
Why is hypertensive retinopathy different from the other conditions on this page?
It's reversible with good blood pressure control — most of the other conditions on this page are not reversible.
What are the classic symptoms of retinal detachment, and why is it an emergency?
Sudden, painless, unilateral floaters, flashing lights, and a curtain/shadow across the visual field — delayed treatment risks permanent vision loss.
What's the difference between dry and wet macular degeneration?
Dry (about 90%) is gradual thinning/breakdown of the macula. Wet (about 10%) involves abnormal new blood vessels growing under the macula and leaking.
What are macular degeneration's risk factors?
UV light exposure, hyperopia (farsightedness), and smoking.
What's the mechanism difference between open-angle and closed-angle glaucoma?
Open-angle: the trabecular meshwork drains fluid too slowly, so pressure builds gradually. Closed-angle: the iris blocks the drainage angle outright, causing a sudden dangerous pressure spike.
What can precipitate an acute closed-angle glaucoma attack?
Anticholinergic drugs — they dilate the pupil, which can push the iris into the drainage angle.
What does the instructor's "chill out drugs" phrase refer to, and how do they work?
Beta blockers (timolol, betaxolol) for glaucoma — they reduce aqueous humor production, calming fluid production the way beta blockers calm the heart.
Why is nasolacrimal occlusion used after instilling glaucoma eye drops?
Pressing on the inner corner of the eye for about a minute prevents the drop from draining into the nasolacrimal duct and being absorbed systemically, reducing systemic side effects.
What causes Meniere's disease, and what are its classic symptoms?
Endolymphatic hydrops (abnormal endolymph buildup in the inner ear) causing episodic vertigo, hearing loss, tinnitus, and ear fullness.
How is Meniere's disease managed?
Primarily through dietary/lifestyle trigger management (commonly reducing sodium and caffeine) — there is no direct pharmacologic cure.
