Week 7

Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new — it's a map of Week 7's twelve topics (plus the Comprehensive Study Guide, which pulls the whole week together on its own page), so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. Week 7 is really two units back to back: cancer and chemotherapy (Topics 1–6), then the musculoskeletal system (Topics 7–12). The cancer lecture states its own order out loud — cancer in general, then the specific cancers you are responsible for, then the chemotherapies. The musculoskeletal lecture doesn't announce a framework, so the map below follows only the order it was taught in and what each topic page says about its own condition. No flashcards or quiz questions here — just the map.

The Lectures' Own Order, Applied to All Twelve Topics

Twelve pages is easier to hold in your head when you see which ones belong together. Each row below is one step in the order the lectures were given.

StepTopicsThe Common Thread
Cancer in General — What It Is and How It SpreadsCancer Fundamentals; Metastasis & Cancer EtiologyThe lecture's first step. Cancer cells lack differentiation, checkpoints, apoptosis, and response to signals from other cells, so they keep proliferating; the cell growth cycle introduced here is the background for the chemotherapy pages. The second page covers how malignant cells spread (lymphatic and vascular routes, seeding, implantation) and what causes cancer in the first place (risk factors, tumor suppressor genes versus proto-oncogenes, carcinogens versus promoters).
The Specific CancersLung Cancer; Breast, Cervical & Colorectal CancerThe four cancers the lecture singles out as the ones it focuses on in Kentucky (the highest-prevalence cancer, non-melanoma skin cancer, was covered in NUR 325). The pattern is the same each time: who gets it, the risk factors, and what can be done about it through screening, vaccination, genetic testing, or prevention. Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death, largely because most patients are not diagnosed until it is advanced.
Treatment — ChemotherapyChemotherapy Principles & Toxicities; Antineoplastic Drug Classes & AntiemeticsThe general rules first (these drugs hit rapidly dividing cells, so hair follicles, the GI tract, and bone marrow take the damage along with the cancer), then the specific classes, each tied to key toxicities, and finally the two antiemetics for the nausea and vomiting nearly all of these drugs cause.
The Bone ItselfOsteoporosis; Fractures & OsteomyelitisThe first musculoskeletal topics. Osteoporosis is low bone density with structural deterioration (too much osteoclast activity, too little osteoblast activity) and ends in hip fractures; the next page picks up with fractures in general, how they heal, and what goes wrong, including osteomyelitis, the bone infection the instructor calls probably the most common complication of fractures.
The Joint — Wear and TearOsteoarthritis & Degenerative Disc DiseaseOpens with a review of the synovial joint, because most joint disorders affect synovial joints. Osteoarthritis is degeneration from aging and stress, localized to the affected joints rather than systemic; degenerative disc disease is the same process in the spine.
The Joint and the Whole Body — Autoimmune DiseaseRheumatoid Arthritis; Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (Lupus)Both are autoimmune and systemic, and both involve immune complexes. RA is taught as the contrast to OA (the body attacks the synovial tissue); lupus is the last topic of the musculoskeletal deck and is compared directly with RA: in RA it is really only the joints, while in lupus any organ can be involved.
The Metabolic One — Uric AcidGoutTaught between RA and lupus in the lecture order. Gout is the metabolic problem (a disruption in uric acid metabolism with urate crystals deposited in and around joints); gouty arthritis is the resulting joint inflammation. Its drug page has the lecture's three gout drugs plus NSAIDs.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, follow the lecture order within each unit. Read Cancer Fundamentals before the chemotherapy pages (the cell cycle matters for them), the two chemotherapy pages in order, Osteoporosis before Fractures & Osteomyelitis (which picks up where the hip-fracture material ends), Osteoarthritis before Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA is taught as the contrast to OA), and Rheumatoid Arthritis before Lupus (hydroxychloroquine and the RA-versus-lupus comparison are built on it).

The Twelve Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1Cancer FundamentalsCancer cells lack differentiation, checkpoints, apoptosis, contact inhibition, and response to signals, so they proliferate without control; know benign versus malignant (starred), the cell growth cycle (it explains chemotherapy), tumor grades 1–3, TNM, and stages I–IV.
2Metastasis & Cancer EtiologyCancer spreads by seeding, implantation, and metastasis (lymphatic or vascular, with the liver as the first stop for blood spread); causes include risk factors, tumor suppressor genes (the brake) versus proto-oncogenes (the gas pedal), and the difference between a carcinogen (alters DNA) and a promoter (helps damaged cells proliferate).
3Lung CancerThe leading cause of cancer-related death, mostly caught late (stage 1 survival over 60%, stage 4 below 10%); smoking is the leading cause, radon plus smoking is a very dangerous combination, and the only types to recognize are non-small cell (more common) and small cell.
4Breast, Cervical & Colorectal CancerEach is a population-level pattern to know: breast cancer (most common, hormone-exposure and BRCA risks), cervical cancer (almost all HPV-positive and preventable with the HPV vaccine and Pap smears), and colorectal cancer (polyps, symptoms that are nonspecific, screening from age 45).
5Chemotherapy Principles & ToxicitiesChemotherapy works best on rapidly growing tumors and has a low therapeutic index, so the rapidly dividing normal tissues (hair, GI tract, bone marrow) take the damage; know myelosuppression and the nadir, extravasation, and the practical safety issues.
6Antineoplastic Drug Classes & AntiemeticsThe instructor gives you the class, and the class tells you the toxicity: alkylating agents to hemorrhagic cystitis, antimetabolites to nephrotoxicity and category X, antitumor antibiotics to cardiotoxicity, vinca alkaloids to peripheral neuropathy and IV only; plus ondansetron and promethazine.
7OsteoporosisLow bone density with structural deterioration, measured by DEXA T-scores, caused by increased osteoclast and decreased osteoblast activity; hip fracture is the biggest complication, and the goal of drug therapy is to reduce fractures (alendronate first line).
8Fractures & OsteomyelitisA fracture is a break in the continuity of bone; know the causes, the types, PED (pain, edema, deformity), the phases of healing, and the complications, especially compartment syndrome, fat embolism, and osteomyelitis (long IV antibiotic treatment).
9Osteoarthritis & Degenerative Disc DiseaseOA is localized wear and tear of the joint (cartilage loss, bone spurs, bone on bone) with deep aching pain relieved by rest; no drug stops or reverses it, so management is pain control, mobility, and minimizing disability; degenerative disc disease is the same process in the spine, with nerve impingement.
10Rheumatoid ArthritisA systemic, autoimmune disease in which the body attacks the synovial tissue (symmetrical joints, spongy swelling, pannus, systemic involvement); unlike OA, drugs can slow it, and methotrexate (once a week, never daily) and hydroxychloroquine are the DMARDs for your drug matrix.
11GoutA disruption in uric acid metabolism (overproduction or underexcretion) that leaves urate crystals in and around joints, classically the big toe; NSAIDs are first line for an acute attack, and allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid each need a place on your drug matrix.
12Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (Lupus)An autoimmune disease in which autoantibodies (mainly ANA) form immune complexes that can land in almost any organ, most commonly the kidney; know the hallmark signs (fatigue, photosensitivity, butterfly rash), the flare triggers, and how lupus compares with RA.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

The danger callouts and safety statements from across the twelve pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule. Some pages (Metastasis & Cancer Etiology, Breast, Cervical & Colorectal Cancer) have no danger callout of their own, so their row lists the instructor's must-know points instead.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Cancer FundamentalsA benign tumor can still be lethal if it grows large enough to mechanically interrupt a critical tissue or organ (a dangerous benign tumor in the brain, or one taking over part of a lung). If you are given a T and M (TNM) classification or a stage, you must understand what it means for the patient.
Metastasis & Cancer EtiologyNo danger callout. Know the difference between a carcinogen (alters DNA, causes the mutation) and a promoter (helps already-damaged cells proliferate), and that some things, such as tobacco and estrogen therapy, are both.
Lung CancerLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death. Radon plus smoking is a very dangerous combination (a compounding effect). Smoke and toxins paralyze the cilia (stressed twice). Take a thorough health history of work and exposures. Symptoms overlap with those of chronic smokers, which is why screening and risk factors matter.
Breast, Cervical & Colorectal CancerNo danger callout. Prevention and screening points: the HPV vaccine (best around age 11 or 12, can be given up to age 45) and Pap smears prevent cervical cancer; family history of breast or ovarian cancer is a red flag for prevention and screening; colorectal screening begins at 45, or earlier with IBD, family history, or a genetic condition.
Chemotherapy Principles & ToxicitiesThe nadir is the time of highest risk for infection, anemia, and bleeding. Extravasation is a very serious complication of IV chemotherapy (central access such as a portacath). Chemotherapy may be delayed or adjusted for a very low WBC or neutrophil count, active infection, poor nutrition or hydration, or liver or kidney dysfunction. Most chemotherapy drugs are class D or X, and fertility should be discussed before treatment.
Antineoplastic Drug Classes & AntiemeticsCytotoxic agents are dangerous to handle (certification, strict protocols, many are vesicants). Alkylating agents: hemorrhagic cystitis. Antimetabolites: keep the patient hydrated, monitor liver enzymes, category X. Doxorubicin: cardiotoxicity, maximum lifetime dose, monitor EF and baseline echocardiogram. Vinca alkaloids: IV only, because intrathecal administration is fatal. CAR-T: cytokine release syndrome is life-threatening. Promethazine: black box warnings for children under 2 and gangrenous extravasation.
OsteoporosisThe goal of drug therapy is to reduce fractures, not just raise bone density. Alendronate: do not lie down afterward, do not take with calcium, take with one sip of water in the morning (esophageal ulcerations). Raloxifene: stroke and clot risk, stop at least 72 hours before a planned procedure, not for pregnancy. Hormone replacement is not recommended (clots, breast cancer, other cancers).
Fractures & OsteomyelitisCompartment syndrome (pain out of proportion, weak pulses, edema) is treated with a fasciotomy. Fat embolism can go from fine to respiratory arrest within minutes (hypoxemia, altered level of consciousness, petechial rash). Osteomyelitis is hard to treat and needs weeks of IV antibiotics; avoid it at all costs.
Osteoarthritis & Degenerative Disc DiseaseNo drug prevents progression, prevents damage, or reverses the course of arthritis, so the focus is pain management and disability. Review your NSAIDs and know their risks.
Rheumatoid ArthritisMethotrexate is given ONCE PER WEEK, never daily — death can occur if given daily. Folic acid replacement, no alcohol, monitor liver enzymes and the CBC, two forms of birth control, and a patient with signs of infection goes to the doctor ASAP. Methotrexate users have a shortened life expectancy, so weigh risks and benefits. Hydroxychloroquine: report blurry vision or any vision problem.
GoutAllopurinol: rash can mean Stevens-Johnson syndrome and TEN; agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia risk (monitor WBC and CBC); watch the INR with warfarin and blood sugar with hypoglycemic agents. Colchicine: stomach-flu-type symptoms or persistent vomiting mean toxicity, so stop immediately. Probenecid: report kidney or liver problems. Allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid all go on the drug matrix.
Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (Lupus)Kidney involvement is the big one: about half of patients develop kidney complications, which can require dialysis or a kidney transplant. Flare triggers to avoid: sunlight, infection, abruptly stopping a medicine, and stress. Multi-system organ involvement carries a very poor prognosis.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Rapidly dividing cells take the hit, so watch the blood counts." Chemotherapy damages hair follicles, the GI tract, and bone marrow along with the cancer, which is why the nadir, neutropenia, and infection risk are the central safety points on the chemotherapy pages. The same blood-count monitoring comes back with methotrexate (CBC), allopurinol (WBC and CBC), and colchicine (short-term leukopenia and bone marrow suppression).
  • "With these drugs, the safety rule is usually how or when you give them." Vinca alkaloids are IV only; promethazine is best given by IV piggyback; methotrexate is once a week, never daily; alendronate means one sip of water and staying upright; raloxifene is stopped at least 72 hours before a planned procedure. Teaching the route or the schedule is itself the safety intervention.
  • "Every high-alert drug has an early warning sign to teach." Blood in the urine with alkylating agents, peripheral neuropathy with vinca alkaloids, persistent vomiting with colchicine, a rash with allopurinol, blurry vision with hydroxychloroquine, and any sign of infection with methotrexate. The patient-teaching message is "know this sign and call."
  • "The deadly part is often the complication, not the first problem." A benign tumor becomes lethal when it grows large enough to interrupt a critical organ; deaths after an osteoporotic hip fracture come from immobility complications (sepsis, skin breakdown, pneumonia, blood clots), not the fracture itself; a fracture can bring compartment syndrome, fat embolism, or osteomyelitis; lupus becomes dangerous when the kidneys are involved.
  • "When symptoms are vague or missing, screening and risk factors do the work." Lung cancer is usually caught late because early symptoms are lacking (stage 1 survival over 60%, stage 4 below 10%); colorectal polyps are usually asymptomatic; osteoporosis has no early manifestations, so DEXA starts earlier in high-risk patients; RA's early picture is vague. The instructor's rule of thumb on the cancer pages, that you are probably okay while you can eat, drink, pee, and poop, comes with the reminder to get significant changes checked.
