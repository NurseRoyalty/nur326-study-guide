Intro to Pharmacology — Definitions, Naming & Regulation

TermDetail
PharmacologyThe study of drugs/chemicals/medications and the changes they cause in the body.
PharmacokineticsWhat the body does to the drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion).
PharmacodynamicsWhat the drug does to the body — its mechanism of action and overall effects.
Chemical / Generic / Trade NameChemical name: long, complex, used in research (e.g., acetylparaminophenol). Generic name: the one official name, lowercase, used on exams and NCLEX (e.g., acetaminophen). Trade name: the capitalized brand name from the manufacturer (e.g., Tylenol) — patent-protected until it expires, after which other companies can sell the generic.
Prototype DrugThe first drug in a class, used as the comparison point for effectiveness/side effects of newer drugs in that class (e.g., ibuprofen/Advil is the NSAID prototype).
TermDetail
Therapeutic EffectThe intended effect of the drug (e.g., lowering blood pressure).
Side EffectAn unintended effect of a normal dose.
Toxicity / Adverse EffectToxicity: a harmful effect. Adverse effect: an unexpected, dangerous reaction. (Used interchangeably with "side effect" at this course level.)
Allergic ReactionAn unexpected, potentially dangerous immune system response — ranges from itching/hives to anaphylaxis.

How New Drugs Are Approved (FDA)

  • Preclinical: tested on lab animals for therapeutic/adverse effects.
  • Phase 1: healthy human volunteers — checking for major adverse reactions.
  • Phase 2: patients with the target disease — identifying rarer/less frequent side effects; the drug may be pulled here if effects are too dangerous.
  • Phase 3: a vast clinical market — unexpected responses may still lead to withdrawal.
  • Phase 4: post-market, ongoing FDA monitoring. If a pattern of severe reactions emerges, the FDA can require a black box warning — the strictest FDA warning a drug can carry while staying on the market.
Controlled Substance ScheduleDetail
Schedule INo accepted medical use, very high abuse potential (e.g., heroin, LSD, ecstasy).
Schedule IIHigh abuse potential but some medical use; severe psychological/physical dependence risk (e.g., opioids, amphetamines). No automatic refills — each fill needs a new prescription.
Schedule IIIModerate-to-low potential for dependence (e.g., ketamine, anabolic steroids).
Schedule IVLow potential for abuse/dependence (e.g., Xanax, Valium, Ambien).
Schedule VVery low abuse potential — small amounts of narcotics/stimulants (e.g., cough syrup with a small amount of codeine).

To be sold over the counter (OTC), a drug must be safe (low risk of side effects/abuse) and the consumer must be able to diagnose the condition and monitor effectiveness themselves. Some OTC drugs (e.g., pseudoephedrine — a meth precursor) are still kept behind the pharmacy counter with purchase limits.

Dietary/Herbal Supplements, Teratogens & Pharmacogenomics

  • Dietary/herbal supplements can only claim an effect on body structure or function (not that they treat a medical condition) and aren't FDA-evaluated for safety/efficacy before marketing. They can still interact with medications — e.g., ginkgo biloba can suppress platelet aggregation, raising bleeding risk with anticoagulants/antiplatelets.
  • Teratogens (e.g., alcohol, marijuana, nicotine) cause congenital malformations. The FDA's old pregnancy risk categories (A = safest, through X = contraindicated, known fetal risk) have been replaced since 2015 by the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), which gives a narrative risk summary instead of a single letter.
  • Pharmacogenomics — studying how a person's genes affect their drug response, to tailor medication choice/dosing (already used in some cancer and psychiatric drug selection).

Pharmacokinetics — Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism & Excretion

RouteDetail
EnteralVia the GI tract — oral, sublingual, buccal, rectal.
ParenteralOutside the GI tract — typically injection (intradermal, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intrathecal, intra-articular, intravenous).
TopicalApplied to body surfaces — ointments, creams, pastes, powders, transdermal patches.

Three phases of drug activity: pharmaceutical (the drug dissolves — disintegration breaks a tablet into particles, dissolution dissolves those particles into GI fluid so they can be absorbed), pharmacokinetic (absorption → distribution → metabolism → excretion), and pharmacodynamic (what the drug does to the body). Enteric-coated tablets resist breakdown in stomach acid and dissolve later/slower, in the more alkaline intestine.

TermDetail
BioavailabilityThe percentage of an administered drug that reaches systemic circulation in active form. IV drugs: 100% (no absorption barrier). Oral/GI drugs: less than 100%, due to the first-pass effect.
First-Pass EffectAn orally absorbed drug must pass through the liver before reaching systemic circulation, and the liver metabolizes a portion of it to an inactive form — reducing bioavailability. This is why oral doses (e.g., oral morphine) are typically much higher than IV doses of the same drug.
DistributionMovement of the drug from blood to its site of action — best in highly vascularized areas (heart, liver, kidneys, brain); slower in muscle, skin, and fat, and impaired by conditions like peripheral vascular disease or a poorly perfused abscess.
Blood-Brain BarrierTight capillary junctions in the brain that block most drugs — only lipid-soluble drugs or ones with a transport system can cross (e.g., alcohol, glucose). Not fully developed in infants, so extra caution is needed with drugs given to them.
Protein BindingDrugs travel partly bound to plasma proteins (e.g., albumin) and partly free/unbound — only the unbound drug is active. Low albumin (malnutrition, liver disease, alcoholism) means more free drug and a higher risk of toxicity — e.g., warfarin is 97–99% protein bound, so low albumin can significantly increase bleeding risk.

Pharmacokinetics — Metabolism, Excretion & Half-Life

TermDetail
Metabolism (Biotransformation)Chemically alters the drug into a metabolite — from active/lipid-soluble to inactive/water-soluble, ready for excretion. The liver is the main organ responsible.
CYP450 SystemThe liver's main drug-metabolizing enzyme system. A drug is a substrate if it's metabolized by a CYP450 enzyme. A CYP450 inducer speeds up metabolism of substrate drugs (less drug in the body, lower therapeutic effect). A CYP450 inhibitor slows metabolism of substrate drugs (drug builds up, higher toxicity risk) — grapefruit juice is a classic CYP450 inhibitor, so substrate drugs should be avoided with grapefruit for 2–4 hours around dosing.
ExcretionElimination of the drug (mostly via the kidneys, through glomerular filtration, tubular secretion, and reabsorption). Kidney function directly affects how well a drug is excreted.
Half-Life (T½)The time it takes for serum drug concentration to fall by 50%. After about 5 half-lives, ~97% of the drug is eliminated. It also takes about 4–5 half-lives to reach steady state — where the amount of drug eliminated equals the amount absorbed with each dose, giving consistent blood levels and maximum therapeutic benefit.

Half-life guides dosing intervals. With a drug like morphine (half-life ~2–4 hours), levels drop meaningfully between doses — around-the-clock dosing (a scheduled baseline dose plus PRN doses for breakthrough symptoms) is used, especially for chronic pain, to keep a steady therapeutic concentration on board rather than letting levels swing widely.

Pharmacodynamics & Drug Safety

TermDetail
ReceptorA reactive site on/inside a cell (usually a protein) that a drug binds to, forming a drug-receptor complex that stimulates or inhibits a response.
AgonistBinds a receptor and initiates the desired therapeutic effect (e.g., a beta-1 adrenergic agonist causing vasodilation).
AntagonistBlocks/inhibits a natural substance from binding and acting (e.g., diphenhydramine/Benadryl blocking histamine at the H1 receptor).
Receptor-less ActivationSome drugs act through direct chemical interaction rather than a receptor (e.g., antacids neutralizing stomach acid, or magnesium sulfate acting as a laxative via an osmotic effect).
Therapeutic IndexThe ratio of a drug's toxic level to its therapeutic level. A narrow therapeutic index (e.g., warfarin, digoxin) means a small margin between helpful and toxic — needs closer monitoring. A wide/high index (e.g., amoxicillin) is rarely associated with toxicity.

Drug Interactions

  • Additive effect: two drugs with a similar mechanism of action are given together on purpose, for a stronger combined effect (e.g., two antibiotics for a complicated infection).
  • Synergism/potentiation: two drugs with different mechanisms combine for an effect greater than either alone (e.g., Coumadin plus aspirin).
  • Antidote: a drug given to antagonize another drug's toxic effects (e.g., naloxone/Narcan for opioid overdose).
  • Interactions can also decrease therapeutic effects — CYP450 inducers speeding up elimination, impaired GI absorption (e.g., after intestinal surgery), or activated charcoal intentionally blocking absorption.
  • Polypharmacy raises drug interaction risk — a special concern in older adults, who also have slower hepatic metabolism, reduced circulation/distribution, decreased GI absorption, and reduced renal excretion, raising risk for adverse reactions like confusion, falls, and hip fractures.

Medication safety practices: tall-man lettering for look-alike names (e.g., "heparin"), barcode scanning and automated dispensing (e.g., Pyxis), no trailing zeros (write "1 mg," not "1.0 mg") and always a leading zero ("0.25 mg," not ".25 mg") to prevent misreads, and identifying high-alert medications (a formal list first created in 1995) that need extra precautions in prescribing, dispensing, and administration.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the difference between pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics?
Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion). Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body (its mechanism of action and effects).
What's the difference between a drug's chemical, generic, and trade name?
Chemical name: long research name (acetylparaminophenol). Generic name: the one official, lowercase name used on exams/NCLEX (acetaminophen). Trade name: the capitalized, patent-protected brand name (Tylenol).
What's the difference between a therapeutic effect, a side effect, and a toxic/adverse effect?
Therapeutic effect: the intended effect. Side effect: an unintended effect at a normal dose. Toxic/adverse effect: a harmful or dangerous, unexpected reaction — beyond a routine side effect.
What is a black box warning, and when does the FDA require one?
The strictest warning the FDA can put on a drug while it stays on the market — required when post-market (Phase 4) monitoring reveals a pattern of severe reactions.
What's the key difference between a Schedule I and a Schedule II controlled substance?
Schedule I: no accepted medical use, very high abuse potential (heroin, LSD). Schedule II: high abuse potential but some accepted medical use (opioids, amphetamines) — and no automatic refills.
Why is an IV drug's bioavailability 100%, while an oral drug's is not?
IV goes directly into systemic circulation with no absorption barrier. Oral drugs pass through the liver first (the first-pass effect), which inactivates a portion before it ever reaches systemic circulation.
What's the difference between a CYP450 inducer and a CYP450 inhibitor?
An inducer speeds up metabolism of substrate drugs (less drug in the body, lower therapeutic effect). An inhibitor slows metabolism (drug builds up, higher toxicity risk) — grapefruit juice is a classic inhibitor.
Why does low albumin raise toxicity risk for a highly protein-bound drug like warfarin?
Only the unbound (free) drug is active. Low albumin means less protein to bind the drug, so more of it circulates free and active — raising toxicity/bleeding risk.
What's the difference between an agonist and an antagonist?
An agonist binds a receptor and triggers the desired therapeutic effect. An antagonist blocks/inhibits a natural substance from binding and acting — e.g., Benadryl blocking histamine at the H1 receptor.
What is half-life, and what does it mean for a drug to reach "steady state"?
Half-life is the time for serum drug concentration to drop by 50%. Steady state (reached after about 4-5 half-lives) is when the amount of drug eliminated equals the amount absorbed with each dose — consistent blood levels and maximum therapeutic benefit.
What's the difference between a narrow and a wide/high therapeutic index?
A narrow therapeutic index (warfarin, digoxin) means a small margin between a helpful dose and a toxic one — needs close monitoring. A wide/high index (amoxicillin) is rarely associated with toxicity.
Name two medication safety practices that prevent dosing/administration errors, and what each prevents.
Correct decimal formatting (leading zero, no trailing zero — e.g., "0.25 mg" not ".25 mg" or "0.250 mg") prevents tenfold misreads. Tall-man lettering (e.g., "hEPARIN") prevents mixing up look-alike drug names.
