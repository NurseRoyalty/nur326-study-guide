Renal Disorders

This page covers five renal conditions in the order the lecture teaches them: pyelonephritis, urinary obstruction, nephrolithiasis (kidney stones), urologic cancers, and the glomerular disorders (glomerulonephritis, nephrotic syndrome, and glomerulopathy). Acute Kidney Injury and Chronic Kidney Disease build directly on this page and get their own page next — the lecture treats them as a distinct, later topic once this foundation is in place.

Pyelonephritis (Upper UTI / Kidney Infection)

TermDetail
DefinitionInflammation of one or both kidneys, focused on the upper urinary tract. Usually ascending — a lower UTI climbs up the urinary tract — but can also come from a systemic bloodstream infection reaching the kidney.
PathophysiologyE. coli is the most common pathogen. The kidney becomes swollen and fills with infectious exudate; if severe, this can compress the renal artery. The kidney can become necrotic or form abscesses.
Acute vs. ChronicUsually acute. Untreated or recurrent pyelonephritis can become chronic — a cause of chronic kidney disease and a risk for sepsis down the road.
Risk FactorsPregnancy, recurrent lower UTIs, and an antibiotic-resistant strain (a lower UTI treated with an antibiotic it's resistant to stays untreated and can ascend).
Lower UTIPyelonephritis (Upper UTI)
OnsetGradual — "do I have a UTI, do I not."Sudden — wakes up or develops within an afternoon.
How They LookOtherwise well.Look and feel systemically sick — this is a systemic infection.
Key FindingsDysuria, frequency, urgency.Fever, chills, CVA (costovertebral angle) tenderness over the back — plus the same frequency/urgency/dysuria, since the lower tract is usually still involved. Hematuria and nausea/vomiting/anorexia can occur with either.

Treatment

  • Community-acquired, low resistance risk: Bactrim (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) first-line, or ciprofloxacin. Nitrofurantoin (Macrobid) is used clinically but isn't on the drug matrix.
  • Uncomplicated community cases get a short course; recurrent UTIs get a more conventional 7–14 day course.
  • More severe cases may need inpatient IV antibiotics. A urinalysis and culture identify the organism and its sensitivity — patients typically start on a broad-spectrum antibiotic and get narrowed once results are back.
  • Pregnancy complicates treatment — Bactrim and ciprofloxacin are avoided, which narrows the antibiotic options right when pregnant patients are already at higher risk for pyelonephritis.
Urosepsis: in elderly or already-sick patients, a urinary tract infection (upper or lower) can progress to sepsis — a severe systemic response with a high mortality rate.

Urinary Obstruction

A blockage can occur anywhere along the urinary tract — the renal pelvis, the ureter, or the bladder/urethra — and whatever is downstream of the blockage backs up, the same way a clogged pipe backs up.

LevelMost Likely Cause
Renal PelvisKidney stones (renal calculi) — most common cause at this level.
UreterKidney stones again, but also pregnancy (an enlarging fetus can compress the ureter) or tumors.
Bladder / UrethraStones rarely reach this far — more likely bladder cancer, a prostate problem, a stricture, or a neurogenic bladder (not a true obstruction, but related to a spinal or neuromuscular injury).

Complication 1 — Infection

  • When urine can't flow freely, infection is much more likely — this is why recurrent UTIs or recurrent pyelonephritis are a common presenting picture for an underlying obstruction.
  • An enlarged prostate is a classic example: urine can't fully empty, leaving residual urine that raises infection risk.

Complication 2 — Back-Pressure

  • The kidneys keep making urine even when it can't get out. This raises pressure upstream of the blockage, causing hydroureter or hydronephrosis (the kidney or ureter distending with retained urine) — and can progress to a postrenal acute kidney injury.
  • This is typically treated as an emergency depending on the patient's urine output and kidney function, and usually needs a surgical fix for the blockage itself.
TRICK: Manifestations of an obstruction come down to three questions: where is the blockage (the renal pelvis has room, so less pain; the ureter is narrow, so more pain), how fast did it happen (fast onset is far less tolerable than a slow-developing one, like a prostate problem that built up over time), and is it internal or external (a stone vs. something compressing from outside). Speed of onset is the single biggest driver of how severe the pain feels.

Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)

What's Happening

  • Kidney stones (renal calculi) are the most common cause of urinary obstruction — crumbs or clumps of crystals ranging from tiny to as large as a golf ball, smooth or jagged depending on composition.
  • Urine is a solution of water (solvent) and particles (solutes). When a particular solute becomes supersaturated, crystals begin forming — always at the level of the kidney/nephron, never in the bladder or ureter.
  • Crystal formation is enhanced by pH changes (seen with frequent UTIs), excess salt concentration in the urine, dehydration, bone disease, gout, renal disease, and urinary stasis (a very immobile, sedentary lifestyle — not just a desk job).
Risk Factor
Male sex (women's risk is catching up, but men are still slightly higher)
Younger age — this condition actually skews toward 20s–30s
Caucasian race
Family history (a parent or grandparent with kidney stones)
Congenital defects or strictures anywhere in the urinary system
Hot weather — more dehydration, more concentrated (higher-solute) urine
Obesity and immobility/sedentary lifestyle
Stone TypeDetail
Calcium (Oxalate or Phosphate)By far the most common — 70 to 80% of all stones. Mostly idiopathic, though family history matters; diet (high sodium, high oxalate, high protein) and high blood/urine calcium or oxalate levels can contribute.
Struvite ("Staghorn")Related to urinary tract infections.
Uric AcidRelated to gout — a patient with gout who develops a kidney stone most likely has a uric acid stone.
CystineRare, less than 1% of stones — not something you need to know in depth.

Renal Colic — What These Patients Look Like

  • Flank pain that radiates to the lower abdomen or groin — spasmodic, coming in waves of about 20 to 60 minutes as the ureter tries to move the stone along.
  • Sharp pain, because the stone is scraping the walls of the ureter.
  • Nausea and vomiting are common because of the pain; difficulty urinating can occur.
  • Fever only occurs if an infection is also present (from urine backup) — a fever alongside a kidney stone points toward pyelonephritis as well.
  • Hematuria, pyuria, and diaphoresis can also be seen.
GoalApproach
Pain ControlAn NSAID (IV Toradol/ketorolac) for milder pain; most patients need an opioid, usually morphine, for acute pain.
HydrationIV fluids, especially if the patient can't keep fluids down from the pain — dilutes the urine to keep crystals from reforming and helps move the stone through.
Prevention (Recurrent Stones)Calcium stones: thiazide diuretics. Struvite stones: treat the underlying infection with antibiotics plus fluids. Uric acid stones: treat the gout, usually with allopurinol.

Urologic Cancers

Kidney Cancer

  • 85% of kidney cancer is renal cell carcinoma — a rare cancer overall (kidney cancers are under 5% of all cancers).
  • #1 risk factor is smoking, by far; also obesity, older age, male sex, and genetic/family history.
  • No early manifestations — this is why it usually isn't diagnosed until it has metastasized (sometimes found incidentally on an unrelated abdominal CT). Once metastasized, five-year survival is only about 10%.
  • Late manifestations: CVA tenderness, cola-colored hematuria, a palpable abdominal mass. Metastasizes to bone or lung — dyspnea, cough, and bone pain are often how it's finally caught.
  • Resistant to chemo. If it hasn't metastasized, the kidney is simply removed (the other kidney compensates); if it has, chemo and radiation are needed.

Bladder Cancer

  • The 4th most common cancer in men — typically urothelial carcinoma (cancer of the bladder lining).
  • #1 risk factor is smoking again; also male sex, occupational toxin exposure (rubber factories, paint), and low fluid intake (concentrates toxins against the bladder lining for longer).
  • Early: hematuria — urine may look pink or reddish, or the blood may only be visible on a UA. Later: UTI-like symptoms (frequency, urgency, dysuria).
  • Stage 1 is treated with intravesical (inside-the-bladder) chemotherapy; advanced disease needs systemic chemo.
BCG (Intravesical) TherapyDetail
What It IsA live vaccine instilled directly into the bladder (via an in-and-out catheter) for early-stage bladder cancer. It stimulates an inflammatory response so the patient's own immune system learns to recognize and attack cancer cells.
AdministrationGiven weekly for six to twelve weeks. Held in the bladder for 2 hours; the patient changes position every 15 minutes so the medication contacts all portions of the bladder.
Adverse EffectsBladder irritation and UTI-type symptoms are common, even after treatment ends.
Nursing CareDisinfect (bleach) the patient's urine for 6 hours after treatment to kill the live bacteria before disposal.
BCG is a live vaccine: it must not be given to a patient with a compromised immune system — someone with HIV/AIDS or otherwise immunosuppressed can develop a systemic infection from the weakened live bacteria.

Glomerular Disorders

Where the Damage Occurs

  • Damage happens within the glomerulus, a delicate network of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule. An afferent arteriole brings blood in and an efferent arteriole takes it out; the pressure difference between them pushes toxins, waste, and chemicals out of the blood and into what becomes urine.
  • The glomerular capillary membrane has three layers: the endothelium, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM — the layer where most of the problems below actually occur), and the podocyte layer (specialized epithelial cells that start urine production).
  • The kidneys use about 20% of cardiac output relative to only about 0.5% of body mass — this is why damage at the glomerular level causes such widespread illness.

Glomerulonephritis

TermDetail
DefinitionInflammation within the glomeruli from any cause — infection, post-infection, or a systemic inflammatory condition. Can be focal (one area) or diffuse (both kidneys, all areas). The third leading cause of kidney failure in the US, responsible for about 25% of end-stage renal disease cases.
Immune MechanismPrimarily immune-mediated, through two hypersensitivity patterns: antibodies attacking the GBM directly (about 5% of cases — anti-GBM antibodies), or the more common pattern of circulating antibody-antigen complexes depositing into the GBM. Both trigger complement activation and inflammation that injures the GBM.
Common TriggersPost-strep infection is the most common trigger (bacterial, viral, and parasitic infections can also trigger it); IgA buildup in the kidney (Berger's disease); and systemic autoimmune diseases — lupus, Goodpasture syndrome, and vasculitis.

The Type II vs. Type III hypersensitivity reactions behind glomerulonephritis are covered in full on GI, GU, Skin & Hypersensitivity

Goodpasture Syndrome — A Special Case

  • The glomerular basement membrane is structurally very similar to the basement membrane of the lungs. In Goodpasture syndrome, anti-GBM antibodies attack both — patients often present with hemoptysis (coughing up blood) alongside renal problems, since the same antibody is damaging both organs at once.
HARP: acute glomerulonephritis is characterized by Hematuria, Azotemia (waste product buildup), Retention of sodium and water (generalized edema, increased blood pressure), and Proteinuria (protein spilling into the urine as the damaged GBM lets it through). Chronic glomerulonephritis shows the same picture, just over months instead of hours to days — and it ends the same way, in scar tissue and progressive loss of kidney function toward end-stage renal disease.

Nephrotic Syndrome

TermDetail
DefinitionThe glomerulus becomes too permeable to plasma proteins — measured as more than 3 g of protein lost in a 24-hour urine collection. A common cause is glomerulonephritis, but uncontrolled diabetes damaging the GBM can also cause it.
Why the Protein Loss MattersAlbumin is normally too large to pass the GBM and provides oncotic (pulling) pressure to hold fluid inside the vessels. Once it spills into the urine (proteinuria) and drops in the blood, fluid shifts out of the vessels into the tissue — edema and third-spacing.
HypercoagulabilityAntithrombin III and plasminogen (a precursor to plasmin, which breaks down clots) are also lost in the urine — without them, the blood becomes hypercoagulable, raising the risk of DVT and PE.
HyperlipidemiaThe liver responds to the ongoing protein loss by increasing lipid production, so these patients also develop elevated lipid levels.
HypertensionThe kidneys sense reduced circulating volume (since fluid has third-spaced out of the vessels) and activate the renin-angiotensin system to compensate, raising blood pressure.

Glomerulopathy (Diabetic & Hypertensive)

TermDetail
DefinitionDamage specific to the glomerulus from chronic, uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension — distinct from the inflammatory process of glomerulonephritis.
Diabetic MechanismChronically elevated glucose thickens the GBM over time, so it filters particles and fluid less effectively.
Hypertensive MechanismChronic high pressure on the blood vessels causes sclerosis (scarring) of the glomerulus, decreasing perfusion within the kidney.
PresentationLooks like acute glomerulonephritis — hematuria, oliguria (less than 400 mL/day), fluid retention, hypertension that's harder to control, a BUN:creatinine ratio above 20:1, proteinuria, and low blood protein.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the key difference in presentation between a lower UTI and pyelonephritis?
Pyelonephritis has a sudden onset with fever, chills, and CVA tenderness — the patient looks systemically sick. A lower UTI is more gradual and usually just dysuria, frequency, and urgency.
What are the two big complications of a urinary obstruction, and why do they happen?
Infection (urine isn't flowing freely, so residual urine breeds bacteria) and back-pressure (hydroureter/hydronephrosis, which can become a postrenal AKI).
Where do kidney stone crystals always begin forming, and why does that matter?
Always at the level of the kidney/nephron — never in the bladder or ureter, since that's where urine first becomes supersaturated with a solute.
What are the three main kidney stone types and their most common cause?
Calcium (70–80%, mostly idiopathic/family history), struvite/staghorn (related to UTIs), and uric acid (related to gout).
A kidney stone patient develops a fever. What does that suggest?
An infection is also present — most likely pyelonephritis, since fever isn't a typical renal colic symptom by itself.
What is the #1 risk factor for both kidney cancer and bladder cancer?
Smoking.
Why is renal cell carcinoma usually not diagnosed until it has metastasized?
It has no early manifestations — it's often found incidentally on an unrelated abdominal CT, or only once late signs like CVA tenderness or hematuria appear.
What is BCG therapy, and who should never receive it?
A live vaccine instilled into the bladder to treat early-stage bladder cancer. It should never be given to an immunocompromised patient (e.g., HIV/AIDS) — risk of systemic infection from the live bacteria.
What does the HARP mnemonic stand for in acute glomerulonephritis?
Hematuria, Azotemia, Retention of sodium/water, and Proteinuria.
In nephrotic syndrome, why does losing albumin cause edema?
Albumin provides the oncotic pressure that holds fluid inside blood vessels. Without enough of it, fluid shifts out into the tissues (third-spacing).
Why are nephrotic syndrome patients at higher risk for DVT and PE?
They lose antithrombin III and plasminogen (a clot-breakdown precursor) in the urine along with albumin, creating a hypercoagulable state.
What is glomerulopathy, and what are its two chronic causes?
Glomerulus-specific damage from chronic, uncontrolled diabetes (thickens the GBM) or hypertension (causes glomerular sclerosis/scarring) — distinct from the inflammatory process of glomerulonephritis.
