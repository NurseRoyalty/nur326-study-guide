Pathopharm Review

Coagulation & DVT

NUR 325 review, covering: blood/plasma basics and platelets, the coagulation cascade and hemostasis, anticoagulant pharmacology (heparin, enoxaparin, warfarin, factor Xa inhibitors), antiplatelet pharmacology, and deep vein thrombosis/venous thromboembolism.

Blood Basics — Plasma, Platelets & Hemostasis

Whole blood is about 55% plasma and 45% formed elements (red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets) — and blood itself is only about 8% of total body fluid. Plasma is about 92% water, plus plasma proteins — most of which are made in the liver, which is why liver dysfunction can cause clotting problems.

Plasma ProteinDetail
Albumin~57% of plasma proteins — a carrier molecule whose most important role is regulating water/solute movement through capillaries (drives oncotic pressure).
GlobulinsIncludes HDL, prothrombin (a clotting factor), and hormone-transport proteins.
Clotting FactorsFibrinogen is the most plentiful. Serum = plasma minus clotting factors.

Formed elements include erythrocytes (RBCs, carry oxygen), leukocytes (WBCs — neutrophils for early inflammation/phagocytosis, eosinophils for allergic/parasitic response, basophils for allergic reactions, monocytes/macrophages for phagocytosis, lymphocytes and natural killer cells for immunity), and platelets — the focus of this topic.

Platelets

  • Not true cells — anucleate cytoplasmic fragments made in the bone marrow (with additional storage in the spleen).
  • Normal count: 150,000–400,000. Thrombocytopenia is generally < 100,000 — high bleeding risk.
  • Platelet activation: a resting platelet is smooth; on sensing vessel injury, it transitions to a spiky ("dendritic") shape, becomes sticky, and adheres to the injury site — then platelets aggregate with each other and with the vessel wall, initiating the clotting system and forming an immobilizing meshwork of platelets and fibrin (a clot).

The Coagulation Cascade — Clot Formation & Breakdown

The coagulation cascade (clotting system/coagulation system — same thing) is a group of proteins that activate in sequence to form a blood clot. Platelets are the primary initiators of the cascade. The cascade can be triggered through the extrinsic or intrinsic pathway; thrombin is especially active in the intrinsic pathway and is a major drug target. The clot itself is a meshwork of fibrin strands (produced by the cascade) that traps platelets and other cells (often RBCs) at the injury site. When bleeding stops, that's hemostasis.

Natural "Stop" MechanismHow It Works
Antithrombin IIIA circulating inhibitor of thrombin — stops thrombin from converting fibrinogen to fibrin, halting the clotting process.
Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI)Produced by platelets and endothelial cells — inhibits factor Xa, stopping further clot activation.

Deficiencies in these natural stop mechanisms can cause bleeding disorders. Once the vessel has healed, the clot itself is removed by fibrinolysis ("lysis" = breakdown), carried out by the fibrinolytic system: tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) both convert circulating plasminogen into plasmin, which breaks down fibrin and releases the trapped platelets/RBCs.

Anticoagulant vs. Antiplatelet Therapy — Overview & Bleeding Surveillance

Anticoagulants

  • Inhibit the action/formation of clotting factors.
  • Prevent a clot from forming, or prevent an existing clot from extending — but have no direct effect on an existing clot (don't break it up).

Antiplatelets

  • Inhibit platelet aggregation, preventing the platelet plug from forming.
  • Best for preventing heart attack and stroke.

Both classes are considered antithrombotic (prevent clot/thrombus formation), just by different mechanisms. Common indications: atrial fibrillation, heart disease/heart failure, history of DVT, prolonged immobility (e.g., ortho surgery), and history of stroke.

The universal concern with anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy is bleeding — internal (brain bleed, GI bleed — may not be obvious until it's an emergency) or external (prolonged bleeding from a cut, gum bleeding with a hard-bristle toothbrush). Trend hemoglobin/hematocrit daily — a sudden drop from a previously stable baseline suggests internal bleeding. Vital sign changes from bleeding follow a pattern: tachycardia first (from fluid volume deficit), then later hypotension, pale/ashen skin, and increased respiratory rate/work of breathing. Also watch for bruising and petechiae (tiny pinpoint red/purple dots clustered on the skin). A post-op dressing with a small amount of bright red blood a few hours after surgery is expected; the same finding 4–5 days out in a patient on an anticoagulant is a bleeding concern.

Heparin & Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (Enoxaparin)

DrugDetail
HeparinActivates antithrombin, which indirectly inactivates both thrombin and factor Xa, inhibiting fibrin formation. Given IV or subcutaneously. Derived from pig lung/intestinal mucosa (may raise kosher/halal considerations; pig livestock issues can cause shortages). Onset: ~immediate IV, 20–30 min subcutaneous. Dosed on a weight-based protocol (weight in kilograms — a known source of dosing error). Used for conditions needing prompt anticoagulation (evolving stroke, PE, DVT), as an adjunct for open-heart surgery/dialysis, low-dose post-op DVT prophylaxis, and DIC.
Enoxaparin (Lovenox)Low-molecular-weight heparin — cleaved into smaller fragments than heparin, giving greater factor Xa affinity, better bioavailability, and a longer half-life. Inactivates factor Xa only (not thrombin). Subcutaneous only; no routine lab monitoring needed. Slower onset than heparin (3–5 hr) but longer duration (~12 hr, dosed roughly twice daily vs. heparin's more frequent dosing). Safe in pregnancy (choose an injection site with enough subcutaneous fat). Don't expel the small air bubble in the prefilled syringe — it ensures the full dose is delivered. Don't rub the injection site after administration; rotate sites.

Shared adverse effects: bleeding and thrombocytopenia (including HIT — see below). Use both cautiously with spinal/epidural anesthesia (hematoma risk — monitor for neurologic changes); enoxaparin carries a black box warning for spinal hematoma with an epidural catheter in place. Don't give enoxaparin together with heparin or other anticoagulants (an exception: enoxaparin may be paired with oral warfarin when treating a DVT/PE). Labs for heparin: anti-Xa or aPTT (activated partial thromboplastin time), drawn at baseline and roughly every 6 hours, with dose/rate adjusted by protocol until therapeutic (similar in concept to an insulin sliding-scale protocol) — draw from a site other than the heparin infusion line when possible, or stop the infusion and flush thoroughly first to avoid a diluted/contaminated result.

Heparin safety: if giving protamine sulfate (the heparin/enoxaparin antidote — binds heparin, inactivating its anticoagulant effect) IV, push it slowly — too fast can cause profound hypotension. Also watch for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — a low platelet count (< 100,000) with new thrombi forming, seen in ~5–15% of patients on heparin. Monitor platelet counts, stop heparin immediately if HIT is suspected, and switch to a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g., argatroban) if anticoagulation is still needed.

Warfarin (Coumadin)

Warfarin is a vitamin K inhibitor — it blocks vitamin K's role in synthesizing several coagulation factors, so those factors aren't made. Given PO only, once daily, typically in the evening. Onset is delayed ~24 hours after the dose, but duration is long (2–5 days) — this lag is why bridging with a fast-acting anticoagulant (like enoxaparin) is common when starting warfarin. Indications: prevention of VTE/DVT/PE, thrombus prevention in atrial fibrillation or with mechanical heart valves, and reducing recurrence of TIA/MI. Contraindicated in pregnancy/breastfeeding (teratogenic).

TopicDetail
Adverse EffectsBleeding, lethargy, muscle pain, and — notably — purple toes.
AntidoteVitamin K (IV) — restores the substrate warfarin blocks. Fresh frozen plasma or whole blood can also replace the missing clotting factors directly.
Lab MonitoringPT/INR. A person not on warfarin has an INR around 1 or less; the therapeutic range on warfarin is about 2–3.5 (goal varies by indication). Checked at baseline, then monthly once therapeutic; dose adjusted based on trend.
Pre-Op HoldingHeld before surgery (including dental procedures) — anywhere from ~72 hours up to 2 weeks depending on the procedure and the patient's INR.

Significant interactions: many common medications (including OTC cimetidine) raise or lower INR — patients need to disclose all medications/supplements. Alcohol also thins the blood. Since warfarin blocks vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors, teach patients to keep vitamin-K intake (green leafy vegetables, cabbage, asparagus, okra, lettuce, green beans) consistent rather than avoided entirely — big swings in vitamin K intake are the problem, not the food itself. Herbal supplements (ginkgo biloba, St. John's wort) also interact. Patient teaching: wear a medical alert bracelet, use a soft-bristle or electric toothbrush and an electric razor to reduce bleeding risk.

Direct Factor Xa Inhibitors — Apixaban & Rivaroxaban

Apixaban (Eliquis) and rivaroxaban (Xarelto) directly inhibit factor Xa. Used preemptively before knee/hip replacement, to prevent stroke in atrial fibrillation, postoperatively for thromboprophylaxis, and to treat DVT/PE. Side effects: bleeding, hematoma, dizziness, rash, GI distress, peripheral edema. Black box warnings: spinal hematoma with an epidural catheter, and thrombosis risk if the drug is stopped abruptly (don't discontinue without guidance).

TopicDetail
InteractionsSt. John's wort, rifampin, and phenytoin decrease effectiveness. Both are CYP substrates — grapefruit juice and CYP inhibitors increase drug levels/bleeding risk. Don't combine with other anticoagulants.
MonitoringNo routine coagulation lab monitoring needed (a major selling point vs. warfarin) — but monitor liver function, since these drugs can affect it.
AntidoteAndexanet alfa (Andexxa) — recombinant factor Xa; a newer, extremely expensive antidote (early doses ran roughly $50,000), so it's used only when truly needed.

Antiplatelet Pharmacology — Aspirin

Aspirin is classified as an NSAID but is the only NSAID used for its antiplatelet effect: it blocks prostaglandin synthesis via the COX pathway, which also blocks platelet aggregation, making platelets less sticky. Used to prevent/treat MI and to prevent ischemic stroke. Given PO — 81 mg ("baby aspirin") or 325 mg, dose matched to indication.

For an acute MI, the patient chews a baby aspirin rather than swallowing it whole — and it must be a non-enteric-coated (plain, white) tablet, not the orange enteric-coated pill, since enteric coating is meant to survive intact to the intestine and would delay absorption.

Side effects: bleeding, nausea/vomiting, drowsiness, confusion. Enteric-coated/extended-release formulations reduce GI upset for routine (non-emergency) use — but never crush an enteric-coated tablet. Daily aspirin for primary cardiovascular prevention has fallen out of favor, since bleeding risk generally outweighs the benefit in patients without known cardiovascular disease. Not given to children (Reye syndrome risk). Contraindications: thrombocytopenia, active bleeding, blood cancers, traumatic injury, GI ulcers, vitamin K deficiency, recent hemorrhagic stroke. Antidote: desmopressin (DDAVP).

Antiplatelet Pharmacology — Clopidogrel, Ticagrelor & Direct Thrombin Inhibitors

Clopidogrel (Plavix) and ticagrelor (Brilinta) are ADP inhibitors — they alter the platelet membrane so it doesn't receive the aggregation signal, decreasing platelet accumulation. Used to reduce stroke risk, for TIA prophylaxis, and post-MI; sometimes combined with aspirin, though recent evidence questions how much added benefit that combination provides. Given PO. Same contraindications as aspirin (thrombocytopenia, active bleeding, blood cancers, traumatic injury, GI ulcers, vitamin K deficiency, recent hemorrhagic stroke). Side effects differ from other agents here: chest pain, edema, flu-like symptoms, abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, epistaxis (nosebleed), pruritus (itching). Antidote: DDAVP, or a platelet transfusion.

DrugNotable Warning
ClopidogrelBlack box warning: certain genetic variants reduce conversion to the active metabolite, raising cardiovascular event risk — ask about genetic/family history before starting. Effectiveness is reduced by amiodarone, calcium channel blockers, and PPIs.
TicagrelorBlack box warning: increased bleeding risk with aspirin doses over 100 mg.

Direct thrombin inhibitors — argatroban and bivalirudin — irreversibly bind and inactivate thrombin, so it can't convert fibrinogen to fibrin. Given IV only; used to treat HIT and in patients undergoing PCI who are at high HIT risk from heparin exposure. Use argatroban cautiously with hepatic dysfunction. Adverse effect: bleeding. Monitor anti-Xa, H&H, and platelets.

DVT & VTE — Terminology and Virchow's Triad

A thrombus is a blood clot still attached to the vessel wall. Once it detaches, it's a thromboembolus; if it originated in the venous system, it's a venous thromboembolism (VTE). Thrombi form more often in veins than arteries because venous flow is slower, steadier, and lower-pressure. A thrombus in a deep vein — most often the iliac or femoral vein — is a deep vein thrombosis (DVT), typically forming near a venous valve where inflammation promotes inappropriate platelet aggregation.

Virchow's Triad — Three Factors Promoting Venous Thrombosis

  • Venous stasis — slow blood flow. Risk: older age, immobility (hospitalized/post-op patients), heart failure.
  • Venous endothelial damage — injury to the vein lining, from trauma, certain IV medications, or surgery.
  • Hypercoagulable states — inherited clotting disorders, malignancy, pregnancy, oral contraceptives/hormone replacement, recent surgery, and genetic factors (prothrombin/antithrombin mutations).

Additional risk factors: ortho surgery (especially hip/knee replacement, hip fracture — high immobility, sometimes in casts), spinal cord injury, OB/GYN conditions, obesity, smoking, certain cancers, inflammatory bowel disease, prior personal or family history of DVT, age > 60, and varicose veins. Pregnancy/oral contraceptive risk extends up to 6 weeks postpartum.

DVT & VTE — Signs, Symptoms & Complications

Classic DVT findings: pain, redness, warmth, and swelling in the affected extremity (most often a lower extremity) — always compare the affected limb to the other side. Some patients are entirely asymptomatic. Significant obstruction to venous flow raises venous pressure, causing edema in the affected limb.

ComplicationDetail
Pulmonary Embolism (PE)A thrombus lodges in the pulmonary circulation, blocking gas exchange.
Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary HypertensionRare — repeated pulmonary clots raise pulmonary circulation pressure over time; presents with shortness of breath.
Post-Thrombotic SyndromeAffects 8–70% of patients — chronic inflammation and venous hypertension from the thrombus blocking flow and damaging the vein wall. Presents with pain, aching, fatigue, swelling sensation, cramps, itching, heaviness, paresthesia, bursting pain, and venous claudication (thigh/calf/buttock pain with walking); visually, spider veins, venous dilation, cyanosis/discoloration, increased pigmentation, and venous ulceration.
Phlegmasia cerulea dolens ("painful blue inflammation") — rare, but a true emergency: major leg veins become totally or near-totally occluded, causing sudden massive swelling, intense deep pain, and severe cyanosis. Left untreated, it can progress to amputation or gangrene.

DVT & VTE — Prevention & Treatment

InterventionDetail
Anticoagulation TherapyFor active DVT/VTE — see the coagulation modifier drugs above.

IVC FilterAn inferior vena cava interruption device — mechanically catches traveling clots before they reach the heart, lungs, or brain.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the difference between plasma and serum?
Plasma is the liquid portion of blood (~92% water, plus plasma proteins — albumin, globulins, clotting factors). Serum is plasma minus the clotting factors — "serum + clotting factors = plasma."
Walk through platelet activation from a resting state to clot formation.
A resting platelet is smooth and circulates freely. On sensing vessel injury, it transitions to a spiky ("dendritic") shape, becomes sticky, and adheres to the injury site. Platelets then aggregate with each other and the vessel wall, activating the clotting cascade and forming a meshwork of platelets and fibrin (a clot).
What are the two natural mechanisms that stop the coagulation cascade, and how does each work?
Antithrombin III — a circulating inhibitor of thrombin, stopping fibrinogen from converting to fibrin. Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) — produced by platelets/endothelial cells, inhibits factor Xa. Deficiencies in either can cause bleeding disorders.
What's the key mechanistic difference between heparin and enoxaparin (Lovenox)?
Heparin activates antithrombin to inactivate BOTH thrombin and factor Xa. Enoxaparin (low-molecular-weight heparin) inactivates factor Xa ONLY, not thrombin. This is also why enoxaparin doesn't need routine coagulation lab monitoring the way heparin does.
A patient on a heparin drip develops a platelet count of 85,000 with new clot formation. What's happening, and what's the nursing action?
This is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — an antibody-mediated reaction causing low platelets and paradoxical new thrombus formation, seen in ~5-15% of patients on heparin. Stop the heparin immediately and anticipate a switch to a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g., argatroban).
Why does warfarin dosing take days to become effective, and what lab is used to monitor it?
Warfarin blocks the synthesis of new vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors, but existing factors already in circulation still have to clear the body first — onset is delayed about 24 hours and effects last 2-5 days. Monitored with PT/INR; therapeutic range on warfarin is about 2-3.5 (vs. ~1 or less off warfarin).
What dietary teaching should a nurse give a patient starting warfarin, regarding vitamin K?
Keep vitamin K intake CONSISTENT rather than avoiding it entirely — big day-to-day swings in intake of vitamin-K-rich foods (green leafy vegetables, cabbage, asparagus) are what throws off INR control, not eating them at all.
What's the major advantage of apixaban/rivaroxaban over warfarin, and what is their reversal agent?
No routine coagulation lab monitoring is required (unlike warfarin's PT/INR draws), which improves adherence. Reversal agent: andexanet alfa (Andexxa) — a recombinant factor Xa, though it's extremely expensive and used only when truly needed.
A patient is having an acute MI and is given aspirin. How should it be administered, and why does tablet color matter?
The patient should CHEW a non-enteric-coated (plain, white) baby aspirin rather than swallow it whole, for fast absorption. Enteric-coated tablets are orange and are designed to survive intact to the intestine — chewing one wouldn't provide the fast antiplatelet effect needed in an acute MI.
What are the three components of Virchow's triad, and give an example risk factor for each.
Venous stasis (immobility, heart failure), venous endothelial damage (trauma, surgery, certain IV medications), and hypercoagulable states (pregnancy, oral contraceptives, malignancy, inherited clotting disorders). Together, these three factors promote inappropriate venous thrombosis.
What's the difference between a thrombus, a thromboembolus, and a VTE?
A thrombus is a clot still attached to the vessel wall. Once it detaches, it becomes a thromboembolus. If it originated in the venous system, it's specifically called a venous thromboembolism (VTE).
A patient has sudden massive leg swelling, intense pain, and severe cyanosis. What condition should the nurse suspect, and why is it an emergency?
Phlegmasia cerulea dolens ("painful blue inflammation") — near-total occlusion of major leg veins. It's a true emergency because, left untreated, it can progress to amputation or gangrene.
