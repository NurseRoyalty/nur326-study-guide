Gallbladder & Pancreatic Disorders

These two organ systems are grouped together because they share the same root problem: a gallstone blocking the common bile duct can cause cholecystitis in the gallbladder AND back up pancreatic enzymes into the pancreas at the same time. Understanding cholelithiasis first makes the pancreatitis section make a lot more sense.

Cholelithiasis (Gallstones)

TermDetail
DefinitionStones in the gallbladder — colloquially "gallstones."
Why They FormImpaired metabolism/breakdown of cholesterol, bilirubin, and bile acids — these components saturate in the gallbladder, sit there, and aggregate into stones.
Stone TypesCholesterol stones (~70%, the most common); pigmented stones; mixed pigmented stones (black, hard, less common — composed of something other than mainly cholesterol).

Risk Factors

  • Obesity — a major risk factor.
  • Being female, middle-aged (most common around 40), and using oral contraceptives.
  • Rapid weight loss.
  • Genetic predisposition; higher rates in Indigenous populations (60–70% lifetime risk) than in white Americans (10–15%) — a very common condition in developed countries overall.
  • Pre-existing pancreatic disease.
MNEMONIC (the instructor's own, dated but memorable): "Fair, fat, and 40" — a classic (if old-fashioned) shorthand for the classic gallstone patient profile: female, overweight, and around age 40.

Biliary Colic & Symptoms of Obstructed Bile Flow

Biliary Colic

  • Most patients with stones confined to the gallbladder are asymptomatic. Pain starts when one or more stones lodge in the cystic or common duct during a gallbladder contraction.
  • Epigastric or right-upper-quadrant pain, often radiating to the back — usually severe, intermittent but can be steady.
  • Classically triggered by fatty food intake, occurring about 30 minutes to a couple of hours after a fatty meal (think: after a fast-food meal).
  • Other vague symptoms: heartburn, flatulence, epigastric discomfort, food intolerances (especially fats).
FindingMechanism
Jaundice (Obstructive)A migrated stone blocks the common bile duct — bile can't flow into the duodenum.
Dark, Foamy UrineConjugated bilirubin backs up into the blood; being water-soluble, it spills into the urine.
Clay-Colored StoolsBilirubin can't reach the intestines to become urobilinogen, the pigment that gives stool its normal brown color.
SteatorrheaBile salts can't reach the duodenum, so fat isn't broken down/digested — fatty, undigested stool results.
PruritusBacked-up bile salts deposit into the skin, causing itching.
Bleeding TendencyWithout bile, vitamin K (needed to produce clotting factors) can't be absorbed.

Not every patient with cholelithiasis develops these manifestations — they only appear if a stone actually blocks bile from reaching the GI system.

Cholecystitis

What It Is

  • Inflammation of the gallbladder — usually caused by a gallstone lodged in the cystic duct, causing the gallbladder to become edematous, hyperemic, and distended, and over time to lose function. Can be acute or chronic.
  • Pain resembles biliary colic. It's more dangerous than simple cholelithiasis because pressure from the inflamed, distended gallbladder wall decreases blood flow — which can lead to ischemia, necrosis, and perforation if untreated. This is why cholecystitis pain is taken very seriously in the ER.
  • Signs of inflammation: fever, leukocytosis, rebound tenderness, guarding (more specific to cholecystitis than plain cholelithiasis).
  • Labs: increased bilirubin, increased alkaline phosphatase.
  • Chronic, ongoing cholecystitis can lead to scarring and eventual degeneration of the gallbladder.
Cholecystitis can progress to ischemia, necrosis, and perforation if the inflammatory pressure against the gallbladder wall isn't relieved — this is why it's treated as a surgical priority.

Gallbladder Disorders — Pharmacology

TermDetail
IV Ketorolac (Toradol)First-line pain control for both cholelithiasis and cholecystitis — an NSAID. Opioids (morphine, hydromorphone/Dilaudid) are used if pain isn't well controlled with this alone.
AntiemeticsOndansetron (Zofran) or promethazine (Phenergan) for nausea/vomiting.
AnticholinergicsDecrease gallbladder secretion and stop smooth-muscle spasms — reduces the risk of lodging additional stones and causing more issues.
Bile Acid TherapyGiven to help dissolve stones — typically reserved for patients who can't have surgery. Not common.

The definitive treatment for both cholelithiasis and cholecystitis is usually gallbladder removal (cholecystectomy); medications mainly manage symptoms and risk factors in the meantime.

Acute Pancreatitis

What It Is

  • Inflammation of the pancreas. Most cases are mild and resolve spontaneously, but about 20% become severe enough to require hospitalization.
  • Most often caused by obstruction of pancreatic enzyme outflow — usually from a gallstone blocking the common bile duct, which the pancreas also uses to secrete its exocrine chemicals. Also caused by alcohol, certain medications/drugs, and some viral infections.
  • When pancreatic enzymes can't get out, they auto-digest the pancreas itself, causing vascular damage, coagulopathy, necrosis, and fat necrosis.
EnzymeEffect When Trapped in the Pancreas
TrypsinEdema, necrosis, hemorrhage.
ElastaseHemorrhage.
Phospholipase AFat necrosis within the pancreas.
KallikreinEdema and increased vascular permeability (fluid leaks into the pancreas and surrounding space — can cause ascites); also triggers smooth-muscle contraction, which causes pain and can contribute to shock in severe cases.
LipaseFat necrosis — its normal job is fat breakdown, so trapped in the pancreas it breaks down pancreatic cells instead.

Clinical Presentation of Acute Pancreatitis

What These Patients Look Like

  • Pain is the hallmark — severe, sudden-onset epigastric or mid-abdominal pain, often radiating to the back. The abdomen is tender with guarding; patients resist palpation. Pain comes from edema-driven distension of the pancreatic ducts/capsule plus chemical irritation and inflammation of the peritoneum.
  • Nausea/vomiting, abdominal distension (fluid seeping into the peritoneal cavity), fever, and hypoactive bowel sounds.
  • In severe cases: hypotension and tachycardia (from vascular permeability/fluid loss), possibly progressing to hypovolemic shock.
  • Jaundice if a blocked common bile duct is the underlying cause.
  • Labs: amylase rises with organ damage; glucose can be elevated OR low depending on which pancreatic cells are damaged (monitor closely); elevated WBC count (inflammatory state).
MNEMONIC (the instructor's own): Gray Turner sign (bruising in the flank) and Cullen sign (bruising around the umbilicus/belly button) both indicate hemorrhagic pancreatitis. To remember Cullen = belly button: think of vampires — they don't have belly buttons — and "the Cullens" from Twilight. These are severe, uncommon findings; you do NOT need them present to diagnose pancreatitis.

Complications of Acute Pancreatitis

Pseudocyst

  • A fluid-filled cavity that forms OUTSIDE the pancreas, visible on CT.
  • Contains necrotic products and secretions — creates more inflammation and scarring.
  • Can sometimes be palpated as an epigastric mass.
  • If it perforates, it spills necrotic contents into the peritoneal cavity, causing peritonitis — may need surgical intervention.

Pancreatic Abscess

  • A large fluid-filled cavity INSIDE the pancreas — the result of extensive necrosis (usually with necrotic hemorrhagic pancreatitis).
  • Can result in further infection or perforation and peritonitis.
  • Presents with the usual pancreatitis picture plus an abdominal mass, very high fever, and leukocytosis (infection).

Other Complications

  • Pulmonary: pleural effusion (from vascular permeability/edema) and diaphragmatic irritation, both worsened by patients avoiding deep breaths due to pain.
  • Hypovolemic shock (from vascular permeability and fluid loss).
  • Tetany from hypocalcemia — lipase-generated free fatty acids bind calcium and deposit it in the retroperitoneum, dropping serum calcium. Monitor electrolyte status.

Chronic Pancreatitis

TermDetail
DefinitionA progressive, fibrotic disease of the pancreas. Unlike acute pancreatitis (which resolves once a gallstone is removed or an offending medication is stopped), chronic pancreatitis does not resolve.
Most Common CauseAlcohol abuse — alcohol is processed by acinar cells in the pancreas; when these stop working, a toxic metabolite forms that destroys pancreatic tissue and releases inflammatory cytokines, damaging both the acinar cells and the islet cells (which manage glucose/insulin). Other causes: genetics, chronic gallstone obstruction, smoking.
PresentationRecurring attacks of acute pancreatitis (severe, sudden-onset pain) plus ongoing loss of normal pancreatic function between attacks. Pain is the biggest reported symptom — a chronic pain condition that's very hard to treat.
Other ManifestationsWeight loss (from both pain and fat malabsorption — the enzymes needed to digest fat are missing); diabetes, from loss of the islet cells that manage insulin/glucose.

Diabetes mellitus itself — a disorder of the pancreas's endocrine function — is covered in full on Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome; this page covers the pancreas's exocrine (digestive enzyme) dysfunction

Pancreatitis — Pharmacology

TermDetail
Opioids (Hydromorphone or Morphine)First-line pain control for acute/chronic pancreatitis.
DicyclomineAn anticholinergic antispasmodic — reduces secretions and relaxes smooth muscle to decrease duct spasms (also used for cholecystitis, above).
Antacids & H2 Receptor BlockersBoth decrease hydrochloric acid secretion in the stomach, which in turn decreases pancreatic enzyme secretion — reducing the auto-digestion driving the disease.
PancrelipasePancreatic enzyme replacement for CHRONIC pancreatitis — replaces the digestive enzymes patients no longer produce. Must be taken with every single meal and snack. No significant adverse effects, but adherence (taking it every time they eat) is essential.

Patients who lose islet cell function from chronic pancreatitis may also need insulin or glucagon, depending on their blood sugar.

What percentage of gallstones are primarily cholesterol stones?
About 70%.
What is biliary colic, and what classically triggers it?
Severe epigastric/RUQ pain, often radiating to the back, from a gallstone lodged in the cystic or common duct — classically triggered 30 minutes to a couple hours after a fatty meal.
Why does obstructed bile flow cause steatorrhea?
Bile salts can't reach the duodenum, so dietary fat isn't broken down/digested.
What makes cholecystitis more dangerous than simple cholelithiasis?
Pressure from the inflamed, distended gallbladder wall decreases blood flow, risking ischemia, necrosis, and perforation.
What is the first-line pain medication for cholelithiasis/cholecystitis?
IV ketorolac (Toradol), an NSAID — opioids are added if pain isn't controlled.
What is the most common cause of acute pancreatitis?
A gallstone obstructing the common bile duct, blocking pancreatic enzyme outflow.
Why is trapped lipase dangerous inside the pancreas?
Its normal job is fat breakdown — trapped in the pancreas, it causes fat necrosis of pancreatic tissue instead.
What are Gray Turner sign and Cullen sign, and what do they indicate?
Gray Turner = flank bruising; Cullen = periumbilical bruising. Both indicate hemorrhagic pancreatitis (a severe, uncommon finding).
What's the difference between a pancreatic pseudocyst and a pancreatic abscess?
A pseudocyst is a necrotic fluid-filled cavity OUTSIDE the pancreas; an abscess is a large infected fluid-filled cavity INSIDE the pancreas, from extensive necrosis.
Why can acute pancreatitis cause tetany?
Lipase-generated free fatty acids bind calcium and deposit it in the retroperitoneum, causing hypocalcemia.
What is the most common cause of CHRONIC pancreatitis, and what two problems does the resulting islet cell loss cause?
Alcohol abuse. Islet cell loss causes diabetes; loss of digestive enzymes causes fat malabsorption/weight loss.
What is pancrelipase, and what's the key adherence point for taking it?
Pancreatic enzyme replacement for chronic pancreatitis — it must be taken with every single meal and snack.
