Pathopharm Review

GI, GU, Skin & Hypersensitivity

NUR 325 review, covering: skin disorders (fungal, viral, and bacterial skin infections; skin cancer; eczema and psoriasis) and antifungal pharmacology, basic GI pharmacology (constipation and diarrhea), hypersensitivity reactions (Types I–IV) and their pharmacotherapy, and an introduction to urologic problems (UTIs, overactive bladder, incontinence).

Fungal Skin Infections — Transmission & Classification

Fungi are spore-producing organisms that live naturally on skin, in soil, on plants, and on household surfaces — usually harmlessly. Fungus becomes a problem three ways: implantation (entering through broken skin after an injury), inhalation (airborne fungus entering the body), and after antibiotic use (wiping out normal flora lets fungus proliferate). Fungal infections are more common in the elderly and immunocompromised — including patients with vascular indwelling catheters, organ transplant recipients, and chemotherapy patients.

TermMeaning
MycosisA fungal infection in general — dermatophytes cause superficial mycoses (affecting only the surface layers of skin).
TineaMycosis caused by dermatophytes — fungi that love keratinized tissue (hair, skin, nails). Also called "ringworm," not because a worm is involved, but because it can cause a ring-shaped rash.
CandidiasisMycosis caused by Candida albicans, a yeast.

Tinea and candidiasis are the two most common fungal infections. Superficial fungal infections are typically treated with topical antifungal creams/ointments; severe, extensive cases need systemic (PO or IV) antifungals.

Candidiasis & Systemic Fungal Infections

Candidiasis (thrush, yeast infection) is caused by Candida albicans, normally found on mucous membranes, skin, the GI tract, and the vagina — it becomes pathogenic particularly in critically ill and immunocompromised patients. Risk factors: immunosuppression (iatrogenic, e.g. post-transplant, or acquired, e.g. HIV/chemotherapy), antibiotic use (the stronger the antibiotic, the higher the risk), a moist/warm local environment, pregnancy, diabetes, infancy (under 6 months, from decreased immune reactivity).

Candidiasis affects only the outer layers of mucous membranes and skin — typically the mouth, vagina, an uncircumcised penis, nail folds, and large skin folds (intertriginous areas). Presents as white lesions in the mouth or beefy-red spots with satellite lesions in warm, moist skin folds. Treated with topical antifungal agents.

Systemic Fungal Infections

  • Require aggressive oral or IV antifungal treatment and typically infect internal organs — intestines, urinary tract, lungs, brain.
  • Common lung infections: histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, pneumocystis.
  • Cryptococcus affects both the lungs and meninges.

Fluconazole nursing considerations: needs an acidic stomach environment to absorb properly, so separate it by at least 2 hours from anything that reduces stomach acidity (antacids, PPIs). Never mix IV fluconazole with other IV medications. Watch for drug interactions — increased bleeding/hematoma risk with warfarin, hypoglycemia risk with sulfonylureas, and increased Haldol or Dilantin levels.

Amphotericin B is a high-alert drug — it must be diluted and infused slowly. It's hard on the kidneys (monitor BUN/creatinine) and can cause cardiac dysrhythmias (put the patient on a cardiac monitor during IV administration).

Herpes Zoster (Shingles)

Shingles is caused by reactivation of the varicella zoster virus (the chickenpox virus), which lies dormant in a dermatome after a chickenpox infection. Reactivated by immunosuppression, significant stress, or illness — most common in people over 50, but can occur at any age in anyone who has had chickenpox. A prodromal period of burning/tingling along a dermatome precedes a rash of vesicles on a red base that dry and crust over.

Key Features

  • Follows dermatomal lines but is asymmetric and does not cross midline — appears on only one side of the body.
  • Vesicles are extremely painful; the rash clears in about 2–3 weeks.
  • Most contagious when vesicles are weeping — can transmit chickenpox (not shingles) to someone who has never had chickenpox or the vaccine.
  • Treated with antivirals. A notable complication is postherpetic neuralgia — persistent, indefinite pain in the same area — affecting about 13% of patients over 60, which is why the shingles vaccine is recommended starting around age 50–60.

MRSA

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a staph strain resistant to methicillin and the penicillin family as a whole.

Hospital-Acquired MRSA

  • Associated with invasive procedures — surgery, IV tubing, central lines, artificial joints.
  • Treated with IV vancomycin or linezolid.

Community-Acquired MRSA

  • Often begins as a painful boil; transmitted person-to-person. Highest risk: high school wrestlers, childcare workers, crowded living conditions.
  • Treated with Bactrim or dicloxacillin.

MRSA presents with warm skin, purulent drainage, possible fever, and can develop an abscess quickly — it can burrow deep into tissue and spread to bones, joints, surgical wounds, the bloodstream, heart valves, and lungs. It's not limited to skin — MRSA can also appear in urine, lungs, and sputum. Pre-surgical prophylaxis uses mupirocin nasal ointment.

Skin Cancer — Precancerous Lesions & ABCDE Screening

LesionDescription
Actinic KeratosisBenign, precancerous lesion from sun/UV damage — rough, scaly, red plaques. More common in light-skinned people.
Solar LentigoBenign, precancerous "liver spots"/"age spots," also from sun exposure.

Both are watched, measured, and followed over time to catch malignant transformation early. Skin cancer disproportionately affects Caucasians (20× more likely to be diagnosed than African-Americans) — but African-Americans are 4× more likely to be diagnosed after the cancer has already spread to an advanced stage, a disparity tied to social determinants of health, medical racism, and inadequate provider training in recognizing skin findings across all skin tones.

ABCDE melanoma screening: Asymmetry, Border (uneven, notched, crusty), Color (variation — white, brown, blue), Diameter (larger than a pencil eraser, >6mm), Evolving (changing size, shape, or color; new bleeding/scabbing).

Constipation & Diarrhea Pathophysiology

Constipation is small, infrequent, or difficult bowel movements — a general guideline is fewer than 3 bowel movements per week. Common causes: a low-fiber diet, lack of exercise (exercise stimulates peristalsis), slowed peristalsis (especially in elderly patients), and pathologic conditions altering GI motility or integrity (obstruction, diverticulitis). Unrelieved constipation can progress to fecal impaction — a firm, immovable stool obstructing the lower GI tract. Patients with an impaction can still have continuous "diarrhea" as liquid squeezes around the blockage, plus loss of appetite, nausea/vomiting, abdominal distention, cramping, and rectal pain.

Diarrhea is increased frequency and fluidity of bowel movements — often the first sign of a larger GI disorder. Acute diarrhea can result from infection (viral/bacterial), emotional stress, or medications (antibiotics, antacids, chemotherapy). Chronic diarrhea (symptoms >4 weeks) often reflects chronic GI infection, altered motility/integrity, malabsorption, or endocrine disorders.

MechanismWhat HappensExamples
OsmoticIncreased osmotically active solutes (e.g., magnesium sulfate) pull sodium and water into the gut.Common with concentrated tube feedings.
SecretoryA bacterial toxin increases secretion and inhibits reabsorption.Vibrio cholerae, Staphylococcus aureus.

Diarrhea's consequences go beyond the underlying cause: skin breakdown (especially in hospitalized or low-motility patients), fluid/electrolyte imbalance, and nutritional concerns from poor absorption — all bigger risks in the very young, very old, and debilitated.

GI Pharmacology — Antidiarrheals

Antidiarrheals are chosen based on the specific cause when known, and are always given orally (no IV options). Three groups: adsorbents, anti-motility drugs, and probiotics. (Opioids have anti-motility properties too, but are rarely used for this due to respiratory depression and dependency risk.)

GI Pharmacology — Laxatives

Laxative choice is individualized by the patient's age, constipation severity, and contributing factors, alongside dietary fiber and increased activity. Laxatives are among the most misused OTC medications — inappropriate use can cause dependence and bowel damage. Only bulk-forming laxatives are appropriate for long-term use; the rest are for short-term use only.

ClassDrug(s)Mechanism & Notes
Bulk-FormingPsyllium (Metamucil)Works like dietary fiber — absorbs water, increases stool bulk, distends the bowel to trigger motility. No systemic effects. Must be taken with liberal water (prevents esophageal obstruction/impaction). Contraindicated with intestinal obstruction/impaction. The only class safe for long-term use.
EmollientDocusate sodium (Colace), mineral oilDocusate lubricates fecal material/intestinal walls and softens stool — used for fecal impaction and to prevent opioid-induced constipation. Mineral oil lubricates the intestines (PO or PR/enema) for hard stools/impaction. Can decrease vitamin absorption.

Hypersensitivity Basics & Type I (IgE-Mediated) Reactions

A hypersensitivity reaction is a normal immune response inappropriately triggered, excessive relative to the trigger, or producing effects damaging to the body. Triggered by a specific antigen-antibody or antibody-lymphocyte reaction. Types I–III are antibody-mediated (via B cells/plasma cells); Type IV is T-cell-mediated and delayed.

Memory aid: B cells make antiBodies (Types I–III); T cells provide long-Term, delayed immunity (Type IV).

Hypersensitivity reactions are antigen-specific and typically don't occur on first exposure — the first exposure sensitizes the patient (antibody production begins), and the reaction happens on a later exposure.

FeatureDetail
MechanismFirst exposure to an antigen (e.g., pollen) triggers a B cell to mature into a plasma cell, which produces IgE antibodies. These IgE antibodies attach to mast cells and wait. On re-exposure, the antigen links to the mast-cell-bound IgE, triggering degranulation — the mast cell dumps chemical mediators (e.g., histamine).
Effects of MediatorsVasodilation (drop in BP), laryngeal edema/swelling (the most severe reaction — anaphylactic shock), urticaria (hives), increased vascular permeability (fluid leak, worsening hypotension), bronchial smooth muscle constriction (wheezing), and irritant receptor stimulation (pruritus/itching).
Common TriggersEnvironmental (pet dander, pollen, dust, mold), foods (nuts, shellfish, eggs — most are proteins), medications (penicillin, contrast dye). Note: a shellfish/seafood allergy is a red flag for a possible contrast dye allergy, since the two hypersensitivities are related.
EtiologyGenetic component — 30% risk if one parent is allergic to an antigen, 50% if both. Theorized to be an inherited overactive immune system.

Type I reactions can be local (e.g., allergic rhinitis — about 50% of the US population reacts to one of the ten most common allergens) or systemic (anaphylaxis — a medical emergency, covered next). An allergy shot works by building tolerance to the allergen (decreasing sensitivity); an allergy medication works by blocking the mediator response instead.

Anaphylaxis, Epinephrine & Hypersensitivity Pharmacotherapy

Systemic Type I reactions release chemical mediators throughout the body and are considered a medical emergency — laryngeal edema can make breathing difficult or impossible, and widespread vasodilation causes vascular collapse with severe hypotension. Most common triggers: medications, bee stings, foods — even tiny doses can trigger a reaction, which can start within minutes (early signs: itching, hives, progressing quickly to bronchoconstriction and swelling).

Anaphylaxis is life-threatening — laryngeal edema can obstruct breathing, and widespread vasodilation causes vascular collapse. Treat with antihistamines, glucocorticoids, and epinephrine. Educate patients to wear a MedicAlert bracelet, carry an EpiPen, and seek medical attention after any epinephrine use — symptoms can return once its short half-life wears off.

Epinephrine is a sympathomimetic (mimics fight-or-flight): a vasopressor, bronchodilator, anti-asthmatic, and vasoconstrictor, acting on both alpha (arteries) and beta (heart, lungs, arteries, skeletal muscle) receptors. It inhibits mast cell mediator release. Used for anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest (ACLS protocol), and severe asthma attacks. Can be given IM, IV, subcutaneous (often preferred), or via endotracheal tube — but never PO. Side effects (why it's an emergency-only drug): arrhythmias, angina, chest pain, hypertension, tachycardia, stroke risk, CNS changes (nervousness, restlessness, tremor).

Epinephrine concentration matters — mixing it up can be fatal. Anaphylaxis dosing uses a 1:1,000 concentration (adult dose 0.2–1 mg; pediatric 0.01 mg/kg); cardiac arrest uses a more dilute 1:10,000 concentration. Giving an IM-concentration dose by the IV route can cause death — always double- and triple-check concentration and route before administering.

Immunosuppressants

  • Decrease immune response — used in transplant patients to reduce rejection of foreign tissue.
  • Increases infection risk.

Antihistamines

  • Reduce allergic symptoms by blocking histamine's mediator activity — don't stop the underlying reaction.

Type II (Cytotoxic) & Type III (Immune Complex) Hypersensitivity

Type II — cytotoxic reaction (memory aid: what happens if a patient gets the wrong blood type). IgG/IgM antibodies bind directly to antigens on a foreign cell's surface, forming an antibody-antigen complex that attacks and lyses the target cell; phagocytes then clean up the debris. Examples: mismatched blood transfusions, autoimmune disease (e.g., type 1 diabetes attacking pancreatic beta cells), and erythroblastosis fetalis (Rh incompatibility — an Rh-negative parent develops anti-Rh antibodies after exposure to an Rh-positive fetus, which then attack red blood cells in a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy).

During a blood transfusion, watch for fever/chills, flushing, changes in heart rate or blood pressure, chest or back pain, restlessness, anxiety, and headache — if any of these appear, stop the transfusion immediately. Take the patient's temperature before starting and 15 minutes after starting, and stay with the patient for the first 15 minutes.

Type III — immune complex reaction (memory aid: rheumatoid arthritis). Antigen-antibody complexes (IgG/IgM) form in circulation and deposit into tissue, where the resulting inflammation — not the complex itself — causes tissue damage. Neutrophils release toxins/mediators that destroy tissue; mast cells add to the inflammation. Triggers include autoimmune attack, infections that resemble self-cells, and inhaled antigens (mold, contaminated plants). Manifestations depend on where complexes deposit: joints (rheumatoid arthritis), kidneys (glomerulonephritis), skin/multiple organs (systemic lupus erythematosus). Types II and III both involve immune complexes, but in Type II the complex attacks the cell directly, while in Type III the complex deposits into tissue and the resulting inflammation does the damage.

Type IV (Delayed) Hypersensitivity

Type IV — delayed hypersensitivity (memory aid: poison ivy, a positive TB skin test, a jellyfish sting, a jewelry reaction, Crohn's disease). Unlike Types I–III, this involves no antibodies — it's mediated by T lymphocytes, and reactions are delayed (taking time for T cells to migrate to the site and become active) rather than immediate.

FeatureDetail
MechanismA small incomplete protein (hapten) attaches to a human protein to form a complete antigen. An antigen-processing cell presents this to a T lymphocyte, which — after a delay — recognizes and attacks. Cytotoxic (CD8) T cells attack directly; helper (CD4) T cells stimulate the immune response and cytokine release. Macrophages then clean up debris.
Common AntigensPlant oils (poison ivy/oak/sumac), adhesives, cosmetics, clothing, nickel alloys (jewelry), tuberculin antigen (the Mantoux/TB skin test), organ transplant/skin graft tissue, and gluten in Crohn's disease.
TimingClinical manifestations generally peak 48–72 hours after antigen exposure — this is why a TB skin test has to be "read" 48–72 hours after placement. The two most common Type IV reactions are contact dermatitis and tuberculin hypersensitivity.

Organ transplant/graft rejection specifically can be a Type I, II, or IV reaction depending on timing and mechanism.

Urinary Tract Infections — Pathophysiology, Risk Factors & Pharmacology

UTIs are more common in females due to a shorter urethra and incomplete bladder emptying. E. coli is the most common causative organism. Bacteriuria (bacteria in the urine) is colonization and does not automatically mean infection; urethritis is infection of the urethra, cystitis is infection of the bladder. Signs/symptoms: can be asymptomatic, or present with urgency, frequency, dysuria, hematuria, cloudy/foul-smelling urine, fever, chills, fatigue. Diagnosed via history/physical, urinalysis, urine culture, and CBC (elevated WBCs).

Risk Factors

  • Catheterization (CAUTI)
  • Female anatomy (shorter urethra, perineal irritation)
  • Older age (up to 50% of nursing home residents have bacteriuria)
  • Pregnancy; spermicide/diaphragm/condom use
  • New sexual activity (~25% of females get a UTI within their first sexually active year)
  • Urinary obstruction/reflux, immobility, incontinence, decreased cognition, hygiene-access barriers

Protective Factors

  • Acidic urine
  • Prostatic secretions (males) and urethral gland secretions (females) — prostatic secretions are more protective
  • Unidirectional urine flow and the bladder's one-way valve
  • A functioning immune system

Males are less likely to get a UTI, but more likely to have recurrent UTIs (bacteria can hide deep in the prostate); BPH also raises urinary retention risk. Atypical presentations: children often present with fever, irritability, poor appetite, vomiting, or diarrhea rather than classic urinary symptoms; elderly patients often present with confusion, anorexia, lethargy, anxiety, or increased falls instead.

DrugUse
Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim)First-line UTI antibiotic — avoid in patients with sulfa allergies.
CiprofloxacinAlternative for patients with sulfa allergies.
NitrofurantoinUsed for recurring lower UTIs.
Phenazopyridine (Pyridium)A urinary analgesic — relieves UTI pain but does not treat the infection itself (the antibiotic does). Harmless but notable side effect: turns urine a reddish-orange color.

Key teaching point for any antibiotic course: finish the entire prescription, even after symptoms improve.

Overactive Bladder & Urinary Incontinence

The bladder is smooth muscle that normally holds about 300–500 mL before signaling the need to urinate. Overactive bladder occurs when bladder muscle contracts involuntarily even at low urine volumes, creating urgency. Causes: neurological disorders (stroke, diabetes), UTIs, menopausal hormonal changes, bladder abnormalities (tumors, stones), or outflow obstruction. Manifestations: sudden urgency, incontinence, frequency (≥8 voids/24h), nocturia (waking ≥2 times/night to void).

Mirabegron is an antispasmodic that selectively stimulates beta-3 adrenergic receptors to relax bladder smooth muscle (PO, 25–50 mg/day). Most serious adverse reaction: hypertension (monitor BP; use cautiously in patients who already have hypertension) — also urinary retention, UTIs, headache.

Type of IncontinenceMechanism
UrgeInvoluntary urine leakage immediately after a sudden urge — no time to reach the toilet. Caused by an overactive detrusor (bladder smooth) muscle; frequency increases with age. Contributing factors: bladder infections, outlet obstruction (enlarged prostate), CNS conditions (Parkinson's, dementia, MS, stroke), diuretics/alcohol.
StressInvoluntary urine loss with increased intra-abdominal pressure (sneezing, coughing, lifting) — from loss of pelvic floor muscle/fascial support (often from childbirth trauma or pelvic surgery), which disrupts the normal bladder-urethra angle.
MixedCombination of stress and urge incontinence — common together, especially in older females.
OverflowThe bladder becomes so full it leaks/overflows — more common in males with an enlarged prostate obstructing outflow.
FunctionalThe urinary system itself works fine, but a physical condition, mobility limitation, cognitive disorder, or lack of toilet access prevents timely toileting.
TransientSudden and reversible — often related to a UTI, constipation, or fecal impaction.

Incontinence is not a normal part of aging, though age-related lower urinary tract changes can contribute. It carries psychosocial impact plus real skin risk — acidic urine causes inflammation/breakdown, and damp, warm, damaged skin becomes a breeding ground for bacterial and fungal infection (genitals, buttocks, inner thighs are highest risk) — good skin care is essential. Treatment combines bladder training and pelvic floor muscle strengthening with anticholinergic medications (e.g., oxybutynin) that block acetylcholine-driven bladder smooth muscle contraction — expect the same anticholinergic side effects seen elsewhere (dry mouth, constipation).

How do tinea infections and candidiasis differ in what organism causes them and where they occur?
Tinea is caused by dermatophytes and affects keratinized tissue — hair, skin, and nails. Candidiasis is caused by the yeast Candida albicans and affects mucous membranes and skin, especially the mouth, vagina, an uncircumcised penis, and moist skin folds (intertriginous areas).
Why can nystatin never be given IV, and what is amphotericin B used for instead?
Nystatin is too toxic to give IV — it's used topically or orally (swish-and-spit/swallow) for superficial candidiasis. Amphotericin B is the agent of choice for systemic mycoses, but it's a high-alert drug: nephrotoxic and can cause cardiac dysrhythmias, so it must be diluted, infused slowly, and monitored with BUN/creatinine and cardiac telemetry.
What side effect should you always monitor for with an azole antifungal like fluconazole, and how should it be timed around a PPI?
Liver toxicity — remember, azoles have an "L" in the name for Liver — monitor AST/ALT and watch for jaundice/abdominal pain. Azoles need an acidic stomach to absorb properly, so separate fluconazole from PPIs or other acid-reducing drugs by at least 2 hours.
Why does a shingles rash never cross the midline, and what's a major long-term complication?
Shingles is a reactivation of the varicella zoster virus, which lies dormant in a single dermatome — so the rash follows that one dermatome's path on only one side of the body (asymmetric, doesn't cross midline). A major complication is postherpetic neuralgia — persistent pain in the same area, affecting about 13% of patients over 60.
How do hospital-acquired and community-acquired MRSA differ in typical cause and treatment?
Hospital-acquired MRSA is linked to invasive procedures (surgery, IV lines, central lines, artificial joints) and treated with IV vancomycin or linezolid. Community-acquired MRSA often starts as a painful boil, spreads person-to-person, and is treated with Bactrim or dicloxacillin.
Recite the ABCDE mnemonic for melanoma screening, and name the three major skin cancer types by malignancy risk.
Asymmetry, Border (uneven/notched), Color (variation), Diameter (>6mm), Evolving (changing over time). The three types: basal cell carcinoma (most common, least malignant), squamous cell carcinoma (second most common, curable if caught early, can metastasize if not), and melanoma (rare but most deadly, spreads both wide and deep).
Name the four pathophysiologic mechanisms of diarrhea and give an example cause for each.
Osmotic (excess osmotically active solutes pull in water — concentrated tube feedings), secretory (a bacterial toxin increases secretion — Vibrio cholerae, Staph aureus), exudative (active bowel inflammation exudes mucus/blood/protein — Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis), and motility disturbance (decreased small-intestine absorption overloads the colon — dumping syndrome, IBS).
Which class of laxative is the only one appropriate for long-term use, and why?
Bulk-forming laxatives (e.g., psyllium/Metamucil) are the only class safe for long-term use — they work like dietary fiber, with no systemic effects, just increasing stool bulk to trigger normal motility. All other classes (emollient, hyperosmotic, saline, stimulant) are for short-term use only, and inappropriate long-term use of any laxative can cause dependence and bowel damage.
Use the B-cell/T-cell memory aid to explain the fundamental difference between Types I-III and Type IV hypersensitivity.
B cells make antiBodies — Types I, II, and III are all antibody-mediated (IgE, IgG, IgM depending on type). T cells provide long-Term, delayed immunity — Type IV is T-cell-mediated with no antibody involvement, and its reaction is delayed (peaking 48-72 hours after exposure) rather than immediate.
A patient is given epinephrine for anaphylaxis. What concentration should be used, and why does this matter so much?
Anaphylaxis uses a 1:1,000 concentration of epinephrine (vs. the more dilute 1:10,000 used for cardiac arrest). Mixing up the concentration and route matters enormously — giving an IM-concentration dose by the IV route can be fatal, so the concentration and route must always be double- and triple-checked before administration.
Why do children and elderly patients with a UTI often present atypically, and what should you watch for in each group?
Neither group reliably presents with classic UTI symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency). Children often present with fever, irritability, poor appetite, vomiting, or diarrhea instead — especially if too young to verbalize discomfort. Elderly patients often present with confusion (the biggest tell), anorexia, lethargy, anxiety, or an increased history of falls.
Differentiate urge, stress, and overflow urinary incontinence by mechanism.
Urge incontinence: an overactive detrusor muscle contracts involuntarily, causing immediate leakage after a sudden urge with no time to reach the toilet. Stress incontinence: increased intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, sneezing, lifting) causes leakage due to lost pelvic floor/fascial support, often from childbirth or pelvic surgery. Overflow incontinence: the bladder becomes so full it leaks/overflows, more common in males with an enlarged prostate obstructing outflow.
