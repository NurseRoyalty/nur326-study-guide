Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new — it's a map of Week 4's four topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. Three of the four pages are really one continuous story about the liver (how it normally works, what happens when a virus inflames it, and what happens when that inflammation becomes permanent scarring); the fourth pairs two organs that share a single duct system. This page shows where each idea is actually taught in full, and where it just comes back as supporting detail. No flashcards or quiz questions here — just the map.

Two Clusters, Not Four Separate Topics

Four pages is easier to hold in your head than six, but they still aren't four unrelated diseases — three of them are one progression, and the fourth is really two organs that fail together.

ClusterTopicsThe Common Thread
The Liver, Start to FinishLiver Anatomy, Function & Jaundice → Viral Hepatitis → Cirrhosis & Liver FailureThese three build on each other in order: normal liver function and how bilirubin/jaundice work, then a viral cause of liver inflammation (reversible), then what happens once that inflammation becomes permanent scarring (irreversible). Every lab value and clinical sign in hepatitis and cirrhosis traces back to one of the liver's five jobs, taught once on the first page.
One Duct System, Two OrgansGallbladder & Pancreatic DisordersGallstones are the thread — a stone can block the common bile duct and cause cholecystitis in the gallbladder, or back up pancreatic enzymes and trigger acute pancreatitis, sometimes both at once. That's why these two organs share one page instead of two.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, work through the liver cluster in order first — each page assumes you already know the one before it — then treat gallbladder/pancreas as its own self-contained unit.

The Four Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1Liver Anatomy, Function & JaundiceThe liver does five jobs (metabolize/store, blood reservoir, blood filter/bilirubin removal, make clotting factors, detoxify drugs) — nearly every finding on the rest of this week's pages is one of those five jobs failing. Jaundice becomes visible around 2–2.5 mg/dL bilirubin, and whether it's conjugated or unconjugated tells you whether the problem is the liver itself or something blocking bile from leaving it.
2Viral Hepatitis (A, B & C)A virus inflames the liver through three predictable phases (prodromal, icteric, recovery); hepatitis A spreads fecal-orally and never goes chronic, while B and C spread through blood/body fluids and go chronic about 10% and 80% of the time, respectively.
3Cirrhosis & Liver FailureOnce inflammation becomes fibrotic scarring, the damage is permanent — alcoholic liver disease is the clearest example, moving through three stages from reversible fatty liver to irreversible cirrhosis. Portal hypertension and hepatic encephalopathy are the two complications that do the most damage beyond the liver itself.
4Gallbladder & Pancreatic DisordersGallstones cause both cholecystitis (gallbladder inflammation that can progress to ischemia and perforation) and most cases of acute pancreatitis (trapped enzymes auto-digest the pancreas). Chronic pancreatitis, usually from alcohol, eventually damages both the digestive-enzyme (exocrine) and insulin-producing (endocrine) sides of the organ.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

Every danger callout across the four pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Viral HepatitisAcetaminophen (Tylenol) is still allowed with active or chronic hepatitis, but the daily limit drops to under 2 grams/day (versus the usual 4 g/day) — avoid it entirely in serious, advanced liver disease.
Cirrhosis & Liver FailureVariceal hemorrhage — a burst esophageal or anorectal varix from portal hypertension — is a true, high-mortality emergency with no drug that reverses the underlying portal hypertension or fibrosis itself.
Gallbladder & Pancreatic DisordersCholecystitis can progress to ischemia, necrosis, and perforation if the inflammatory pressure on the gallbladder wall isn't relieved — this is why it's treated as a surgical priority, not just managed with pain control.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Reversible until it isn't." Hepatitis inflammation, alcoholic fatty liver, and biliary colic are all still reversible stages. Cirrhosis, alcoholic steatohepatitis, and established cholecystitis are not — the whole week is organized around noticing which side of that line a patient is on.
  • "Trace every finding back to a job that stopped happening." The liver's five jobs and the pancreas's enzyme list both work the same way: pick the abnormal lab or symptom, ask which specific job or enzyme is responsible, and the "why" falls out on its own.
  • "One blocked duct causes disease in two directions." A gallstone in the common bile duct backs bile up into the liver (jaundice) and pancreatic enzymes up into the pancreas (pancreatitis) at the same time — it's the same mechanical problem with two different downstream organs.
  • "The safe dose changes when the organ is already hurt." Acetaminophen with hepatitis and vitamin-K-dependent clotting with obstructed bile flow are both reminders that a liver already working overtime can't handle what it normally could.
  • "A normal-sounding lab isn't reassuring on its own." LFT numbers don't track severity, ammonia doesn't diagnose hepatic encephalopathy by itself (level of consciousness does), and amylase/lipase rise only after damage is already underway — the clinical picture matters as much as any single number.
