Osteoporosis, Fractures & Osteoarthritis

This page covers the musculoskeletal bone and joint disorders in the order they were taught. It begins with osteoporosis (bone mass, T-scores and DEXA, risk factors, pathogenesis, hip fractures, and the drug therapies), then fractures in general (causes, classification, healing, and the complications of delayed healing, impaired growth, compartment syndrome, and fat embolism) together with osteomyelitis, the bone infection the instructor calls probably the most common fracture complication. It finishes with osteoarthritis (risk factors, step-by-step pathogenesis, manifestations, and management) and degenerative disc disease, the same process as OA but specific to the spinal column. A self-test deck at the end covers all of it.

Gender & Bone Mass

The lecture starts with why women are affected so much more than men. Several factors affect bone mass: age, gender, race, genetics, reproductive status, calcium levels, and exercise. The lecture looks particularly at age and gender.

Women

  • Experience greater bone loss in the early post-menopausal years; after that period of bone loss ends, the loss continues at a more gradual rate.
  • Also have a lower peak bone density than men.
  • These factors combine so that women reach a fracture threshold (the point where the risk of fracture significantly increases) much earlier than men.
  • Over a lifetime, women lose about 35% of cortical bone and 50% of trabecular bone.
  • Beginning at age 30, the amount of bone resorbed by osteoclasts exceeds the amount formed by osteoblasts, resulting in a steady decrease in bone mass with age.

Men

  • Lose only about two-thirds of the amount women lose over a lifetime, so men lose about a third less than women.
  • The same pattern is seen in men: after age 30 the bone-mass curve starts to go down.
  • Bone mass decreases as a function of age in both males and females, but women lose much more in the immediate post-menopausal period.

The instructor points to a slide graph of bone mass versus age: both curves start to go down after age 30, and women's curve drops steeply in the early post-menopausal years and then continues down at a more gradual rate.

Why We Care: Bone Density, T-Scores & DEXA

We care because low bone density means a greater likelihood of breaking bones, and low bone density is considered osteoporosis. Bone density is measured with a T-score. Osteoporosis is defined as a bone mineral density 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass. The measurement is done with a DEXA scan and reported as T-scores (in standard deviations).

CategoryT-score (DEXA)What it means
Normal−1 or greater (higher than −1) standard deviationsNormal bone density.
OsteopeniaBetween −1 and −2.5 standard deviationsConsidered low bone mass. The stage before osteoporosis, with thinning of the trabecular matrix of the bone.
OsteoporosisLess than −2.5 standard deviationsHoley, porous bones that are weaker.
Severe osteoporosisOsteoporosis (T-score below −2.5) plus a history of a fragility fractureThe diagnosis of "severe" is given when there is a history of a fragility fracture.

What is a fragility fracture?

  • The person fell from standing and broke a bone.
  • It is not a traumatic accident; the bone broke because it is so porous.

Osteopenia vs. Osteoporosis

  • Osteopenia is the stage before osteoporosis, with thinning of the trabecular matrix. Trabecular bone is the porous bone. It is seen with a T-score between −1 and −2.5 on DEXA.
  • Osteoporosis is "holey" (porous) bone, reached at a T-score of −2.5. It is obviously a weaker bone.
  • Osteoporosis is a common yet very serious disease. It is serious because of the risk of fractures, and it is characterized by low bone density and structural deterioration of the bone.
  • It is most common in the hip, vertebrae, and wrist bones, which the lecture describes as trabecular bones.

Characteristics of the Disease

  • Low bone mass.
  • Microarchitectural deterioration of the bone (the lecture shows X-rays/a figure of the changes in bone architecture at a microscopic level).
  • In the slide image, the osteoporotic bone (on the left) shows clear loss of bone, with large gaps and breaks within the architecture of the bone.
  • This increases bone fragility and ultimately leads to weak bones and fractures.

Pathogenesis

Bone is a living tissue. Bone remodeling is the process of resorption and formation. It occurs throughout your lifetime, and about 10% of bone tissue is replaced each year.

Osteoclasts

  • Cause bone resorption (breakdown).
  • Stimulate the production of enzymes that dissolve bone.
  • The instructor's shorthand: osteoclasts "destroy" bone.

Osteoblasts

  • Build up the protein matrix of bone.
  • Osteoblasts build bone.
MEMORY AID (from the instructor): osteoBlasts Build; osteoClasts destroy. The instructor remembers it with the word "build."
In osteoporosis: increased osteoclast activity and decreased osteoblast activity. We cannot make new bone because we do not have the osteoblast function, and we have too much bone resorption from osteoclasts.

Manifestations

Early

  • There are really no manifestations early on.
  • So the approach is risk-factor identification: if someone is high risk, DEXA scans are started earlier.

Later

  • Fractures
  • Pain
  • Loss of height
  • Stooped posture, like kyphosis

Hip Fractures

One of the biggest, most concerning, and most common complications of osteoporosis is the hip fracture.

Epidemiology, Mortality & Outcomes

  • Osteoporotic hip fractures lead to increased risk of mortality: once you have an osteoporotic hip fracture, the risk of death increases by about 2.5 to 4 times.
  • Deaths do not occur from the fracture itself, but from complications after it that are related to immobility: sepsis, skin breakdown, immobility, pneumonia, and blood clots.
  • Much more common in women older than 65, because osteoporosis is much more common in women.
  • The most common location is the proximal third of the femur.
  • In addition to the increased risk of death, independent living is usually lost, and these patients often end up in some kind of care facility such as a nursing home.

Presentation

FindingLecture detail
OnsetElderly patients usually describe the sudden onset of hip pain, either before or after a fall. Which comes first depends on whether it is a fragility fracture or whether they fall and break it or break it and fall.

Tenderness / bruisingThe patients develop tenderness, but there is typically little bruising because the fracture is usually encapsulated. The contrast the instructor gives: a person who breaks an arm skateboarding and the arm breaks open, versus these more encapsulated fractures.

Insufficiency (Fragility) Fractures

  • There may be no obvious history of trauma.
  • The patient may complain of vague knee, groin, or thigh pain.
  • In these cases the bone is so damaged and has lost so much density that it can no longer bear weight, and that is when the fragility fracture develops.
  • Once the fracture occurs there is typically no difference in the presentation; just know that a fragility fracture may be present in this situation.

Pharmacotherapy: Goals & Overview

The goal of pharmacotherapy is to reduce fractures. Pharmaceutical companies test drugs that increase bone mineral density, but an increase in bone density does no good if it does not reduce fractures. That is why, when looking at the effects of these drugs, you want to see how well they reduce fractures.
  • Prevention mainly consists of calcium and vitamin D supplementation.
  • Treatment consists of either promoting bone formation or decreasing bone resorption.
  • The most common drugs (the ones covered) decrease bone resorption.
  • At this time there is only one drug that promotes bone formation. It is a new class of drugs that focuses on the parathyroid. The instructor will not cover it because it is still not commonly used.
  • Drug groups covered: bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), and hormone therapy (which the lecturer says includes calcitonin).

Bisphosphonates: Alendronate (Fosamax)

  • Alendronate is "your first drug" and the first-line drug class for osteoporosis.
  • Reduces fractures by about 50%.
  • Used for both prevention and treatment.
  • Binds permanently to the surface of the bones and inhibits osteoclast activity, so bone breakdown decreases. The true mechanism of how it works is not really well known.
  • Adverse effects: GI effects are common; esophageal ulcerations.
  • It is typically now given only once a week, so patients do not have to think about it every day.
  • The instructor stresses that the low bioavailability is what causes many of the teaching points.
Safety / administration teaching for alendronate: tell patients they do not want to lie down right after taking it; they want to stay sitting up (standing) for a while, because it can cause esophageal ulcerations. Do not take it with calcium because it has low bioavailability (only about 1%), and even a glass of juice or coffee can cut that 1% by about 60%. So tell people to take it with one sip of water in the morning and make sure they stay up standing so it does not cause esophageal ulcerations.

SERMs: Raloxifene (Evista)

SERM = selective estrogen receptor modulator. The one the instructor says you need to know is raloxifene (Evista).

  • Works by mimicking estrogen: it produces estrogen-like effects on bone by binding to estrogen receptors.
  • Reduces bone turnover and increases bone density, but not as well as bisphosphonates.
  • This drug is safer than using estrogen (hormone replacement).
  • Used to prevent and treat postmenopausal osteoporosis only; not everybody will get it. Bisphosphonates are definitely the first-line drugs.
  • In one study it reduced the risk of spinal fractures by 50% but not any other types of fractures, including hip.
  • Side effects: hot flashes and leg cramping, and there is a stroke risk.
  • You must take adequate calcium and vitamin D replacement for SERMs to work.
Raloxifene clot / stroke risk: there is a stroke risk. Discontinue at least 72 hours before a planned procedure, because the drug mimics estrogen and there is an increased risk of clotting. Any prolonged immobilization period or high risk of blood clotting is problematic. "That's why we also say not to smoke or drink." It cannot be used if the patient is pregnant.

Calcitonin

The instructor covers calcitonin "quickly." Note that in this lecture calcitonin is described as part of hormone therapy.

  • Not used for long-term therapy; it is not as effective as SERMs or bisphosphonates for long-term therapy, but you may see it used short term.
  • Inhibits bone removal by osteoclasts.
  • Treatment only, not prevention.
  • May provide an analgesic effect in patients with acute vertebral fractures.
  • Has been shown to reduce spinal fractures only, and the benefits come only after taking it for 5 years.
  • It can also reduce pain in someone who has a hip fracture, which is a unique feature compared to the other drugs: you will not see that with a SERM or a bisphosphonate.
  • Increases spinal bone density, but it is not a first-line drug: it is expensive, and bisphosphonates are much more effective.
  • It is typically given intranasal, so it can cause nasal irritation.

Hormone Replacement Therapy

  • HRT is no longer commonly used to treat osteoporosis because of its risks: blood clots, breast cancer, and other cancers.
  • Remember that post-menopause is related to the drop in estrogen, so estrogen replacement is sometimes used in the post-menopausal period because of other issues.
  • However, estrogen/hormone replacement therapy is not recommended in osteoporosis because those risks are just too high.

What Is a Fracture?

Definition

  • A fracture is any break in the continuity of bone.
  • It occurs when more stress is placed on the bone than the bone is able to absorb.
  • The amount of stress needed to break a bone is relative: weak, brittle bones will not take very much, whereas young, healthy bone can withstand really high forces.

Hip and fragility fractures related to osteoporosis were covered earlier on this page (see Hip Fractures).

Causes of Fractures

CauseLecture Detail
TraumaticExample given: a fall.
IndirectExample given: a massive muscle contraction can break a bone. The instructor notes this would not be very common.
FatigueUsually seen with endurance activity — repeated, prolonged stress. Think marathon runners and stress fractures. (The lecture slide's picture is an example of a fatigue/stress fracture.)
PathologicThe highest-risk population is the elderly. Seen in relation to osteoporosis, bone tumors, or infection — infections that decrease the integrity of the bone.

Classification of Fractures

Open vs. Closed

Open = Compound

  • The bone penetrates the skin.
  • The lecture shows a very extreme example in a picture.
  • Later in the topic: an open (compound) fracture carries a higher osteomyelitis risk than a closed fracture.

Closed = Simple

  • The fracture does not break through the skin.
  • The lecture shows a picture of a closed fracture.

Phases of Bone Healing

The instructor calls this a review from A&P. The three phases are inflammatory, reparative, and remodeling.

Step (in order)What Happens
1. Inflammatory phaseA hematoma sets (forms) at the fracture.
2. Fibrous cartilageFibrous cartilage forms after the hematoma.
3. CallusA callus forms.
4. OssificationThe callus ossifies.
5. RemodelingUltimately the bone remodels.

Why this matters: many fracture complications are related to the healing process. The instructor's examples: maybe the callus did not form, maybe something went wrong with the hematoma, or maybe the callus was not resorbed.

Delayed Healing, Malunion & Non-Union

Delayed Healing (Delayed Union)

What it looks like

  • Usually identified three months to a year after the fracture.
  • Bone pain and tenderness are increasing, even though you would expect the bone should have healed by now.
  • Healing is slow — possibly due to infection or other risk factors such as smoking, malnutrition, and poor circulation.
  • Delayed healing can lead to malunion and non-union.

Malunion vs. Non-Union

Malunion

  • Improper alignment.
  • Occurs with unequal stresses of muscle pull and gravity.
  • Analogy: you did not put the stick back together straight.
  • Common in a person who began weight bearing too soon.

Non-union

  • The fracture has not healed four to six months after the fracture.
  • No healing at all.
  • The instructor says this is really particular to people with diabetes and infection in the area.

Risk Factors for Delayed Union / Malunion / Non-Union

The lecture lists these risk factors for delayed union (delayed healing), malunion (improper alignment — the sticks do not align), and non-union (no healing).

Risk factors named

  • Tobacco use
  • Older age
  • Severe anemia
  • Uncontrolled diabetes
  • Low vitamin D level
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Poor nutrition
  • Infection
  • Complicated breaks

Management: ideally you would address or fix the problem when it is only a delayed union or malunion. This is done by following these people frequently and getting frequent extra x-rays as the bone begins to heal.

Impaired Bone Growth

  • This is a pediatric consideration.
  • It is especially common when there is a fracture through the epiphyseal plate.
  • It can delay future bone growth.
  • The instructor notes you will talk about this more in your pediatric class.

Compartment Syndrome

Another complication from some types of fractures.

Causes / Setups

  • More common with crush injuries.
  • People whose casts are put on too tight.
  • If swelling begins, or bleeding occurs.
  • If the patient has a cast, you must closely evaluate the development of swelling within the confines of the cast and watch it carefully.

Mechanism

  • It results from increased pressure within a limited anatomic space.
  • This causes compression of the internal structures of the limb.
  • Blood vessels are compressed, which leads to decreased circulation and necrosis.
  • Nerves are compressed, which can cause pain, numbness, and really uncomfortable sensations.
  • The result is a tourniquet effect: the edema at the fracture site puts such intense pressure on the soft tissue and circulation that it leads to tissue hypoxia of the muscles and nerves.

Manifestations

FindingLecture Detail
Extreme pain out of proportion to the injuryExample: the patient was stabilized, and then begins to have extreme pain again.
Weak or lost palpable pulsesThe pulses are very weak; you may need to Doppler them.
EdemaA lot of edema. The instructor stresses this is not pitting edema; it is actual edema. It typically happens very quickly.

Treatment

Compartment syndrome is treated with a fasciotomy. They cut into the muscle space and release some of the edema and tissue swelling. The area is packed and left so the edema can ooze out. The instructor notes these can be done right at the bedside, and also in the emergency department if this happens there.

Fat Embolism

Luckily this is pretty rare, but it is potentially a catastrophic event.

What it is

  • A fat molecule in the lung following a long bone fracture, usually associated with a large trauma.
  • It is a type of pulmonary embolism: fat molecules block a blood vessel in the lung.
  • Fat molecules from the bone marrow or traumatized tissue are released into the bloodstream and travel to the lungs.
  • The lecture says there are several theories as to why fat emboli occur (not detailed).

Who gets it

  • The instructor says to be most concerned about who gets this: a long bone fracture.
  • Think the big bones — the legs are the most common.
Presentation: a very acute onset of pulmonary embolism. The patient may be fine one minute and in respiratory arrest the next minute. The instructor calls it very scary.

Classic Triad

SignLecture Detail
HypoxemiaLow O2.
Altered level of consciousnessOr a completely decreased level of consciousness.
Petechial rashThe rash is often the last symptom to occur. It results from occlusion of dermal capillaries by fat globules, leading to extravasation of erythrocytes.

Treatment

  • Treatment is supportive.
  • The embolism is self-limiting: it will eventually be absorbed by the body (it dissolves) if the person can be stabilized and supported sufficiently until then.
  • If it is small enough, a ventilator may sustain the patient.
  • They may need something more advanced, like ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation). The instructor says she has seen people put on ECMO for a fat embolism.

Osteomyelitis

The instructor says probably the most common complication we would see with fractures is osteomyelitis.

Definition & Organism

The word

  • -itis = inflammation
  • osteo = bone
  • myelo = marrow cavity
  • A severe infection of the bone and the local tissue that requires immediate treatment.
  • It can be acute or chronic — a pus-producing infection of the bone.
  • Typically caused by bacteria found on the skin: Staphylococcus aureus.
  • The bacteria get into the bone and soft tissue while the area is trying to heal, get trapped, and begin an infection. The infection causes delayed healing.

Who Is Most Likely to Get It

Risk Group
People with very recent trauma
Open (compound) fractures — a higher risk than closed fractures
People with diabetes
People on hemodialysis
IV drug use
People with splenectomies

Routes of Contamination

Direct

  • Outside bacteria get in through an open wound: an open fracture, a gunshot, a puncture, or surgery.
  • The instructor says direct contamination is kind of uncommon.

Indirect

  • Seen commonly.
  • The person gets an infection in the bloodstream, and it goes to a bone in an area and causes it not to heal.
  • Example from the lecture picture: a patient with a sternotomy who had a bloodstream infection, probably from an IV line, which then got into the sternal area and became an infection.
  • The lecture slide also shows common areas you might see this; it occurs from something like a bacterial bloodstream infection.

Why It Is So Problematic

Chain of events

  • Pressure increases within the bone.
  • This causes local arteries to collapse.
  • Without arterial blood supply, there is a decreased supply of oxygen, nutrients, and immune cells.
  • It is very difficult to get antibiotics to the area.
  • This leads to impaired healing and makes the infection very difficult to treat.

Manifestations

Local

  • Local tenderness, warmth, and redness — which is why you are always monitoring those areas.
  • Infected wound drainage.
  • More restricted movement than expected, or the person had a level of mobility and then lost it.
  • If it is from a bacteremia, the person may have a spontaneous fracture.

Systemic (common)

  • Fever
  • Positive blood culture
  • Increased white blood cells

Treatment

StepLecture Detail
1. CultureObtain a blood culture to see which antibiotics apply.
2. Empiric startIt is common to start with nafcillin and vancomycin.
3. NarrowOnce the specific culture results are back, you typically switch to an antibiotic specific for that type of bacteria.
4. DurationIV for a long time: four to six weeks. If the infection gets under control quickly, they may do two weeks of IV antibiotics and two weeks of oral. Often, though, it depends on severity.
Severe casesBecause it is difficult for blood flow to carry the antibiotic to the site, the instructor has seen very severe cases treated with 24-hour infusions of antibiotics in the hospital.
Surgical / local optionsSometimes the wound is opened again and antibiotics are infused into the actual area. Sometimes they open the patient, wash the area out with antibiotics, close, go back again a week later, and do it again.
Osteomyelitis can be cured and fixed, but it is difficult and requires long-standing treatment — typically weeks of IV antibiotics. The instructor stresses it is something to avoid at all costs.

Synovial Joint Review

Most joint disorders, arthritis being one of them, affect synovial joints. Synovial joints are typically movable.

Part of the synovial jointRole described in lecture
Outer fibrous capsule (joint capsule)The joint capsule connects the bone and the synovial membrane in the lining.
Inner synovial membraneInner layer of the joint; the capsule connects it to the bone (no separate role given).
Articular cartilagePrevents friction between bones; acts like a protective coating.
Synovial fluidLubricates the joint.

Because of the smooth surfaces of the articular cartilage and the lubricating synovial fluid, the bones can come together and move easily. The key point of the review: "unfortunately, sometimes we lose those things."

Terminology: Arthropathy vs. Arthritis

Arthropathy

  • A joint disorder.
  • The general term for something wrong with a joint.

Arthritis

  • When the joint disorder involves inflammation of one or more joints, it becomes arthritis.
  • There are multiple types of arthritis; osteoarthritis is the first one covered, and rheumatoid arthritis follows in the next part of the lecture.

Osteoarthritis (OA): Definition & Statistics

What OA is

  • Osteoarthritis is degeneration of joints caused by aging and stress.
  • The instructor abbreviates it as OA. It occurs with wear and tear on the joints over time and leads to inflammation in the joint.
  • The inflammation is not systemic, just localized to the affected joint(s).
  • It is a chronic disease process.
Statistic / factDetail
DisabilityThe most common cause of disability in the United States; also stated as the leading cause of chronic disability in the United States. OA is the most common form of arthritis.
Why incidence is risingObesity and longer life expectancy. People are living longer, and age is a big risk factor; people being obese is also causing the incidence of OA to increase.
ProjectionBy 2040, about 78 million Americans are likely to be affected.
Sex differenceAffects men before the age of 45 more than women, but it is more common in women after 45.
Expected consequenceWith obesity on the rise, the instructor expects an increase in joint replacements because of osteoarthritis.

Risk Factors

Degenerative changes begin in the articular cartilage as a result of excessive loading of a healthy joint or normal loading of a previously disturbed (injured) joint.

Risk factorWhat the lecture says
Aging"A big one." You begin to lose articular cartilage as you age. This specifically starts over age 40, but the older you are, the more at risk you are.
ObesityStrains the hips and knees by increasing the load, which increases biomechanical stress and leads to breakdown of cartilage. Being only 10 pounds overweight increases the force on the knee by about 30 to 60 pounds a step.
Team sportsA history of, or participation in, team sports, particularly longer participation. The instructor's example: playing soccer one time as a 12-year-old does not mean you are going to get OA. The risk is wear and tear from overuse in sports. Athletes commonly have OA of the knees or hips because of injury during their time playing.
Occupational workAny repetitive work. This lines up with a history of trauma, overuse, and heavy occupational work. Examples named: gardeners, construction workers, anyone who works outside, heavy lifting, factory work.
MisalignmentMisalignment of the pelvis, hip, knee, ankle, or foot. Women have a built-in misalignment with wider hip joints. One leg shorter than the other can alter the weight balance. If one joint causes pain, the person may shift to compensate, which causes misalignment and increases pressure on the other joints.
Trauma / injuryHistory of trauma and overuse. Injury during sports play leads to OA of the knees or hips; a previously injured joint under normal loading can degenerate.

Pathogenesis: Step by Step

The instructor asks "what is the step-by-step here?" and walks through the sequence below. Follow it in order.

StepWhat happens
1. Cartilage wearPressure on the joint slowly wears away the cartilage surface, exposing the subchondral bone.
2. Subchondral cystsThis causes cysts to develop in the subchondral area. The articular cartilage erodes from the cysts going through it.
3. Repair attempt by chondrocytesChondrocytes synthesize a fluid called proteoglycans in an effort to repair the cartilage.
4. SwellingThis excess fluid, which is supposed to be helpful, is actually what causes swelling of the joint.
5. Inflammation feeds degenerationLocalized inflammation can further degrade the cartilage. The cysts move through the remaining cartilage and destroy the rest, causing more inflammation and more degeneration.
6. Osteoblast activationOsteoblasts (the cells that build up bone) are activated and build bone spurs. Bone spurs come from osteoblasts trying to fix what is going on.
7. Further inflammation and thickened fluidThe bone remodeling ultimately leads to further inflammatory responses and synovial fluid thickening, which causes decreased movement.
8. Bone on boneLoss of cartilage narrows the joint space. You lose the synovial fluid (or it becomes thick and "non-purposeful"), you get bone spurs, and you get bone on bone.

Osteophytes (bone spurs): an important hallmark

  • Osteophytes are bone spurs caused by osteoblasts.
  • The instructor calls them an important hallmark of OA. The remodeling is "not appropriate": the body keeps trying to build and repair, but the result is a bone spur.
  • Bone spurs can be seen on x-ray.
  • They can actually break off and get into the synovial cavity and cause a lot of problems.
  • The synovial fluid thickens as cytokines/inflammation occur, leading to even further decreased movement.

The Whole Cycle in One Pass

Exposed bone develops cysts, the cysts move through the cartilage, chondrocytes try to build and repair but cause swelling, inflammation continues to degrade the cartilage, and the body continues to try to remodel the bone but produces bone spurs. The end picture is a narrowed joint space, bone on bone, thickened synovial fluid, and decreased movement.

Management of OA

Goals

  • Prevention is the goal: decrease the risk factors and try to help before the problem becomes too severe.
  • Once OA is here, the aims are to manage pain, maintain mobility, and minimize disability.
  • Reduce and manage pain and reduce swelling.
  • Reach out to physical therapy so patients learn exercises to keep the joints flexible.
No drug prevents progression, prevents damage, or reverses the course of arthritis. The lecture states plainly that there are no pharmacotherapies that do this, so the focus is on pain management and the disability aspect.

Degenerative Disc Disease (DDD)

The instructor takes a moment to talk about degenerative disc disease. It is the same process just described for osteoarthritis, but specific to the spinal column. The goal of treatment is the same as it was for osteoarthritis.

Where, Who, and How Common

TopicDetail
LocationsOccurs in the lumbar or cervical spine. Lumbar means L4, L5, S1. These two regions are the commons.
Typical causeTypically involves lifting or twisting motions. "Nurses are notorious for not protecting their back, so very common."
Doctor visitsBack pain and degenerative disc disease is the second most common reason people visit their doctor.
Work disabilityBack pain is the most common cause of work-related disabilities in people under age 45.
SexAffects women and men equally.
Age changeIntervertebral disc compression occurs with age.

Mechanism: Nerve Impingement

  • The discs lose that "nice bouncy disc area," and then the vertebrae are just on one another.
  • The nerves then become compressed. This impingement on the nerves is what causes pain, decreased movement, and decreased sensation.
  • Depending on where the impingement occurs, you can have pain in one leg, one side of the buttocks, or down one leg to certain toes. Nerve pain is very common with degenerative disc disease.
  • Once you lose the nice, fluffy, healthy disc, it crushes the nerves.

Lumbar vs. Cervical Presentation

Lumbar spine

  • Lower back pain that often radiates down the back of the leg or the buttocks.
  • This is often called sciatica.
  • Worse when sitting, bending, lifting, or twisting.
  • Better (patients typically say) when walking, changing positions, or lying down.
  • May get numbness, tingling, or weakness down the leg.
  • Can cause foot drop.

Cervical spine

  • Pain can radiate down the shoulders and down the arms.
  • Numbness or tingling in the arm or hand.
  • Weakness in the arm or hand.

Herniated and Bulging Discs

  • DDD typically leads to things like a herniated or ruptured disc, and you can see how it would impinge on the nerves.
  • With bulging discs, sometimes elongation of the spine can let the disc return into the appropriate space, but sometimes it does not.
  • There is disc surgery specific to degenerative disc disease that will be covered in med-surg.

Why do women reach the fracture threshold earlier than men, and at what age does resorption start to exceed formation?
Women lose more bone in the early post-menopausal years (then continue to lose at a gradual rate) and have a lower peak bone density than men. Over a lifetime, women lose about 35% of cortical bone and 50% of trabecular bone; men lose only about two-thirds of this amount (about a third less). Beginning at age 30, bone resorbed by osteoclasts exceeds bone formed by osteoblasts, so bone mass steadily decreases with age (in men as well as women).
How is osteoporosis defined, what test measures it, and what are the T-score cut-offs?
Osteoporosis is a bone mineral density 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass, measured with a DEXA scan and reported as a T-score. Normal: −1 or greater. Osteopenia: between −1 and −2.5 (low bone mass). Osteoporosis: less than −2.5.
What is osteopenia, when is osteoporosis called "severe," and what is a fragility fracture?
Osteopenia is the stage before osteoporosis, with thinning of the trabecular matrix (T-score between −1 and −2.5). Severe osteoporosis is diagnosed when there is a history of a fragility fracture: the person fell from standing and broke a bone (no traumatic accident) because the bone is so porous. Osteoporosis is most common in the hip, vertebrae, and wrist bones.
List the risk factors for osteoporosis given in lecture, and say why long-term corticosteroids are a major one.
Aging; female sex (especially post-menopausal); Caucasian or Asian race (Caucasians about 20% likelihood over age 50); history of an adult fracture; genetics/family history (a first-degree relative with a fragility fracture is a very high risk); low body weight; thin, small frame; smoking; alcohol; long-term corticosteroids; no weight-bearing exercise; lack of calcium or vitamin D; eating disorders and gastric bypass (malnutrition); lack of estrogen or testosterone; excessive caffeine. Corticosteroids inhibit osteoblast function (the build-up of bone when there is breakdown).
Describe the pathogenesis of osteoporosis in terms of osteoclasts and osteoblasts.
Bone remodeling (resorption and formation) goes on for life, with about 10% of bone tissue replaced each year. Osteoclasts break bone down (via enzymes that dissolve bone); osteoblasts build the protein matrix (osteoBlasts Build). In osteoporosis there is increased osteoclast activity and decreased osteoblast activity, so new bone cannot be made and there is too much resorption.
What do patients with osteoporosis look like early versus later?
Early there are really no manifestations, so risk-factor identification is used and high-risk patients get DEXA scans earlier. Later: fractures, pain, loss of height, and stooped posture like kyphosis.
What do hip fractures do to mortality and independence, what causes the deaths, and who gets them?
Risk of death increases about 2.5 to 4 times, and independent living is usually lost (often ending in a nursing home or care facility). Deaths come not from the fracture itself but from immobility-related complications: sepsis, skin breakdown, pneumonia, and blood clots. They are much more common in women older than 65, most often at the proximal third of the femur.
How does a hip fracture present, and why is there little bruising?
Sudden onset of hip pain before or after a fall, loss of ability to walk (though a minimally impacted fracture may still bear weight for a while), severe groin pain, and an externally rotated and shortened leg. There is little bruising because the fracture is usually encapsulated. A fragility fracture may have no obvious trauma and only vague knee, groin, or thigh pain.
What is the goal of osteoporosis pharmacotherapy, and what do prevention and the calcium/vitamin D recommendations look like?
The goal is to reduce fractures (an increase in bone density does no good if it does not reduce fractures). Prevention is mainly calcium and vitamin D supplementation; for a true calcium replacement (not calcium carbonate/Tums), elemental calcium is around 1200 to 2000 (no need to memorize doses). Vitamin D increases calcium absorption, and calcium helps with bone sturdiness. Treatment either promotes bone formation or decreases bone resorption; decreasing resorption is the more common approach.
Alendronate (Fosamax): class, benefit, mechanism, administration teaching, and safety concern?
First-line bisphosphonate for prevention and treatment; reduces fractures by about 50%. It binds permanently to bone surfaces and inhibits osteoclast activity. Bioavailability is only about 1% (juice or coffee cuts it by about 60%), so no calcium with it, take it with one sip of water in the morning, typically once a week. Do not lie down right after taking it; stay sitting up/standing, because it can cause esophageal ulcerations (GI effects are common).
Raloxifene (Evista): how does it work, what fractures does it reduce, and what are its side effects and precautions?
A SERM that mimics estrogen (binds estrogen receptors), reduces bone turnover and increases bone density, but not as well as bisphosphonates. In one study it reduced spinal fractures by 50% but not other types, including hip. Side effects: hot flashes, leg cramping, and stroke risk. Needs adequate calcium and vitamin D; stop at least 72 hours before a planned procedure (increased clotting risk because it mimics estrogen); no smoking or drinking; not for pregnancy.
Calcitonin: place in therapy, benefits, route, and side effect?
Placed by the lecturer under hormone therapy. Not first line and not for long-term therapy (may be seen short term); treatment only, not prevention. Inhibits bone removal by osteoclasts. Reduces spinal fractures only, with benefit only after 5 years; may be analgesic in acute vertebral fractures and can reduce pain in a hip fracture (unique). Expensive; bisphosphonates are much more effective. Given intranasal, so it can cause nasal irritation.
Why is hormone replacement therapy not recommended for osteoporosis?
It is no longer commonly used because of its risks (blood clots, breast cancer, and other cancers); those risks are just too high. Estrogen replacement is sometimes used post-menopause for other issues, but it is not recommended in osteoporosis.
How is a fracture defined, and what are the four causes with the instructor's examples?
A fracture is any break in the continuity of bone that occurs when more stress is placed on the bone than it can absorb (relative: weak, brittle bone takes little, young healthy bone withstands high forces). Causes: traumatic (a fall); indirect (a massive muscle contraction, not very common); fatigue (endurance activity with repeated, prolonged stress, e.g., marathon runners and stress fractures); pathologic (the elderly are the highest-risk population; from osteoporosis, bone tumors, or infection that decreases bone integrity).
Distinguish an open fracture from a closed fracture, including their alternate names.
Open = compound: the bone penetrates the skin (and carries a higher osteomyelitis risk). Closed = simple: the fracture does not break through the skin.
Describe transverse, spiral, comminuted, and greenstick fractures as given in lecture.
Transverse: a straight line, usually 90 degrees to the length of the bone; most common in traumatic falls. Spiral: a twisting injury. Comminuted: more than one fracture line and more than two bone fragments (e.g., people who fall on their feet and break their legs or ankles, like a compression fracture; it can also be spiral or seen as an impacted fracture from jumping from heights). Greenstick: an incomplete break where the bone bends; the most common type of break in children.
What does the "PED" mnemonic stand for, and what does each part mean in a fracture?
Pain, edema, deformity. Pain is an inflammatory response that helps splint (you do not want to move it, but it is very painful). Edema acts as a natural splint and always happens. Deformity: the person holds the limb in a position of comfort and splints it. Also seen: loss of function and abnormal mobility.
List the steps of bone healing in order, and say why they matter for complications.
Inflammatory (hematoma sets), then fibrous cartilage, a callus, ossification, and ultimately remodeling (phases: inflammatory, reparative, remodeling). Many complications relate to healing: the callus may not have formed, something may have gone wrong with the hematoma, or the callus may not have been resorbed.
What are the fracture complications listed, how is delayed healing identified, and what is impaired bone growth?
Delayed healing, bone growth impairment, compartment syndrome, and fat emboli (osteomyelitis is then added as probably the most common). Delayed healing is usually identified three months to a year after the fracture: bone pain and increasing tenderness when the bone should have healed by now (possible causes include infection, smoking, malnutrition, and poor circulation). Impaired bone growth is a pediatric consideration, especially with a fracture through the epiphyseal plate; it can delay future bone growth.
Distinguish malunion from non-union, including the timeline for non-union.
Malunion: improper alignment from unequal stresses of muscle pull and gravity ("the stick is not put back together straight"); common if the person began weight bearing too soon. Non-union: the fracture has not healed four to six months after the fracture; no healing, particular to people with diabetes and infection in the area.
List the risk factors for delayed union, malunion, and non-union, and how they are managed.
Tobacco use, older age, severe anemia, uncontrolled diabetes, low vitamin D level, hypothyroidism, poor nutrition, infection, and complicated breaks. Management: ideally fix the problem while it is only a delayed union or malunion, by following these people frequently with frequent extra x-rays as the bone begins to heal.
What causes compartment syndrome, and how does it harm the limb?
More common with crush injuries, casts put on too tight, swelling, or bleeding. It results from increased pressure within a limited anatomic space, compressing the limb's internal structures: blood vessels (decreased circulation and necrosis) and nerves (pain, numbness). It is a tourniquet effect: edema puts intense pressure on soft tissue and circulation, causing tissue hypoxia of muscles and nerves.
What are the signs of compartment syndrome, what is the treatment, and what must you watch with a cast?
Extreme pain out of proportion to the injury (can return after the patient was stabilized), weak or lost palpable pulses (may need a Doppler), and a lot of non-pitting edema that appears very quickly. Treatment is a fasciotomy: cutting into the muscle space to release edema and swelling, leaving the area packed so the edema can ooze out. With a cast, closely evaluate the development of swelling within the confines of the cast.
What is a fat embolism, who gets it, what is the classic triad, and how is it treated?
Rare but potentially catastrophic: fat molecules from bone marrow or traumatized tissue travel to the lungs and block a vessel (a type of pulmonary embolism), following a long bone fracture (big bones, legs most common). It presents like a very acute PE: fine one minute, in respiratory arrest the next. Triad: hypoxemia, altered or decreased level of consciousness, and a petechial rash (often the last symptom; fat globules occlude dermal capillaries causing extravasation of erythrocytes). Treatment is supportive; it is self-limiting and eventually absorbed (ventilator if small enough, or ECMO).
Break down the word osteomyelitis, describe the condition, and say who gets it and by which routes.
Osteo = bone, myelo = marrow cavity, -itis = inflammation: a severe infection of the bone and local tissue requiring immediate treatment; acute or chronic and pus-producing, typically from skin bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus) that get trapped while healing and cause delayed healing. Risk: very recent trauma, open (compound) fractures (higher risk than closed), diabetes, hemodialysis, IV drug use, and splenectomy. Direct route (uncommon): open wound such as an open fracture, gunshot, puncture, or surgery. Indirect route (common): a bloodstream infection travels to a bone, e.g., a sternotomy infected from a bloodstream infection probably from an IV line.
Why is osteomyelitis so hard to treat, and what does it look like clinically?
Pressure rises within the bone and collapses local arteries, so oxygen, nutrients, and immune cells drop and antibiotics struggle to reach the area, impairing healing. Local signs: tenderness, warmth, redness, wound drainage, more restricted movement than expected (or lost mobility), and possibly spontaneous fracture if from a bacteremia. Systemic: fever, positive blood culture, increased white blood cells.
How is osteomyelitis treated, including antibiotics and durations?
Obtain a blood culture; commonly start with nafcillin and vancomycin, then switch to an antibiotic specific for the bacteria once culture results return. IV for a long time: four to six weeks (or, if quickly controlled, two weeks IV plus two weeks oral). Severe cases may need 24-hour infusions in the hospital; sometimes antibiotics are infused into the area, or the area is opened and washed out with antibiotics and repeated a week later. It can be cured but is difficult; avoid it at all costs.
What are the four components of a synovial joint, and how do arthropathy and arthritis differ?
Outer fibrous capsule (connects the bone and the synovial membrane in the lining), inner synovial membrane, articular cartilage (prevents friction between bones, a protective coating), and synovial fluid (lubricates). Arthropathy is a joint disorder; when it involves inflammation of one or more joints, it becomes arthritis.
Define osteoarthritis, and is its inflammation systemic or localized?
Degeneration of joints caused by aging and stress, from wear and tear over time; a chronic process. The inflammation is localized to the joint, not systemic.
What statistics and trends does the lecture give for OA?
Most common cause of disability (leading cause of chronic disability) in the US and the most common form of arthritis; rising because of obesity and longer life expectancy; about 78 million Americans likely affected by 2040. It affects men before 45 more than women, but is more common in women after 45. More joint replacements are expected.
Which joints does OA typically affect, and which are typically spared?
Affected: back (cervical spine or lumbar-sacral), hip, knee, hands, and big toe. Spared: wrists, elbows, and ankles.
How do aging and obesity increase OA risk, and what numbers does the instructor give?
You begin to lose articular cartilage as you age; this specifically starts over age 40, but the older you are, the more at risk. Obesity strains the hips and knees by increasing the load, raising biomechanical stress and breaking down cartilage: being only 10 pounds overweight increases the force on the knee by about 30 to 60 pounds a step.
Besides aging and obesity, name the other OA risk factors, and the two situations that start cartilage degeneration.
Team sports (especially longer participation), repetitive or heavy occupational work (gardeners, construction workers, factory, heavy lifting), misalignment of the pelvis, hip, knee, ankle, or foot (women's wider hips, one leg shorter, compensating for a painful joint), and a history of trauma or overuse. Degenerative changes begin from excessive loading of a healthy joint, or normal loading of a previously disturbed (injured) joint.
Describe the early steps of OA pathogenesis, including what chondrocytes do.
Pressure slowly wears away the cartilage surface, exposing the subchondral bone. Cysts develop in the subchondral area, and the articular cartilage erodes from the cysts going through it. Chondrocytes synthesize a fluid called proteoglycans to try to repair the cartilage, but this excess fluid causes swelling of the joint. Localized inflammation further degrades the cartilage.
What are osteophytes, and what is the end result of OA in the joint?
Osteophytes are bone spurs, an important hallmark of OA, built by activated osteoblasts trying to fix the problem; they can be seen on x-ray and can break off into the synovial cavity and cause problems. The end result: loss of cartilage narrows the joint space, synovial fluid is lost or becomes thick and non-purposeful, bone spurs form, and you have bone on bone with decreased movement.
Describe the OA pain pattern and the other manifestations.
Deep aching joint pain, especially with exertion, relieved with rest; worse in cold weather and better in drier climates. It starts with stiffness that feels better once you get moving. Also crepitus, altered joint use or gait, joint swelling from proteoglycan fluid, limited range of motion, and deformities and tenderness depending on bone spurs, bone on bone, and swelling.
Distinguish Heberden's nodes from Bouchard's nodes.
Both can develop in the hands with OA. Heberden's are the distal ones; Bouchard's are the proximal ones, closer to the hand.
What are the goals of OA management, and what does the lecture say about drugs that change the disease?
Prevention (decrease risk factors before it becomes severe); once present, manage pain, maintain mobility, and minimize disability, with physical therapy exercises for flexibility. No pharmacotherapies prevent progression or damage or reverse the course of arthritis, so the focus is pain management and disability. Other options: joint replacement, artificial joint fluid, devices such as canes and walkers, and lifestyle interventions.
Walk through the OA drug ladder, and what should you review from your pain lecture?
Mild to moderate: acetaminophen (Tylenol), then OTC NSAIDs; capsaicin is a topical from hot chili peppers that numbs the area. Moderate to severe: prescription-strength NSAIDs, possibly colchicine, then tramadol, eventually opioids or steroid injections into the joint. Review NSAIDs and their risks from the Nursing 325 pain lecture.
What is degenerative disc disease, where does it occur, and how does it cause pain?
The same process as OA but specific to the spinal column, most often in the lumbar spine (L4, L5, S1) or cervical spine, typically with lifting or twisting. Discs lose their bounce and vertebrae end up on one another, so nerves are compressed (impinged), causing pain and decreased movement and sensation. Lumbar: sciatica (worse sitting, bending, lifting, twisting; better walking, changing position, lying down), numbness, tingling, weakness, foot drop. Cervical: radiation down the shoulders and arms with numbness, tingling, or weakness in the arm or hand.
