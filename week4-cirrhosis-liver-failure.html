Hepatitis vs. Cirrhosis — Reversible vs. Irreversible

The Key Distinction

  • Hepatitis = inflammation of the liver. That inflammation is reversible.
  • Cirrhosis = an irreversible, inflammatory, fibrotic liver disease — the liver becomes literally scarred (fibrotic). Once this stage is reached, the damage is permanent.
  • Cirrhosis has a high death rate — about 26%.
  • Typically develops slowly over years; the rate of progression depends on the cause and, for toxin-related cirrhosis (like alcohol), the amount of toxin present. Removing the toxin (e.g., stopping drinking) can slow further progression, but it cannot reverse damage that has already occurred.

How Fibrosis Actually Develops

The Mechanism

  • Structural liver injury (from a toxin like alcohol, or a virus like hepatitis) triggers infiltration of white blood cells (leukocytes), which release inflammatory mediators and activate the fibrotic process.
  • Chaotic fibrosis throughout the liver obstructs biliary channels and blood flow — this is what causes both jaundice and, eventually, portal hypertension.
  • Hepatocytes can normally regenerate, but in cirrhosis that regeneration is disrupted by ongoing hypoxia and inflammation. Damaged cells try to regrow in a disorganized way, forming abnormal tissue with poor blood flow — which develops more scar tissue, more hypoxia, and eventually liver failure.

Alcoholic Liver Disease — A Spectrum

Alcoholic cirrhosis is the single most common type of cirrhosis, though it still only accounts for about 25% of all cirrhosis cases overall. It progresses through three stages:

StageWhat's Happening
1. Alcoholic Fatty LiverMild, asymptomatic, and reversible if the person stops drinking. Alcohol is converted to acetaldehyde, which alters hepatocyte function and activates hepatic stellate cells (the primary cell involved in liver fibrosis); this increases fat deposition (lipogenesis) in liver cells.
2. Alcoholic SteatohepatitisA precursor to cirrhosis. Increased hepatic fat storage, inflammation, and hepatocyte degeneration with white blood cell infiltration. This stage is irreversible — anorexia, nausea, jaundice, and edema can appear.
3. Alcoholic CirrhosisToxic effects of alcohol metabolism cause ongoing immunologic problems, inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular damage — resulting in cellular necrosis, fibrosis, and permanent scarring of the liver structure.

Acetaldehyde also inhibits export proteins from the liver, altering vitamin and mineral metabolism and inducing malnutrition — a major reason patients with alcoholic liver disease are often malnourished. Activated Kupffer cells attract neutrophils (more inflammation), gut bacteria translocate and release toxins, and cell-mediated immunity is suppressed.

Manifestations of Late-Stage Cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is often hard to diagnose early — early signs are vague (nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, flatulence, changed bowel habits, weight loss, a possibly palpable liver). It's usually the late manifestations below that get it diagnosed.

FindingWhy It Happens
JaundiceBilirubin build-up — see the Liver Function & Jaundice page.
Peripheral Edema & AscitesThe liver can't produce enough protein/albumin, so colloid oncotic pressure can't hold fluid in the vessels — fluid third-spaces into tissue (edema) and the peritoneal cavity (ascites). Ascites is also driven by portal hypertension (below).
Bleeding TendencyThe liver can't produce clotting factors (low albumin, prolonged PT). Portal hypertension also causes small veins to burst, worsening bleeding risk.
Spider AngiomataVascular skin lesions specific to cirrhosis and late-stage liver failure — small, spider-like clusters of blood vessels visible on the skin.
Endocrine ChangesWomen can develop chronic anovulation/amenorrhea; men can develop hypogonadism, impotence, or infertility. Most common with alcoholic cirrhosis.
Esophageal & Anorectal VaricesDistended veins caused by portal hypertension. If they burst, the patient is actively bleeding with a high risk of mortality.
Hepatic EncephalopathyThe liver can no longer detoxify the blood — see below.
MNEMONIC: Every late-stage cirrhosis finding traces back to one of the liver's five jobs failing (see the Liver Function page) — no albumin/clotting factors → edema/ascites/bleeding; no bilirubin clearance → jaundice; no detox → hepatic encephalopathy; and on top of all of that, portal hypertension adds varices and worsens the ascites.

Portal Hypertension

The Mechanism

  • Normally, blood from the gut, spleen, and pancreas flows freely through the portal vein into the liver. Fibrosis blocks that free flow — resistant portal blood flow backs everything up.
  • Think of it like stuffing paper towels into a pipe — water can still get through, but it backs up and causes problems everywhere below the blockage.
  • Portal hypertension itself is asymptomatic until complications occur: variceal hemorrhage, ascites, translocation of gut bacteria (increasing peritonitis risk), hepatorenal syndrome (liver AND kidney failure together), and even heart failure.
  • Ascites is driven by a combination of low oncotic pressure, systemic hypotension, vascular underfilling, RAAS activation, and plasma volume expansion — all on top of the portal pressure itself.
Variceal hemorrhage: esophageal or anorectal varices that burst cause active, high-mortality-risk bleeding — a true emergency. There is no drug that reverses portal hypertension or fibrosis; management focuses on preventing and treating this and other complications.

Hepatic Encephalopathy

TermDetail
CauseThe liver can no longer clear toxins from the blood. Ammonia — a neurotoxin normally cleared by the liver — crosses the blood-brain barrier and builds up in the brain.
FrequencyOccurs in about 30–45% of cirrhosis patients.
DiagnosisLevel of consciousness — NOT the ammonia lab value — is the primary driver of diagnosis. Ammonia correlates with and is a marker of the toxin build-up, but hepatic encephalopathy is never diagnosed on an ammonia level alone.
PresentationRanges from mild confusion and behavioral changes to coma, unresponsive to pain. Graded by severity.

Acute (Fulminant) Liver Failure vs. Cirrhosis

Cirrhosis

  • Slow, insidious onset over years.
  • Caused by chronic fibrosis (alcohol, hepatitis, NAFLD, etc.).
  • Chronic, progressive liver failure.

Acute (Fulminant) Liver Failure

  • Sudden onset — NOT related to fibrosis/cirrhosis.
  • Most often caused by acetaminophen (Tylenol) overdose; can also follow severe viral hepatitis or metabolic liver disease.
  • Can occur 5 days to 8 weeks after an acetaminophen overdose, or 6–8 weeks after viral hepatitis.
  • Edematous hepatocytes with patchy necrosis and inflammatory infiltrates acutely disrupt liver tissue.

The signs and symptoms of acute liver failure and late-stage cirrhosis look the same clinically — the difference is the underlying cellular timeline (sudden vs. chronic) and cause, not the presentation. The antidote for an acetaminophen overdose is acetylcysteine; if the patient progresses to liver failure regardless, a transplant is ultimately needed. There is no reversal drug for cirrhosis, fibrosis, or acute liver failure itself.

Drug Therapy for Hepatic Encephalopathy

There is no drug for ascites and no drug that reverses cirrhosis — these two drugs exist specifically to treat hepatic encephalopathy.

TermDetail
LactuloseFirst-line. A lactose derivative that isn't digested/absorbed in the stomach or small intestine — it travels to the colon, where bacteria metabolize it into lactic, formic, and acetic acids. These acids create a hyperosmotic, acidic environment that (1) draws water into the colon to promote stooling, and (2) converts ammonia into ammonium, which is water-soluble and trapped in the intestine (can't be reabsorbed) — reducing blood ammonia. Given PO, NG, or rectally; titrated to 2–3 soft stools/day or by ammonia level, but only given for actual signs/symptoms of encephalopathy, not a lab number alone. Monitor potassium — hypokalemia increases renal ammonia production.
RifaximinSecond-line, used if lactulose isn't working. Originally a GI-infection antibiotic; works by inhibiting bacterial RNA synthesis (binds bacterial DNA), which decreases the ammonia-producing gut bacteria. Can be used preventively. Ammonia levels are not monitored with this drug. Associated with an increased risk of C. diff infection — monitor for it.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the key difference between hepatitis and cirrhosis?
Hepatitis is inflammation — reversible. Cirrhosis is fibrotic scarring — irreversible.
What is the most common single cause of cirrhosis, and what percentage of all cirrhosis does it account for?
Alcohol — but it still only accounts for about 25% of all cirrhosis cases.
Name the three stages of alcoholic liver disease, in order, and which stage is the last reversible one.
Alcoholic fatty liver (reversible) → alcoholic steatohepatitis (irreversible) → alcoholic cirrhosis. Fatty liver is the last reversible stage.
Why does cirrhosis cause peripheral edema and ascites?
The liver can't make enough albumin/protein to maintain colloid oncotic pressure, so fluid leaks (third-spaces) out of the vessels — worsened further by portal hypertension.
What causes portal hypertension?
Liver fibrosis blocks the free flow of portal blood, causing resistant/backed-up portal blood flow — like paper towels stuffed in a pipe.
Name three complications of portal hypertension.
Any three of: variceal hemorrhage, ascites, translocation of gut bacteria/peritonitis risk, hepatorenal syndrome, heart failure.
What causes hepatic encephalopathy, and what is it primarily diagnosed by?
Ammonia (a neurotoxin the liver can no longer clear) building up in the brain. It's diagnosed primarily by LEVEL OF CONSCIOUSNESS, never by the ammonia lab value alone.
What is the most common cause of acute (fulminant) liver failure?
Acetaminophen (Tylenol) overdose.
How do acute liver failure and cirrhosis differ, even though they can look the same clinically?
Acute liver failure has a sudden onset and is NOT caused by fibrosis; cirrhosis develops slowly over years from chronic fibrosis.
What is lactulose's dual mechanism for treating hepatic encephalopathy?
It creates a hyperosmotic, acidic colonic environment that draws water in (promotes stooling) AND converts ammonia to ammonium, which is trapped in the gut and excreted rather than reabsorbed.
What electrolyte should be monitored with lactulose, and why?
Potassium — hypokalemia increases renal ammonia production.
When is rifaximin used, and what infection risk does it carry?
Second-line, when lactulose isn't working — it carries an increased risk of C. diff infection.
