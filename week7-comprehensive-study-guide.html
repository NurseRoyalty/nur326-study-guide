Part 1A — Cancer Fundamentals

The cancer material runs in this order: cancer in general (this part), then the specific cancers you are responsible for, then chemotherapies; this part covers what cancer is, how cancer cells differ from normal cells, benign vs. malignant (starred), the cell cycle, grading, TNM, and staging.

What Is Cancer?

Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by cellular transformation (example: a genetic mutation in a cell), uncontrolled cellular growth, and possible invasion into other organs with metastasis to other tissues or organs. The instructor stresses that it is very important to understand what makes normal cells and cancer cells so different.

Cancer cells usually lack

  • Cellular differentiation: not as specific as normal body cells.
  • Cellular checkpoints: may not have the checkpoint that recognizes DNA errors.
  • The ability to undergo apoptosis: the cell self-destructing when it recognizes it is not doing what it should.
  • Response to signals from other cells (e.g., "stop invading my space").
  • Result: they continue to proliferate.

The Cell Growth Cycle

Normal cells divide in a controlled, organized fashion regulated through the cell growth cycle. Growth characteristics of normal and neoplastic cells are similar; both pass through distinct phases. The time to go through the cycle is the generation time, which ranges from hours to days depending on the tumor.

PhaseWhat the lecture says
G0Cell at rest, not actively in the cycle; most human cells in the body are here right now. Seen in the normal cycle but not the cancer cycle on the slide. Cancer cells in G0 are not susceptible to the toxic effects of cell-cycle-specific drugs.
G1Cells enter the cycle and enzymes for DNA synthesis are produced; the first post-mitosis phase.
S"Synthesis of structure": structures move to opposite poles in preparation for division into two cells; the 46 chromosomes reorganize into two sets of 23 pairs at opposite poles; two nuclear membranes develop around the two sets.
G2Cells prepare to divide; RNA and specialized proteins are created.
MMitosis/division is completed; two daughter cells are created.

Why it matters for chemotherapy

  • Chemo agents can act on specific phases; certain meds are specific to certain parts of the cycle.
  • The instructor cares about this because it helps you understand chemo agents.
  • Cancer cells in G0 are not susceptible to cell-cycle-specific drugs.

Cancer cycle vs. normal

  • Cancer cells do not rest or go through restriction checkpoints; they cycle constantly and proliferate without control.
  • With no restriction point, DNA repairs do not often occur and the cell continuously mutates, hence the many "mistakes" in cancer cells.

Part 1B — Metastasis & Cancer Etiology

This part defines metastasis and the routes of spread, then moves to why people get cancer: risk factors, tumor suppressor genes vs. oncogenes, carcinogens vs. promoters.

What Metastasis Means

Malignant cancer cells are invasive, penetrative, and destructive to surrounding tissues, and generally metastasize from a primary site through the lymph nodes or the blood. To establish in a target organ the tumor must develop its own blood vessels and connect to the pre-existing blood supply, because tumors need oxygen and nutrients. How fast a secondary tumor grows depends on its angiogenesis rate (is it developing vasculature to get oxygen and nutrients?).

Primary tumor

  • The site of origin, where the original cellular division problem started.

Secondary tumor

  • Generally not the site of origin; results from seeding of malignant cells traveling to distant sites (due to metastasis).
  • "METs" = a metastasis or secondary tumor.
  • Under the microscope the cells look like the primary site's cells (example: breast cancer traveling to bone; a bone biopsy shows cells like the primary breast tumor).

Genetic Mechanisms: Tumor Suppressor Genes vs. Proto-Oncogenes / Oncogenes

The body has signaling systems and pathways that try to stop cancer cells from proliferating. Two major classes of cancer genes/genetic mechanisms. Car analogy: a cell is a car, and there must be ways to control how fast it grows.

Gene typeAnalogyNormal jobWhat goes wrong
Tumor suppressor geneBrake pedalRestrains cell growth; keeps the cell from dividing too quicklyDefective, loses ability to inhibit growth, so division gets out of control; one way cancer develops is lacking this gene
Proto-oncogeneGas pedalStimulates and regulates the cell's movement through the cycle; helps the cell grow and divide normallyWhen mutated, stimulates constantly; the cell is never in the rest cycle
OncogeneGas pedal stuck to the floor ("stuck in the drive")The mutated, always-on formCells constantly reproducing

P53: a specific tumor suppressor gene that controls cellular apoptosis (natural cell death), which should lead to the death of cells with damaged DNA.

Carcinogens

Why do these genes get messed up? "I wish we knew," but carcinogens are one cause. Carcinogens cause cancer by altering a cell's DNA, so they can alter those genetic mechanisms. They often require prolonged exposure over many years; they are demonstrated to cause cancer (causation, not just association/correlation). Damage is cumulative: more exposure makes further mutation of initiated cells more likely.

CategoryExamplesExplanation
KnownTobacco, asbestos, alcohol, some viruses such as HPV and HIV"Definitely" cause cancer; shown in humans
ProbableNight shift workWomen who worked night shift at some point in the past 10 years have a 60% higher risk of breast cancer ("who knows" why)
PossibleEngine exhaustInsufficient evidence; "in some yes and in some no"

Promoters & Promoter vs. Carcinogen

Promoters are agents that help mutated genes proliferate ("sometimes the line gets a little messy"). Examples: diet (a high-fat diet can stimulate further proliferation of a cancer cell), alcoholic beverages, tobacco, and hormones ("big promoters").

Hormone example (estrogen exposure): more menstrual cycles means more estrogen exposure, which can raise cancer risk. Higher-risk women: first period at a young age, very late first pregnancy, did not breastfeed or only a few months. It is estimated that in developed countries breast cancer incidence could be cut by half if breastfeeding time increased; setting the argument aside, it is an exposure issue.

Carcinogen

  • Alters the cell's DNA; can cause cells to mutate and change inappropriately.

Promoter

  • Helps already-mutated/damaged cells proliferate (it is not the alcohol causing the DNA change, but it can proliferate already-damaged cells).

Things can be both: estrogen therapy can alter cell DNA (carcinogen) and also help mutated genes proliferate; tobacco does both. Exam expectation: she will not ask for an example of a promoter, but wants you to know the different definitions, so that reading a study you can tell whether something is associated with promoting development or alters cell DNA.

Part 2A — Lung Cancer

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States and in Kentucky; this part covers late diagnosis and survival, disparities, etiology (smoking, radon), pathogenesis, types, and symptoms.

Context for the Specific-Cancers Section

  • Historically these four (lung, breast, cervical, colorectal) had the highest prevalence, but with advancement they are seen less now, especially cervical cancer (still mentioned, with a little about the HPV vaccine).
  • The highest-prevalence cancer is non-melanoma skin cancer, covered in NUR 325 (the instructor clarifies she means non-melanoma skin cancer).

Late Diagnosis & Survival

Most patients do not present until the cancer is advanced or metastatic. Exact percentages vary by study and region, but it is "pretty well documented" that most lung cancers are diagnosed late, often after spread to distant sites. Reasons: lack of symptoms early and limited screening uptake in communities. Early diagnosis and detection significantly improve survival.

Etiology (Causes)

Cigarette smoking is the leading cause, "to no one's surprise." 80 to 90% of lung cancer patients are current or former smokers. Risk rises with total cigarettes smoked, expressed as a pack-year (example: a 60-year-old who smokes 2 packs/day since age 20 has an 80-pack-year history; calculators exist). Cigarette smoke is both a promoter and a known carcinogen: it causes cancer and promotes proliferation of mutated genes.

E-cigarettes/vaping: smoking rates are falling but vape use is rising; "not good for you." No good studies show increased lung cancer risk; some data suggest slightly less carcinogenic than cigarettes, but "that data is not great right now." The instructor gets many questions and does not have the answers; it is being actively studied.

Breast Cancer Risk Factors

Lists come from the CDC and the American Cancer Society.

Non-modifiable

  • Age: older than 50.
  • Reproductive history: periods before age 12 and menopause after age 55 (longer span means longer hormone exposure).
  • Obesity: increased estrogen and fat deposits; listed under both (the instructor apologizes: you cannot really control estrogen and fat storage, but she considers it more modifiable).
  • Dense breast tissue: larger breasts or more dense tissue.
  • Family history of breast or ovarian cancer: a big red flag for prevention and screening.
  • Chest radiation for any reason (similar to lung cancer).
  • DES and BRCA1/BRCA2 (below).

Modifiable

  • Overweight/obesity after menopause: much higher risk than healthy weight.
  • Hormone replacement therapy: historically closely linked; estrogen and progesterone taken during menopause can raise risk if taken for more than five years.
  • Certain hormonal oral contraceptives (not all birth control is hormonal, even pills).
  • Pregnancy/breastfeeding history: first pregnancy after 20, not breastfeeding, or never a full-term pregnancy (all about hormone exposure; she does not like it on this list, but "you have to take what you can get").
  • Alcohol: even one drink increases risk.
  • Night shift work (specifically women and breast cancer; assumed hormones out of whack long term), smoking, and other carcinogen exposures.

Instructor caution: HRT and oral contraceptives should not be demonized or villainized; they do a lot of good for those who need them; the link is just something to think about.

DES (Diethylstilbestrol) & BRCA1/BRCA2

DES

  • Given to some pregnant women in the US between 1940 and 1971 to prevent miscarriage.
  • Women who took it have a higher breast cancer risk.
  • Still matters: those women would be in their late 60s, 70s, and 80s, or the people who were pregnant in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, so keep an eye on it.
  • DES is also linked to cervical cancer.

BRCA1 and BRCA2

  • Genes; mutations in the genetic code carry the risk (a known genetic link; other genes also connect, but these are most common and well studied).
  • We all have these genes, but can test for mutations.
  • Raised risk of breast, ovarian, colon, pancreatic cancer; in males, prostate cancer.

Genetic counseling, subtypes, prevention: many cancers now get genetic counseling, breast cancer included. There are many tags and markers (triple negative was named); the instructor does not go into types, just know they exist. In oncology you must spend time in the literature to learn which cancers/treatments go with which types (radiation vs. chemo); covered more in med-surg. With family history, providers often test for BRCA mutations early; women with mutations often get a preventative mastectomy or oophorectomy (removal of the ovaries so you do not get ovarian cancer), which is pretty common as genetic counseling increases.

Cervical Cancer

  • Much more prevalent in the 80s, 90s, and early 2000s; prevalence has really decreased because of Pap smear screening and the HPV vaccine; it can essentially be prevented with the vaccine and frequent screening.
  • Like skin cancer, Pap smears detect early cancer mutations so they are found sooner.
  • The HPV vaccine prevents HPV, a huge risk for cervical cancer. Almost 100% of cervical cancer patients test positive for HPV.

Polyps

Screening often looks for polyps: non-cancerous growths that can develop into colorectal cancer. Usually found on routine screening colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy; usually asymptomatic (some may present with rectal bleeding, bowel habit changes, or abdominal pain), so often picked up only by screening. You may see the polyp type on a report.

Adenomatous polyps

  • Most common type.
  • Higher potential to develop into cancer.

Hyperplastic polyps

  • Less common.
  • Typically do not turn into cancer.

Colorectal Symptoms & Screening

Symptoms are nonspecific: changes in bowel habits; blood in the stool; diarrhea; constipation; feeling the bowel does not empty all the way (usually more of a rectal cancer symptom; diarrhea and constipation can be colon or rectal); abdominal pain, aches, or cramping that does not go away; unexplained weight loss. Practical message: as long as you are peeing, pooping, eating, and drinking you are probably okay, but significant changes plus other symptoms need checking; the first thing is probably a colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy.

Part 3A — Chemotherapy Principles & Toxicities

An overview of what chemotherapy is, the two drug groups, what affects success, general principles, the common toxicities (including stomatitis, myelosuppression, and the nadir), extravasation, and contraindications; the instructor says this field changes quickly and the course covers only the basics.

Anti-Neoplastic Drugs & Cytotoxic Chemotherapy

  • Anti-neoplastic drugs: general term for drugs used to treat cancer; chemotherapy agents that inhibit the growth and spread of malignant cells.
  • Cytotoxic chemotherapy: uses cytotoxic drugs (able to kill cells) to kill or inhibit cancer cells, particularly targeting rapidly dividing cells (this idea drives most of the rest).
  • Ongoing research and constant change. For those going into oncology (e.g., Markey or another cancer center): really know the drugs you give and the cancers they treat, take notes, and stay aware. This course gives a general overview; the instructor explains the drug matrix and what you are expected to know.

Two Main Groups of Chemotherapy Drugs

Cell cycle nonspecific

  • Kill cancer cells regardless of cell-cycle phase.

Cell cycle specific

  • Work only during certain phases (mitosis or DNA synthesis).

General Principles of All Chemotherapy Drugs

  • No one drug is effective against all cancers.
  • Low therapeutic index: a fine line between therapeutic and toxic effects; clinicians monitor cancer spread (e.g., PET scans) against the adverse/toxic effects.
  • Toxicity comes from affecting rapidly dividing cells: kills harmful cancer cells but also affects normal cells.
  • Combination therapy usually works better than a single drug (different mechanisms of different cytotoxic meds are maximized with multiples); some single-drug efficacy exists depending on cancer type.

Myelosuppression & the Nadir

  • Myelosuppression = bone marrow suppression/depression; typically from chemotherapy but can also be caused by radiation.
  • Reduced production of WBCs, RBCs, and platelets: neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia; very high infection risk from the leukopenia (usually neutropenia).
  • Neutrophils are critical in fighting bacterial (and other) infections; depletion makes patients very vulnerable, who are also anemic and thrombocytopenic.
  • Prophylactic antibiotics: often recommended (more in med-surg). Levofloxacin is very common, recommended in high-risk patients with prolonged neutropenia, defined as ANC less than 500 for greater than 7 days.

The nadir is the lowest point of WBCs, RBCs, and platelets, when patients are at highest risk. The instructor highlights it and gives the timing more than one way, recorded as stated:

The nadir is about 7 to 14 days after chemotherapy.

The nadir is the time of highest risk for infection, anemia, and bleeding (the nadir is the lowest point of white cells, red cells, and platelets). A very important teaching point and a point of awareness for nursing interventions.

Extravasation & Central Access

Extravasation is a very serious complication of IV chemotherapy: drugs leak into the surrounding tissues during IV administration and can cause severe tissue damage. Treatment protocols are well established; because of this risk, chemo is generally given through a portacath or other deep tunneled line (many chemo drugs are IV).

Cell-Cycle Specific vs. Nonspecific Drugs

The big differentiator is whether the drug affects cells resting in G0.

Cell cycle phase specific

  • Work only on a specific phase: vinca alkaloids (vincristine) only in mitosis; methotrexate only in S phase.
  • Cannot interrupt cells in G0 (the resting phase where many cells sit); do not kill resting cells.
  • Must remain in the blood for extended periods: prolonged infusions or small doses at short intervals over extended times.

Cell cycle phase nonspecific

  • Cell position in the cycle does not matter; the drugs are just toxic.
  • Affect cells in G0; do kill resting cells.
  • Disadvantage: less effective at injuring just the proliferating cells.
  • Examples: cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin.

Combination therapy: generally a cell-cycle-specific agent plus a nonspecific agent; make sure you understand that one includes G0 cells and one does not. You do not need to memorize the cell-cycle slide, but know vinca alkaloids are cell cycle phase specific.

Cytotoxic Agents in General

  • All work by disrupting DNA synthesis; the largest chemotherapy class; act directly on cancer cells to cause death; target high growth fraction cells (reproduce very rapidly).
  • All commonly affect hair follicles and cause nausea, vomiting, and stomatitis; myelosuppression is also among the usual toxicities (named with doxorubicin).
Handling cytotoxic agents is dangerous. Strict protocols apply and you must be certified to deliver cancer drugs. Many are vesicants. Part 4A — Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is low bone density with structural deterioration of the bone, and it matters because of fracture risk; this part covers gender and bone mass, T-scores and DEXA, risk factors, pathogenesis, hip fractures, and the drug therapy (calcium/vitamin D, alendronate, raloxifene, calcitonin, and why HRT is not recommended).

Gender & Bone Mass

Factors affecting bone mass: age, gender, race, genetics, reproductive status, calcium levels, and exercise (the lecture focuses on age and gender). Bone mass falls with age in both sexes, and both curves start down after age 30.

Women

  • Greater bone loss in the early post-menopausal years, then a more gradual ongoing loss
  • Lower peak bone density than men
  • Together these reach the fracture threshold (risk of fracture significantly increases) much earlier than men
  • Lifetime loss: about 35% of cortical bone and 50% of trabecular bone
  • From age 30, bone resorbed by osteoclasts exceeds bone formed by osteoblasts, so bone mass steadily decreases

Men

  • Lose only about two-thirds of what women lose (about a third less)
  • Same pattern: curve goes down after 30
  • Women lose much more in the immediate post-menopausal period

T-Scores & DEXA

Low bone density means more likelihood of breaking bones, and low bone density is considered osteoporosis. Osteoporosis is defined as bone mineral density 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass, measured with a DEXA scan and reported as T-scores.

CategoryT-score (DEXA)Meaning
Normal−1 or greaterNormal bone density
OsteopeniaBetween −1 and −2.5Low bone mass; stage before osteoporosis; thinning of the trabecular matrix (trabecular = the porous bone)
OsteoporosisLess than −2.5"Holey," porous, weaker bone
Severe osteoporosisBelow −2.5 plus a history of a fragility fracture"Severe" is diagnosed when there is a fragility fracture history

Fragility fracture: the person fell from standing and broke a bone — not a traumatic accident; the bone broke because it is so porous. Osteoporosis is common yet very serious because of fracture risk; it is characterized by low bone density and structural (microarchitectural) deterioration, with large gaps and breaks in bone architecture on imaging, increasing fragility. Most common in the hip, vertebrae, and wrist bones (described as trabecular bones).

The goal of pharmacotherapy is to reduce fractures. Companies test drugs that increase bone mineral density, but a density increase does no good if it does not reduce fractures — look at how well a drug reduces fractures.

Delayed Healing, Malunion & Non-Union

  • Delayed healing: usually identified three months to a year after the fracture; bone pain and tenderness increasing when it should have healed; slow healing possibly from infection or smoking, malnutrition, poor circulation.
  • Malunion: improper alignment from unequal stresses of muscle pull and gravity ("did not put the stick back together straight"); common if weight bearing began too soon.
  • Non-union: not healed four to six months after the fracture; no healing at all; particular to people with diabetes and infection in the area.
  • Risk factors (delayed union, malunion, non-union): tobacco use, older age, severe anemia, uncontrolled diabetes, low vitamin D, hypothyroidism, poor nutrition, infection, complicated breaks.
  • Management: ideally fix/address it at the delayed union or malunion stage — follow these patients frequently with frequent extra x-rays as the bone heals.
  • Impaired bone growth: a pediatric consideration, especially a fracture through the epiphyseal plate; can delay future growth (covered more in pediatrics).

Compartment Syndrome

  • Setups: more common with crush injuries; casts put on too tight; swelling or bleeding begins. With a cast, closely evaluate swelling developing within the cast.
  • Mechanism: increased pressure within a limited anatomic space compresses internal structures — blood vessels (decreased circulation, necrosis) and nerves (pain, numbness). A tourniquet effect: edema at the fracture puts intense pressure on soft tissue and circulation, causing tissue hypoxia of muscles and nerves.

Part 4C — Osteoarthritis & Degenerative Disc Disease

Most joint disorders (arthritis included) affect synovial joints; this part reviews the synovial joint, then covers osteoarthritis (who, which joints, step-by-step pathogenesis, manifestations, management) and degenerative disc disease, which is the same process as OA but specific to the spinal column.

Synovial Joint Review & Terminology

  • Synovial joints are typically movable. Parts: outer fibrous (joint) capsule connecting the bone and the synovial membrane; inner synovial membrane (lining); articular cartilage (prevents friction between bones, a protective coating); synovial fluid (lubricates).
  • Smooth cartilage plus lubricating fluid let bones come together and move easily — "unfortunately, sometimes we lose those things."
  • Arthropathy = a joint disorder. When it involves inflammation of one or more joints it becomes arthritis. Multiple types; OA first, RA next.

Osteoarthritis (OA): Definition & Statistics

OA is degeneration of joints caused by aging and stress — wear and tear over time leading to inflammation that is not systemic, just localized to the affected joint(s). It is a chronic disease process.

Part 5A — Rheumatoid Arthritis

RA differs from OA pathophysiologically, pharmacologically, and in treatment — you must know the distinct differences; the biggest is that RA is a systemic autoimmune disease rather than a localized one. This part covers etiology, pathophysiology (immune complexes, RF, pannus), manifestations, systemic involvement, and drug management (NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, DMARDs, biologics).

What RA Is, Etiology & Risk Factors

  • Systemic, autoimmune — the body attacks the synovial tissue in the joints; an inflammatory disease of the synovium that destroys the joint area.
  • Classified as a type 3 hypersensitivity (go back to the hypersensitivity lecture if unfamiliar).
  • Cause not well understood despite intensive research; thought to involve an environmental factor and a genetic factor — a genetic link and a triggering event. You can be genetically predisposed without developing it. Environmental factors: an infection, an injury, or hormonal/reproductive factors.
Risk factorLecture detail
Genetic linkSignificant, paired with a triggering event; family history is pretty significant
AgeMost diagnoses between 40 and 60 (OA is the age-related one; RA is middle age); some are diagnosed even earlier. This is a very active time of life (mid-to-late career, children growing up), so it interferes with lifestyle
SexWomen have a 2.5 times higher risk than men
Tobacco useLinked to RA; possibly a trigger or involves infectious agents from the trigger

Pathophysiology & Diagnosis

  • Immune (autoimmune) complexes deposit in the synovial tissue and immune cells attack it (we do not know why they target the synovium).
  • Activated cells are typically B cells (lymphocytes) and macrophages, which secrete cytokines that attract more white blood cells.
  • They produce RF (rheumatoid factor) — a special antibody against our own IgG antibodies.
  • During phagocytosis, large quantities of lysosomal enzymes are released and further damage surrounding tissue. The process is chronic, persistent, and destructive; the intense inflammation destroys cartilage.
  • As destruction progresses, scar tissue develops — in RA joints called pannus: very vascularized (all scar tissue is, trying to bring in nutrients to heal); made of many inflammatory cells (T cells, macrophages, others); because it contains active immune and inflammatory cells it is very destructive to surrounding tissue. End result: loss of synovial tissue and fluid — bone on bone with inflammation.

Manifestations

  • Early: not a ton of obvious symptoms (maybe joint pain/discomfort); vague systemic symptoms — fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, generalized stiffness.
  • Later: symmetrical joint involvement — heat, swelling, tenderness, stiffness. Stiffness starts in the morning and lasts longer than an hour. Swelling feels spongy, soft, and warm (OA swelling is hard — like a sprained, swollen ankle). OA is wear and tear on a unilateral joint; RA is symmetrical.
  • Subluxation (with muscle atrophy and tendon destruction): bone displaced from its normal position, partially losing contact with the articulating surface (a dislocation is a complete loss of contact; subluxation is "just not aligned perfectly"). Decreases ROM, very painful, cannot be fixed because tissue is eroded away. Very common in the hands, also the knees.
  • Deformity: no Heberden's or Bouchard's nodes (those are OA). Instead a swan deformity — not bony growths but actual curving of the joint from inflammation, in more advanced stages along with subluxations.

RA can affect any and all body systems depending on severity; on physical assessment assess all systems, particularly the heart. Part 5B — Gout

Gout is a disruption in uric acid metabolism with urate salt deposits in and around joints; this part covers gout versus gouty arthritis, purine and hyperuricemia, risk factors, the phases, manifestations, complications, and the drugs (NSAIDs first; allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid go on the drug matrix).

What Gout Is

Gout

  • The metabolic problem itself
  • A disruption in uric acid metabolism
  • Urate salts deposited in tissues, generally in and around joints

Gouty arthritis

  • How gout affects the joint — inflammation of the joint
  • Acute and very painful
  • Uric acid crystal deposits in the joint

"The disease of kings": traditionally from foods associated with the wealthy — smoked meats, cheeses, red wine, beer. Many old rich kings had it; now many different people get it.

Purine, Uric Acid & Hyperuricemia

  • Crystals form from the breakdown of purine — a nitrogen-containing compound, the parent compound of nitrogenous waste. Purine breaks down into uric acid, excreted in urine by the kidneys. Normally uric acid dissolves in blood and is excreted with no problem; in gout there is an inability to process it and hyperuricemia develops.
  • High-purine foods/drinks named: alcohol (beer, red wine), organ meat (liver, kidney, brain), red meat, seafood such as scallops.
  • Hyperuricemia is usually from overproduction of uric acid (allopurinol's target) and underexcretion by the kidneys (probenecid inhibits reabsorption).

  • Pain is the hallmark sign; can be excruciating. Burning pain, redness, swelling, warmth of the joint; fever; symptoms typically last days to weeks; difficult to walk; attacks can recur.
  • Most common joint: the big toe (large metatarsophalangeal/MTP joint) — about 50% of people with gout. Can occur in other joints, but feet and big toe are very common.
  • Pain can be so intense patients cannot put on socks and their feet cannot touch the bedsheets (the instructor saw a patient whose toe could not even have one thin hospital sheet over it).
  • Nursing approach: treat symptoms; teach patients to stop drinking and stop the high-purine diet, hoping they can control it.
  • Tophi: large, hard nodules of uric acid crystal deposits in soft tissue, below the skin around joints; can cause local inflammatory response; may drain chalky material if drained.
  • Kidney stones: gout patients are at high risk (gout was also mentioned in the renal disorders lecture).

Part 5C — Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

Lupus (SLE) is an abnormal immune reaction against the body's own cells — a complex autoimmune disease in which autoantibodies form immune complexes that land in almost any organ; this part covers its mechanism, risk factors, manifestations, course, flares, drugs, and a comparison with RA.

What Lupus Is & How It Damages the Body

  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is the main type (what people mean by "lupus"; the terms are interchangeable). A type affecting only the skin exists but is only named.
  • Production of autoantibodies; the major one is ANA (anti-nuclear antibody), which can be drawn from plasma.
  • Chain: autoantibodies (ANA) are activated against the body's own DNA and form immune complexes → circulate around the body → land in an organ (any major system: heart, skin, kidneys, brain) → trigger an inflammatory response → destroy the tissue where they landed.
  • RA vs lupus complexes: in RA, complexes deposit only in the synovial fluid; in lupus they can deposit anywhere because there is DNA everywhere. The most common area is the kidney — the glomeruli (important and pretty fragile).

What are the three hallmarks in the lecture's definition of cancer?
Cellular transformation (e.g., a genetic mutation), uncontrolled cellular growth, and possible invasion into other organs with metastasis to other tissues or organs.
Contrast benign and malignant tumors on differentiation, boundary, and metastasis.
Benign: well differentiated, demarcated/often encapsulated and movable, no metastasis, no necrotic core. Malignant: poorly differentiated (anaplastic), poorly demarcated, invasive, frequently metastasize, can have a necrotic core.
Why can a benign tumor still be lethal?
If it grows large enough to mechanically interrupt the function of a critical tissue or organ (e.g., a benign tumor in the brain, or one taking over a significant portion of a lung).
What do T, N, and M stand for, and what do M0 and M1 mean? What do stages I to IV mean?
T = tumor size/location/involvement (T0, Tis, T1-T4); N = lymph node involvement (N0-N2); M = metastasis (M0 none, M1 present). Stage I confined to organ of origin; II locally invasive; III regional spread; IV distant sites.
What are the two primary routes of metastasis, and where is the "first stop" for each?
Lymphatic (first stop: lymph nodes, where cells can die, go dormant, or flourish) and vascular/blood (first stop: the liver, because most blood goes through the portal vein; cells clump and get trapped in liver capillaries).
Name the primary-to-secondary pairings the instructor gave for common sites of metastasis.
Lung: bone and brain. Colon: liver. Breast: bone, brain, liver, or lung. Prostate: vertebrae. Melanoma: brain.
Explain the car analogy for tumor suppressor genes, proto-oncogenes, and oncogenes. What is P53?
Tumor suppressor gene = brake pedal (restrains growth); proto-oncogene = gas pedal (normal growth); oncogene = gas pedal stuck to the floor (constant stimulation, cell never rests). P53 is a tumor suppressor gene that controls apoptosis of cells with damaged DNA.
How does a carcinogen differ from a promoter, and can something be both?
A carcinogen alters the cell's DNA (causation, cumulative, often prolonged exposure); a promoter helps already-mutated cells proliferate. Things can be both, e.g., estrogen therapy and tobacco.
What are the five-year survival rates for lung cancer caught at stage 1 vs. stage 4, and why is it caught late?
Stage 1: over 60%; stage 4: below 10%. Caught late because of a lack of early symptoms and limited screening uptake. Screening is with low-dose CT.
What is a pack-year, and what is the example given?
Risk rises with total cigarettes smoked, expressed in pack-years. A 60-year-old smoking 2 packs/day since age 20 has an 80-pack-year history. 80 to 90% of lung cancer patients are current or former smokers.
Why is radon emphasized, and what other lung cancer causes were listed?
Radon comes from the ground, high levels are very common in Kentucky, and radon plus smoking is a very dangerous compounding combination. Others: passive smoke (20-30% increased risk), COPD, asbestos, arsenic, genetics, prior chest radiation.
Describe the pathogenesis of lung cancer and the two types you must recognize.
Smoke and toxins paralyze cilia, carcinogens accumulate, plus genetic predisposition; it begins in an area of dysplasia, proliferates, erodes the epithelium, and grows into the lumen or as a mass. Types: non-small cell (85-90%, slower) and small cell (more rapid, metastasizes quicker, responds well initially but relapses quicker).
Give the key breast cancer statistics and the HRT timing risk from the lecture.
1 in 8 women diagnosed in their lifetime; most common US cancer (outside skin cancer). HRT with estrogen and progesterone raises risk if taken for more than five years. Black women: 40% more likely to die, lowest five-year survival, and higher rates of triple negative breast cancer.
What are DES and BRCA1/BRCA2, and what do they raise risk for?
DES was given to some pregnant women in the US from 1940 to 1971 to prevent miscarriage; it raises breast (and cervical) cancer risk. BRCA1/2 mutations raise risk of breast, ovarian, colon, pancreatic cancer (and prostate in males); women with mutations often get preventative mastectomy or oophorectomy.
What does the lecture say about the HPV vaccine and cervical cancer?
Almost 100% of cervical cancer patients test positive for HPV. The vaccine is 97% effective against cervical cancer/changes, 100% effective for genital warts, very safe, best at age 11-12, and can be given up to age 45. Pap smears and the vaccine reduced prevalence.
Describe colorectal cancer risk factors, polyps, and screening.
4th most common and 4th leading cause of cancer death; higher in males. Risks: age, IBD, family history/polyps, FAP or Lynch syndrome, sedentary lifestyle, low fiber, high fat/processed meat, obesity, alcohol, tobacco. Adenomatous polyps (most common) can become cancer; hyperplastic typically do not. Screening starts at 45 (earlier if high risk); sigmoidoscopy covers one third of the colon; colonoscopy about every 10 years.
What are the two main groups of chemo drugs, and which tumors respond best?
Cell cycle nonspecific (kill regardless of phase) and cell cycle specific (certain phases such as mitosis or DNA synthesis: anti-metabolites, mitotic inhibitors). Best against rapidly growing tumors with a high growth fraction and short doubling time.
List the general principles that apply to all chemotherapy drugs.
No one drug works for all cancers; low therapeutic index; toxicity comes from affecting rapidly dividing cells; combination therapy usually works better; avoid overlapping toxicity (e.g., not two cardiotoxic agents).
Which tissues cause the universal chemo toxicities, and what is magic mouthwash?
Hair follicles (hair loss, begins 7-14 days, regrows 1-2 months after), GI tract (nausea/vomiting, stomatitis), bone marrow (neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia). Magic mouthwash is a prescription cocktail (viscous lidocaine, Maalox/Mylanta, diphenhydramine, nystatin, prednisolone, distilled water) that treats stomatitis symptoms but does not cure it.
What is the nadir, and when is prophylactic levofloxacin recommended?
The nadir is the lowest point of WBCs, RBCs, and platelets (about 7-14 days after chemo), the time of highest risk for infection, anemia, and bleeding. Levofloxacin is recommended for prolonged neutropenia: ANC less than 500 for more than 7 days.
What will the instructor give you for drug questions, and what is expected?
She will always give the drug class (not just the name); know the classes (alkylating, antimetabolites, antitumor antibiotics, mitotic inhibitors, hormonal, biologics) and their expected toxicities. No picking a drug for a cancer.
Match each class to its example and key toxicities.
Alkylating (cyclophosphamide): hemorrhagic cystitis, extreme vesicant, sterility. Antimetabolite (methotrexate): nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, category X. Antitumor antibiotic (doxorubicin): cardiotoxicity/CHF with cumulative dosing. Mitotic inhibitor (vincristine): peripheral neuropathy.
What is the critical safety rule for vinca alkaloids, and what is notable about their bone marrow effect?
Vinca alkaloids (e.g., vincristine) are given IV only; if given intrathecally they will kill the patient. They do not typically cause significant bone marrow suppression, so they combine well with myelosuppressive drugs.
Compare ondansetron and promethazine, including the promethazine black box warnings.
Ondansetron: blocks type 3 serotonin receptors in the chemo trigger zone; oral or IV; headache, diarrhea, dizziness; works well with steroids. Promethazine: first-generation antihistamine; sedation and opioid-like respiratory depression; give IV piggyback. Black box: children under 2 and gangrenous extravasation.
What T-score defines osteoporosis, and what turns it into 'severe' osteoporosis?
A T-score below −2.5 (bone mineral density 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass, measured by DEXA). It is called severe when there is also a history of a fragility fracture (a bone broken from a fall from standing).
What happens to osteoclast and osteoblast activity in osteoporosis?
Osteoclast activity is increased (too much resorption) and osteoblast activity is decreased (cannot make new bone). Memory aid: osteoBlasts Build; osteoClasts destroy.
What are the key alendronate (Fosamax) teaching points?
First-line, about 50% fracture reduction, usually once a week. Take with one sip of water in the morning and stay upright (do not lie down) because of esophageal ulceration risk. Do not take with calcium; bioavailability is only about 1% and juice or coffee cuts it by about 60%.
What are the raloxifene (Evista) safety points, and why is HRT not recommended?
Raloxifene (SERM, postmenopausal osteoporosis only) carries stroke and clotting risk: stop at least 72 hours before a planned procedure; not for pregnancy; needs calcium and vitamin D. Estrogen/HRT is not recommended for osteoporosis because of blood clots, breast cancer, and other cancers.
What are the three signs of a fat embolism, and which one comes last?
Hypoxemia, altered (or decreased) level of consciousness, and a petechial rash — the rash is often the last symptom. It follows a long bone fracture and can go from fine to respiratory arrest within minutes; treatment is supportive (ventilator, possibly ECMO).
What are the manifestations and treatment of compartment syndrome?
Extreme pain out of proportion to the injury, weak or lost palpable pulses (may need Doppler), and rapid, non-pitting edema from increased pressure in a limited space (tourniquet effect). Treated with a fasciotomy; closely watch swelling inside a cast.
How is delayed healing different from non-union, and what is malunion?
Delayed healing is identified three months to a year after the fracture (increasing pain and tenderness). Non-union is no healing at four to six months. Malunion is improper alignment (the stick not put back straight), common if weight bearing began too soon.
How is osteomyelitis treated, and who is most at risk?
Blood culture, then empiric nafcillin and vancomycin, narrowed once results return; IV for four to six weeks (or two weeks IV plus two oral if controlled quickly). Risk: recent trauma, open fractures, diabetes, hemodialysis, IV drug use, splenectomy. Usually Staphylococcus aureus; avoid it at all costs.
What are the eight steps of OA pathogenesis in brief?
Pressure wears cartilage and exposes subchondral bone; cysts form; chondrocytes make proteoglycans (which cause swelling); inflammation further degrades cartilage; osteoblasts build bone spurs; synovial fluid thickens and movement decreases; the joint space narrows to bone on bone.
Which joints does OA typically affect and spare, and what are Heberden's vs. Bouchard's nodes?
Affected: cervical or lumbar-sacral spine, hip, knee, hands, big toe. Spared: wrists, elbows, ankles. Heberden's nodes are the distal ones; Bouchard's nodes are the proximal ones (closer to the hand).
What is the lecture's message about OA drug therapy?
No drug prevents progression, prevents damage, or reverses OA, so manage pain, maintain mobility, minimize disability. Start with acetaminophen or OTC NSAIDs (capsaicin topical), then prescription NSAIDs (may add colchicine), tramadol, opioids, or steroid injections; know NSAID risks.
How does degenerative disc disease present in the lumbar versus cervical spine?
Lumbar (L4, L5, S1): low back pain radiating down the back of the leg or buttocks (sciatica), worse sitting/bending/lifting/twisting, better walking or lying down; numbness, tingling, weakness, foot drop. Cervical: pain down the shoulders and arms, with numbness, tingling, or weakness in the arm or hand.
What is the key safety rule for methotrexate in RA?
It is given ONCE PER WEEK, never daily — death can occur with daily dosing. Also: always give folic acid; no alcohol; teratogenic (two forms of birth control); monitor AST/ALT/alk phos and CBC; high infection risk; 11 black box warnings.
How does RA differ from OA in distribution, swelling, and joint changes?
RA is systemic, autoimmune (type 3 hypersensitivity), and symmetrical, with spongy, soft, warm swelling and morning stiffness lasting over an hour; swan deformity and subluxations, no Heberden's/Bouchard's nodes. OA is localized, unilateral wear and tear with hard swelling.
What are pannus and rheumatoid factor in RA?
RF is an antibody against our own IgG antibodies, measured in serum to confirm diagnosis (CRP or sed rate can also be drawn). Pannus is the very vascularized scar tissue in the RA joint, full of active inflammatory cells (T cells, macrophages), that destroys surrounding tissue, leaving bone on bone.
What are hydroxychloroquine's use and teaching point, and which RA systemic findings should be recognized?
Hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil), an antimalarial DMARD, is better tolerated than methotrexate; about 1% retinopathy, so report blurry vision. Recognize Sjogren's syndrome (dry eyes and mouth) and rheumatoid nodules (about 25%); assess the heart (pericarditis or myocarditis).
Differentiate allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid by job.
Allopurinol: prophylaxis only; xanthine oxidase inhibitor that blocks uric acid production (2 to 6 weeks to take effect). Colchicine: oldest, second-line, reduces inflammation, used for flares. Probenecid: uricosuric that inhibits uric acid reabsorption. All three go on the drug matrix; NSAIDs are first line for acute attacks.
What are the allopurinol adverse effects and interactions?
Most common rash, but Stevens-Johnson syndrome can turn into TEN (fatal skin condition); agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia (monitor WBC/CBC). Increases effects of hypoglycemic agents (watch blood sugar) and warfarin (watch INR). Effectiveness is checked with serum uric acid.
What is the colchicine toxicity warning?
Stomach-flu-type symptoms and persistent vomiting are early signs of toxicity (GI bleeding, disrupted cell mitosis) — stop immediately, or multiple organ failure and death can follow. It is PO only and can cause leukopenia and bone marrow suppression.
What phases of gout does the lecture describe, and what is the classic joint involved?
1) Asymptomatic hyperuricemia; 2) acute flare; 3) clinically inactive (hyperuricemia without inflammation); 4) chronic gouty arthritis (attacks closer together). The big toe MTP joint is hit in about 50%; pain is the hallmark; complications include tophi and kidney stones.
How does lupus damage the body?
Autoantibodies (mainly ANA) activate against the body's own DNA and form immune complexes that circulate, land in an organ (heart, skin, kidneys, brain), and trigger inflammation that destroys tissue. Complexes can deposit anywhere because DNA is everywhere; the glomeruli of the kidney are most common.
What are the hallmark and most common features of lupus?
Fatigue is the most common symptom; the butterfly rash (bilateral across the face) is nearly a hallmark sign; also photosensitivity, edema (legs or around the eyes), fever, unusual hair loss, and cold-triggered Raynaud's-type changes treated with calcium channel blockers.
What is the big organ complication of lupus, and what is the prognosis picture?
Kidney involvement: about half of patients, with possible permanent damage needing dialysis or transplant. Severe multi-system involvement carries a poor prognosis; most people have mild disease with a good prognosis, with exacerbations and remissions.
What are lupus flare warning signs and triggers, and how are drugs chosen?
Warning signs: more severe fatigue, pain, headache. Triggers: sunlight, infection, abruptly stopping a medicine, stress. Drugs follow the systems involved: NSAIDs; high-dose steroids for severe kidney or CNS disease (low dose for arthritis); hydroxychloroquine (can prevent kidney and CNS damage); biologics/immunosuppressives for multi-organ disease.
